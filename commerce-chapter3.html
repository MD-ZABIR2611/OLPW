Wholesale Trade

The role of the wholesaler as the middleman between producers and retailers, the types of wholesaler, and the arguments for and against eliminating the wholesaler.

Overview

The wholesaler buys goods in large quantities from manufacturers and sells them in smaller quantities to retailers, standing in the middle of the chain of distribution. Although wholesalers are sometimes criticised for raising prices, they perform essential services such as breaking bulk, storing goods, providing credit and bearing risk. Cambridge questions often ask candidates to explain the services of the wholesaler to manufacturers and to retailers, to describe cash and carry warehouses, and to evaluate whether the wholesaler can be eliminated. This chapter explains each of these areas in detail and shows how to build balanced, well-developed answers.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Define wholesale trade and explain the position of the wholesaler in the chain of distribution.
  • Explain the services the wholesaler provides to manufacturers and to retailers.
  • Describe different types of wholesaler, including general, specialist, regional, national and cash and carry.
  • Explain the operation, advantages and disadvantages of cash and carry warehouses.
  • Explain why and how the wholesaler is sometimes eliminated from the chain of distribution.
  • Evaluate whether the wholesaler is still necessary in modern commerce.
02

The Wholesaler in the Chain of Distribution

Wholesale trade is the buying of goods in bulk from manufacturers or importers and selling them in smaller quantities to retailers or other traders. The wholesaler does not usually sell to the general public. In the traditional chain, manufacturers produce large volumes of a few products, while retailers want small quantities of many products. The wholesaler bridges this gap, buying from many manufacturers and selling assortments of goods to many retailers.

Without a wholesaler, each manufacturer would have to deal with thousands of retailers, and each retailer with dozens of manufacturers. The wholesaler reduces the number of transactions. If five manufacturers each sell directly to twenty retailers, there are 100 transactions; with one wholesaler in between there are only 25 (five plus twenty). Fewer transactions means lower administration, delivery and communication costs throughout the chain.

Wholesalers are found in most trades, including groceries, pharmaceuticals, textiles, hardware, electrical goods and agricultural produce. In many developing countries wholesale markets, such as fruit and vegetable markets and grain markets, are the main link between farmers and small shops.

Key fact:Wholesaler: buys in bulk from producers, sells in smaller quantities to retailers, and reduces the number of transactions in the chain.
03

Services of the Wholesaler to Manufacturers

The wholesaler buys in bulk, which allows manufacturers to produce on a large scale and gain economies of scale. By placing large orders in advance, the wholesaler saves the manufacturer the cost of storing large stocks, since the wholesaler holds them in its own warehouses. This reduces the manufacturer's need for storage space and ties up less of its capital in stock.

Wholesalers relieve manufacturers of many selling and distribution tasks. The manufacturer delivers large loads to a few wholesalers rather than small loads to many retailers, cutting transport costs. Wholesalers often pay promptly or in advance, which improves the manufacturer's cash flow, and they bear the risk of goods becoming damaged, going out of fashion or falling in price after purchase.

Wholesalers also provide valuable market information, passing back retailers' reports about which products are selling well, consumer complaints and changes in taste. They may help to advertise and promote products and can even finance production by paying for goods before they are made. Some wholesalers carry out final processes such as packing, grading, blending and branding.

  • Buys in bulk, enabling large-scale production.
  • Stores goods, saving the manufacturer warehouse costs.
  • Reduces distribution and transport costs.
  • Provides finance by prompt or advance payment.
  • Bears risks of price changes, damage and fashion change.
  • Supplies market information and may pack, grade and brand goods.
04

Services of the Wholesaler to Retailers

The most important service to retailers is breaking bulk. A small shopkeeper cannot buy a lorry-load of soap direct from the factory, but can buy two cartons from the wholesaler. The wholesaler also offers a wide variety of goods from many manufacturers in one place, so the retailer can compare brands and buy all its needs in a single visit or order, saving time and transport costs.

Wholesalers often give credit, allowing retailers to pay after they have sold the goods, which is very important for small shops with little working capital. Many deliver goods to the retailer's premises. Because the wholesaler holds large stocks, the retailer can keep small stocks and reorder frequently, saving storage space and reducing the risk of holding unsold goods.

Wholesalers also advise retailers on new products, display techniques and price trends, and they may help to stabilise prices by releasing stock when goods are scarce and holding stock when supply is plentiful. For seasonal goods such as rice, sugar or winter clothing, this storage function ensures that retailers can buy supplies throughout the year.

05

Types of Wholesaler

Wholesalers can be classified by the range of goods they stock. General wholesalers deal in a wide range of goods, such as groceries, toiletries and household items, supplying general stores. Specialist wholesalers deal in one line of goods, for example pharmaceuticals, electrical components, timber or textiles, and offer expert advice. They can also be classified by area: local or regional wholesalers serve one town or district, while national wholesalers operate across a whole country with depots in several cities.

Some wholesalers are classified by how they serve retailers. Traditional service wholesalers employ sales representatives who visit shops to take orders, provide credit and deliver goods. Cash and carry wholesalers operate large self-service warehouses. There are also agents and brokers who arrange sales between buyers and sellers without owning the goods, earning commission, and rack jobbers who stock and manage display racks in retail stores. In agriculture, produce wholesalers collect crops from many farmers, grade and pack them and sell them in wholesale markets.

06

Cash and Carry Wholesalers

A cash and carry wholesaler operates a large self-service warehouse where registered retailers, caterers and small businesses select goods from shelves, pay in cash or by card at the checkout, and transport the goods themselves. Examples include Makro and Metro. Customers usually need a trade card to enter, proving they are a business rather than a member of the public.

Because the wholesaler does not employ sales representatives, give credit or deliver, its costs are lower and it can offer lower prices than a traditional wholesaler. Retailers benefit because they can buy exactly what they need, when they need it, can see and inspect goods before purchase and can take advantage of special offers. Cash and carry warehouses also stay open for long hours.

The disadvantages for retailers are that they must use their own transport and time to travel to the warehouse, they must pay immediately with no credit, there is no advice from a sales representative, and they may be tempted to buy more than needed because of offers. For retailers far from the warehouse or without a vehicle, the traditional service wholesaler may be more convenient.

Key fact:Cash and carry = self-service + cash payment + no delivery + no credit → lower prices for retailers.
07

Elimination of the Wholesaler

In many trades the wholesaler has been bypassed, a process called elimination of the wholesaler. Large supermarket chains and multiples buy in such large quantities that they can deal directly with manufacturers and obtain the bulk discounts themselves. They run their own central warehouses and distribution fleets, performing the wholesale functions internally. Manufacturers may also sell direct to retailers to control how their products are displayed and priced, or sell directly to consumers through factory shops, mail order and websites.

Goods that are commonly sold without wholesalers include perishable goods such as fresh bread, milk and flowers, which must reach consumers quickly; expensive or technical goods such as cars, machinery and computers, where the manufacturer provides after-sales service; goods made to order such as tailored suits or furniture; and fashion goods that change quickly. Online platforms have also allowed small producers to reach consumers directly.

However, eliminating the wholesaler does not eliminate the functions of wholesaling. Somebody must still break bulk, store goods, transport them, finance stock and bear risk. When a supermarket bypasses the wholesaler, it takes over these functions and their costs. The question is simply who performs the functions most efficiently.

08

Is the Wholesaler Still Necessary?

Critics argue that wholesalers add an extra profit margin to prices, delay the flow of goods and may hoard goods to push up prices. They also say that the wholesaler separates manufacturers from consumers, so information about consumer needs may be distorted. The growth of large retailers and e-commerce reduces the need for wholesalers in many markets.

Supporters argue that wholesalers remain essential for small retailers, who cannot buy direct from manufacturers because their orders are too small. They are especially important where there are many small producers, such as farmers, and many small shops, as in much of South Asia and Africa. Because the wholesaler specialises in distribution, it may perform storage and delivery more cheaply than individual manufacturers or retailers could. A balanced conclusion is that the wholesaler remains necessary where markets are fragmented, but its role is declining where large retailers dominate.

Worked Examples

Calculating the reduction in transactions

Six manufacturers of snack foods supply 40 small shops in a town. Calculate the number of transactions with and without a wholesaler, and explain the significance.

  1. Without a wholesaler, each manufacturer deals with each shop: 6 × 40 = 240 transactions.
  2. With one wholesaler, each manufacturer deals only with the wholesaler (6 transactions) and each shop deals only with the wholesaler (40 transactions): 6 + 40 = 46 transactions.
  3. Reduction: 240 − 46 = 194 fewer transactions.
  4. Significance: fewer orders, invoices, deliveries and payments reduce administration and transport costs for everyone in the chain.
Transactions fall from 240 to 46, a reduction of 194, showing how the wholesaler lowers distribution costs and makes trade more efficient.

Should a small retailer use cash and carry?

Fatima owns a small grocery shop and has a van. She currently buys from a service wholesaler who gives her 30 days' credit and delivers weekly. Should she switch to a cash and carry wholesaler? [6 marks]

  1. Advantages of switching: lower prices because the cash and carry has lower costs; she can inspect goods and take advantage of special offers; she can buy whenever she needs goods rather than waiting for weekly delivery.
  2. Disadvantages of switching: she loses 30 days' credit and must pay immediately, which may strain her cash flow; she must spend time and fuel collecting goods; there is no sales representative to advise her on new products.
  3. Consider her circumstances: she has a van, so transport is possible, but a small shop is likely to have limited working capital.
  4. Judgement: switching is sensible if her cash position is strong and the price savings exceed the cost of time and fuel.
Fatima could use cash and carry for most goods to benefit from lower prices, as she owns a van, but she should keep the service wholesaler for some lines if she depends on credit to manage her cash flow.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Saying that eliminating the wholesaler removes the wholesaler's functions. The functions remain; they are simply performed by someone else.
  • Confusing the services to retailers with the services to manufacturers. Credit and variety are services to retailers; bulk buying and storage relieve the manufacturer.
  • Claiming cash and carry wholesalers give credit or deliver. They do neither, which is why their prices are lower.
  • Asserting that wholesalers always raise prices. Their specialisation may actually lower overall distribution costs.
  • Forgetting to give examples of goods where the wholesaler is often bypassed, such as perishable, expensive or made-to-order goods.
  • Writing one-word points such as 'storage'. Develop each point by explaining who benefits and how.

Exam tips

  • Use the transaction calculation (manufacturers × retailers compared with manufacturers + retailers) to support an explanation of the wholesaler's role.
  • Organise answers under clear headings: services to manufacturers, services to retailers, services to consumers.
  • For 'Is the wholesaler necessary?' questions, always give both sides and a judgement based on the type of market or product.
  • Remember that wholesalers indirectly benefit consumers through price stability and the availability of goods in local shops.
  • In case-study questions, look at the size of the retailer: small retailers need wholesalers; large chains can bypass them.
  • Use examples such as Makro or Metro for cash and carry and supermarket chains for elimination of the wholesaler.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. Define wholesale trade.

Model answerBuying goods in large quantities from manufacturers (1) and selling them in smaller quantities to retailers (1).
[3 marks]

2. State three services a wholesaler provides to retailers.

Model answerAny three: breaking bulk (1); offering variety of goods (1); giving credit (1); delivery (1); advice on new products (1); holding stock so retailers need not (1).
[4 marks]

3. Explain two services a wholesaler provides to manufacturers.

Model answerBulk buying: the wholesaler buys large quantities, enabling the manufacturer to produce on a large scale with lower unit costs (2). Storage: the wholesaler holds goods in its warehouses, saving the manufacturer storage space and capital tied up in stock (2). Also: bears risk, prompt payment, market information.
[5 marks]

4. Describe how a cash and carry wholesaler operates.

Model answerLarge self-service warehouse (1); retailers need a trade card to enter (1); they select goods themselves (1); pay immediately in cash or by card (1); transport the goods themselves (1).
[2 marks]

5. Give two reasons why cash and carry prices are lower than those of service wholesalers.

Model answerNo delivery costs because retailers collect goods (1). No credit, so no bad debts or cost of waiting for payment (1). Fewer staff because of self-service and no sales representatives (1).
[6 marks]

6. Name three types of goods that are often sold without a wholesaler and explain why.

Model answerPerishable goods such as bread or milk, which must reach consumers quickly (2). Expensive or technical goods such as cars, needing manufacturer after-sales service (2). Made-to-order goods such as tailored clothes, produced for a specific customer (2).
[4 marks]

7. Explain how a wholesaler helps to stabilise prices.

Model answerWhen supply is high, for example after a harvest, the wholesaler buys and stores goods, preventing prices falling sharply (2). When supply is low, it releases stock, preventing prices rising sharply (2).
[6 marks]

8. Explain why large supermarket chains usually bypass the wholesaler.

Model answerThey buy in such large quantities that manufacturers will sell to them directly at bulk discounts (2). They have their own central warehouses and lorry fleets to break bulk and distribute to branches (2). Cutting out the wholesaler's profit margin allows lower prices or higher profits (2).
[4 marks]

9. Distinguish between a general wholesaler and a specialist wholesaler.

Model answerA general wholesaler stocks a wide range of different goods such as groceries and household items (2). A specialist wholesaler deals in one line of goods such as medicines or timber and can give expert advice (2).
[6 marks]

10. 'Eliminate the wholesaler and you do not eliminate the functions of the wholesaler.' Explain this statement.

Model answerEven without a wholesaler, goods still need to be bought in bulk, broken into smaller quantities, stored, transported, financed and insured (2–3). These functions must be taken over by the manufacturer or retailer, who bears the costs (2). So the costs of wholesaling remain; only the organisation performing them changes (1).
[10 marks]

11. Discuss whether the wholesaler is still necessary in modern commerce.

Model answerNecessary: small retailers cannot buy direct because orders are small (1–2); wholesalers break bulk, give credit and deliver (1–2); they collect produce from many small farmers (1); specialisation makes distribution efficient (1). Not necessary: large retailers and chains bypass them (1–2); e-commerce links producers directly to consumers (1–2); wholesalers add a profit margin (1). Conclusion that the wholesaler remains necessary where markets are fragmented but is declining where large retailers dominate (1–2).

Quick Recap

  1. Wholesalers buy in bulk from producers and sell smaller quantities to retailers, reducing the number of transactions.
  2. Services to manufacturers include bulk buying, storage, finance, risk bearing and market information.
  3. Services to retailers include breaking bulk, variety, credit, delivery and advice.
  4. Wholesalers may be general or specialist, local or national, service or cash and carry.
  5. Cash and carry wholesalers offer lower prices because retailers serve themselves, pay immediately and collect goods.
  6. Large retailers, direct selling and e-commerce have led to the elimination of the wholesaler in many trades.
  7. Eliminating the wholesaler does not eliminate the wholesale functions, which must still be performed by someone.
  8. Wholesalers remain important where there are many small producers and many small retailers.
