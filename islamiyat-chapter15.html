Chapter 15 · Islamiyat

The Rightly Guided Caliphs in Depth

Each of the four Rightly Guided Caliphs left a distinct legacy of leadership. This chapter examines their administrations, the challenges they confronted, and why their example remains the model of Islamic governance.

Key Concepts

01

Abu Bakr's caliphate (632–634) faced the ridda (apostasy) wars and false prophets such as Musaylima; he also began the collection of the Qur'an and preserved the unity of the ummah.

02

Umar ibn al-Khattab (634–644) instituted the diwan (register) for stipends, the hijri calendar, judges (qadis), and the administrative system of conquered provinces, while his armies expanded Islam to Persia, Egypt and Jerusalem.

03

Uthman ibn Affan (644–656) standardised the Qur'an into one codex (the Uthmanic text) and expanded the navy and irrigation, but nepotism in appointments and economic grievances fed rebellion in which he was martyred while reading the Qur'an.

04

Ali ibn Abi Talib (656–661) moved the capital to Kufa, dealt with the battles of the Camel and Siffin and the arbitration crisis, and was martyred by a Kharijite; his caliphate shows the cost of civil discord (fitna).

05

Justice characterised all four: Umar's accountability of governors, Uthman's patronage of the poor from the bayt al-mal (public treasury), and Ali's insistence on consultation (shura) illustrate the checks placed on rulers.

06

Their collective example defines khilafa rashida (rightly guided caliphate): leaders chosen by consultation, accountable, living simply from public funds, and ruling by the Qur'an and the Prophet's example.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Ridda warsThe campaigns under Abu Bakr, 632–633, against tribes that refused zakat or followed false prophets after the Prophet's death.
DiwanThe register instituted by Umar listing those entitled to stipends from the treasury, ordered by service to Islam.
Hijri calendarThe Islamic lunar calendar beginning from the year of the Hijrah, 622 CE, introduced under Umar.
Uthmanic codexThe standard single edition of the Qur'an compiled under Uthman and sent to the major cities to fix the text.
Battle of the CamelThe first civil war battle of Ali's caliphate, 656, near Basra, named after Aisha's camel.
Battle of SiffinThe 657 battle between Ali and Muawiya that ended in arbitration, creating lasting division in the ummah.
Bayt al-malThe public treasury of the Muslim state, from which the poor, orphans and widows were supported.
ShuraConsultation: the Islamic principle that major decisions be taken by consulting the community's people of knowledge and influence.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Structure each Caliph as "challenge — response — legacy": name the crisis they faced, the measure they took, and the lasting institution or lesson. Examiners award a mark for each leg, so a Caliph answered only as a list of events scores lower than one analysed as leadership.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Match each of the four Rightly Guided Caliphs to one achievement: Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali.

Model answerAbu Bakr — defeated the ridda tribes and began the Qur'an's collection (1); Umar — introduced the diwan, qadis and the hijri calendar (1); Uthman — standardised the Uthmanic codex of the Qur'an (1); Ali — ruled from Kufa and faced the fitna of the Camel and Siffin (1).
CHALLENGE

Umar's caliphate is often called a model of just administration. Discuss two of his reforms and explain how each served justice.

Model answerThe diwan distributed state stipends transparently by service, so public money reached the poor and the fighters fairly rather than the ruler's favourites, institutionalising equity in state finance (1). Appointing qadis as independent judges, and holding governors personally accountable — recalling and disciplining them on complaint — separated judgement from executive power and made even officials answerable to law, the essence of his justice (1). Judgement: his reforms show justice as structure, not personal virtue alone, which is why his model is still cited (1).
