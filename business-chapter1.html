Understanding Business Activity

Why businesses exist, how they combine factors of production to add value, how economic activity is classified into sectors, and why enterprise and entrepreneurs matter.

01

Overview

Every business, from a street food stall to a multinational car maker, exists to satisfy the needs and wants of customers while using scarce resources. This chapter builds the foundation for the whole Business Studies course: the economic problem, opportunity cost, specialisation, added value and the classification of business activity into primary, secondary and tertiary sectors. Examiners regularly test these ideas through short definition questions and through case-study questions that ask you to explain how a named business adds value or why a country's sector balance is changing. If you understand the ideas here, you will find it much easier to apply them in later chapters on finance, marketing and operations.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain the concepts of needs, wants, scarcity and opportunity cost, and why they create the basic economic problem.
  • Identify the four factors of production and the reward each one earns.
  • Explain the importance of specialisation and the division of labour, including their advantages and disadvantages.
  • Define added value and explain how a business can increase the value it adds.
  • Classify business activity into primary, secondary and tertiary sectors and explain the causes of changes in their relative importance.
  • Describe the characteristics of successful entrepreneurs and the purpose and contents of a business plan.
  • Distinguish between the public sector and the private sector in a mixed economy.
02

Needs, Wants and the Economic Problem

A need is a good or service that is essential for survival, such as food, water, shelter and clothing. A want is a good or service that people would like to have but which is not essential for living, such as a smartphone, a holiday abroad or a designer watch. Human wants are unlimited: as soon as one want is satisfied, people usually desire something else. In contrast, the resources available to produce goods and services are limited. This mismatch between unlimited wants and limited resources is called the economic problem, and the shortage that results is called scarcity.

Because of scarcity, individuals, businesses and governments all have to make choices. A household with a fixed income must decide whether to spend on a new fridge or a family holiday. A business with a limited budget must decide whether to spend money on advertising or on new machinery. A government must decide whether to spend tax revenue on hospitals, schools or roads. Every one of these choices involves giving something up, which leads directly to the idea of opportunity cost.

Opportunity cost is the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. If a bakery owner uses $5,000 to buy a new oven instead of spending it on a marketing campaign, the opportunity cost of the oven is the marketing campaign. Notice that opportunity cost is not measured simply in money; it is the benefit of the alternative that was sacrificed. Good business decisions weigh the benefits of an option against its opportunity cost.

Key fact:Opportunity cost = the next best alternative forgone when making a choice.
03

Factors of Production

Businesses produce goods and services by combining resources known as factors of production. There are four factors. Land includes all natural resources, such as the ground a factory stands on, minerals, forests, fish and oil. Labour is the physical and mental effort of people who work in production. Capital refers to man-made goods used to produce other goods and services, such as machinery, tools, vehicles and buildings; in economics, capital does not simply mean money. Enterprise is the skill and risk-taking ability of the person who brings the other three factors together to produce a good or service.

Each factor earns a reward. Land earns rent, labour earns wages or salaries, capital earns interest and enterprise earns profit. Because every factor is scarce, businesses must decide how to use them efficiently. For example, a textile firm in a country where labour is cheap may choose labour-intensive methods, while a firm in a country with high wages may invest more heavily in capital equipment such as automated looms.

  • Land – natural resources – reward: rent.
  • Labour – human effort – reward: wages and salaries.
  • Capital – man-made resources used in production – reward: interest.
  • Enterprise – organising and risk-taking – reward: profit.
04

Specialisation and the Division of Labour

Specialisation occurs when people, businesses, regions or countries concentrate on producing the goods or services they are best at. Instead of every family growing its own food and making its own clothes, individuals specialise in one job and use the money they earn to buy the other things they need. The division of labour is a form of specialisation in which the production process is split into separate tasks, with each worker performing one task. A car assembly line, where one worker fits doors and another fits windscreens, is a classic example.

The division of labour brings important advantages. Workers become skilled and fast at their task, so output per worker rises. Less time is wasted moving between tasks, training is quicker because each worker only needs to learn one job, and specialist machinery can be used more effectively. These benefits usually lower the average cost of each unit produced.

However, there are drawbacks. Repeating the same task all day can be boring, which can reduce motivation and increase labour turnover and absence. Production can be disrupted if one worker or section is absent, because every stage depends on the stage before it. Workers who only know one task may also find it harder to find a new job if their skill becomes obsolete, for example if a machine replaces them.

05

The Purpose of Business Activity and Added Value

The main purpose of business activity is to combine factors of production to make goods and services that satisfy customers' needs and wants. In doing so, businesses create employment, pay taxes and generate incomes. A successful business transforms inputs into outputs that customers value more highly than the original inputs.

Added value is the difference between the selling price of a product and the cost of the bought-in materials and components used to make it. If a furniture maker buys wood, screws and varnish costing $40 and sells the finished chair for $150, the added value is $110. Added value is not the same as profit, because the business still has to pay other costs such as wages, rent and electricity out of the added value.

A business can increase added value in two broad ways: by raising the selling price without increasing the cost of materials, or by reducing the cost of bought-in materials without reducing the price. Ways to raise price include building a strong brand image, offering excellent customer service, improving packaging, adding features or creating a convenient location. Ways to cut material costs include finding cheaper suppliers, buying in bulk or reducing waste. Each method has risks: higher prices may drive customers away, and cheaper materials may reduce quality.

Key fact:Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials and components.
06

Classification of Business Activity

Business activity is classified into three sectors. The primary sector extracts natural resources from the earth; examples include farming, fishing, forestry, mining and oil extraction. The secondary sector manufactures and processes natural resources into finished or semi-finished goods; examples include car manufacturing, food processing, construction and textiles. The tertiary sector provides services to consumers and to other businesses; examples include banking, retailing, transport, insurance, tourism, education and healthcare.

The three sectors are linked through the chain of production. For example, a cotton farmer (primary) sells raw cotton to a textile factory (secondary), which sells cloth to a clothing manufacturer (secondary), and the finished shirts are transported, insured and sold through shops (tertiary). A business in one sector depends on the others, so a problem in one part of the chain, such as a poor harvest, can affect businesses throughout the chain.

The relative importance of each sector is measured by the share of employment or the share of total output it accounts for. In less developed economies, the primary sector usually employs the largest share of the workforce. As countries industrialise, the secondary sector grows. In developed economies, the tertiary sector becomes dominant, often employing more than 70% of workers.

07

Changes in Sector Importance

Industrialisation is the process by which a country's secondary sector grows in importance, as seen in China and Vietnam in recent decades. It usually increases total output and incomes, creates jobs and raises tax revenue for the government. However, it can also cause pollution, overcrowding in cities and the decline of rural communities as workers move to factories.

Deindustrialisation is the decline in importance of the secondary sector, as seen in the UK and much of Western Europe. Causes include competition from countries with lower labour costs, the depletion of natural resources, and rising incomes that lead consumers to spend a greater proportion of their income on services such as leisure, travel and financial services. Deindustrialisation can cause structural unemployment in former manufacturing regions, but it also creates new jobs in the tertiary sector.

  • Rising incomes increase demand for services such as tourism, restaurants and entertainment.
  • Lower-cost competition from abroad reduces domestic manufacturing.
  • Depletion of natural resources reduces primary sector activity.
  • Technological change increases productivity, so fewer workers are needed in farming and manufacturing.
08

Enterprise, Entrepreneurs and Business Plans

An entrepreneur is a person who organises, operates and takes the risk for a new business venture. Successful entrepreneurs tend to share certain characteristics: they are hard-working, risk-takers, creative, optimistic, self-confident, innovative, independent and effective communicators. Entrepreneurs benefit an economy because they create jobs, introduce new products, increase competition and contribute to economic growth.

Setting up a business brings rewards such as independence, the chance to pursue a personal interest and the potential for high profits. It also brings risks: the owner may lose the money invested, may face long working hours and stress, and may lack experience in areas such as finance and marketing. Many new businesses fail within the first few years because of poor planning, lack of cash or too little demand.

A business plan is a written document that describes the business, its objectives, its strategies, the market it serves and its financial forecasts. It usually includes details of the owner, the product, the target market, the marketing plan, the production process, the staff required and cash-flow and profit forecasts. A business plan helps the owner think carefully about every aspect of the business and is usually required by banks before they will lend money. Governments often support new businesses through grants, advice services, training schemes, low-interest loans and the provision of low-rent premises in enterprise zones.

09

Public and Private Sectors in a Mixed Economy

In a mixed economy, some resources are owned and controlled by the private sector and some by the public sector. The private sector consists of businesses owned by private individuals, such as sole traders, partnerships and companies. Their main aim is usually profit. The public sector consists of organisations owned and controlled by the government, such as state hospitals, schools, the police and public corporations.

The government provides some goods and services because the private sector would not supply enough of them, or because they are essential and should be available to everyone regardless of income. Examples include defence, street lighting, basic education and healthcare. Most countries today have mixed economies, although the balance between the two sectors varies widely.

Worked Examples

Calculating and increasing added value

Amira runs a small bakery. She buys flour, sugar, butter and packaging costing $1.20 for each cake and sells each cake for $4.00. Calculate the added value per cake and explain two ways Amira could increase it.

  1. Recall the formula: added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials.
  2. Substitute: $4.00 − $1.20 = $2.80 added value per cake.
  3. Method 1: raise the price by improving the product's image, for example with attractive branded boxes or by advertising that the cakes are handmade with organic ingredients, so customers are willing to pay more.
  4. Method 2: lower material costs by buying flour and sugar in bulk from a wholesaler to obtain discounts, while keeping quality the same.
  5. Evaluate: a price rise could lose price-sensitive customers, and cheaper ingredients could damage the quality that customers expect.
Added value is $2.80 per cake. Amira could increase it by creating a premium brand image to justify a higher price, or by buying ingredients in bulk to cut material costs, but she must protect quality and customer loyalty.

Case study: sector change in a developing economy

In Country X, the share of workers employed in agriculture fell from 60% to 35% over 20 years, while manufacturing rose from 15% to 30% and services rose from 25% to 35%. Explain two reasons for these changes and one likely effect on businesses.

  1. Identify the trend: the primary sector is declining while the secondary and tertiary sectors are growing, which is typical of industrialisation.
  2. Reason 1: new farming machinery and technology raise productivity, so fewer workers are needed to produce the same food output.
  3. Reason 2: foreign investment in factories creates better-paid jobs, attracting workers from rural areas to towns.
  4. Effect: as incomes rise, demand for services such as retail, banking and mobile phones increases, creating opportunities for tertiary businesses.
  5. Consider a negative effect: rural businesses, such as village shops, may lose customers as people migrate to cities.
The changes show industrialisation, caused by agricultural mechanisation and factory investment. Tertiary businesses benefit from rising incomes, although rural firms may suffer from migration.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Confusing added value with profit. Added value only subtracts bought-in materials, whereas profit subtracts all costs, including wages and rent.
  • Describing capital as money. In the factors of production, capital means man-made resources such as machinery, tools and buildings.
  • Giving opportunity cost as all the alternatives given up. It is only the single next best alternative.
  • Placing construction in the tertiary sector. Construction is a secondary sector activity because it produces a physical product.
  • Listing entrepreneur characteristics without explaining why each one helps the business to succeed.
  • Assuming deindustrialisation always means the economy is getting poorer; it often happens as incomes rise and people demand more services.

Exam tips

  • For any 'explain' question on added value, always link your method to either raising price or reducing material costs, and use the business in the case study.
  • When asked to classify a business, look carefully at what it actually does: a fish farm is primary, a fish canning factory is secondary and a fish restaurant is tertiary.
  • Opportunity cost answers should name the specific alternative given up, for example 'the new delivery van', rather than just saying 'the money'.
  • In 6-mark evaluation questions, give both advantages and disadvantages of the division of labour and finish with a justified judgement.
  • Use numbers from the case study wherever possible; examiners reward application to the context rather than generic textbook answers.
  • Learn the four rewards (rent, wages, interest, profit) as a set; they are easy recall marks.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. Define scarcity.

Model answerScarcity is the situation where there are insufficient resources to satisfy all human needs and wants (1 mark for limited resources, 1 mark for unlimited wants).
[2 marks]

2. What is meant by opportunity cost?

Model answerThe next best alternative that is given up when a choice is made (2 marks for full definition; 1 mark for 'what is given up').
[4 marks]

3. Identify the four factors of production.

Model answerLand, labour, capital and enterprise (1 mark each).
[2 marks]

4. State two examples of businesses in the tertiary sector.

Model answerAny two from: bank, insurance company, retailer, hotel, transport firm, school, hospital, hairdresser (1 mark each).
[2 marks]

5. A shoe maker buys leather and laces for $18 and sells each pair of shoes for $60. Calculate the added value per pair.

Model answer$60 − $18 = $42 per pair (1 mark for method, 1 mark for correct answer).
[4 marks]

6. Explain two advantages of the division of labour to a car manufacturer.

Model answerWorkers repeat the same task so they become faster and more skilled, raising output per worker and lowering unit costs. Training is quicker and cheaper because each worker only learns one task, so new workers become productive sooner. (1 mark for each advantage, 1 mark for each explanation.)
[4 marks]

7. Explain two disadvantages of specialisation for workers.

Model answerRepetitive tasks cause boredom, reducing motivation and job satisfaction. Workers have a narrow range of skills, so if their task is automated they may find it hard to find another job. (1 mark for each point, 1 mark for each development.)
[4 marks]

8. Explain two reasons why the tertiary sector has grown in many developed countries.

Model answerRising incomes mean consumers spend more on services such as holidays, restaurants and financial services. Manufacturing has declined because of competition from countries with lower labour costs, so workers move into service jobs. (2 marks for each explained reason.)
[6 marks]

9. Identify and explain three characteristics of a successful entrepreneur.

Model answerRisk-taker: willing to invest savings in an uncertain venture, which is necessary to start a business. Creative: able to think of new ideas that make the product different from competitors. Hard-working: willing to work long hours, especially when the business is new and cannot afford many staff. (1 mark for each characteristic, 1 mark for each explanation.)
[4 marks]

10. Explain why a bank may ask to see a business plan before lending to a new business.

Model answerThe plan shows the bank the business idea, target market and financial forecasts, so the bank can judge whether the business is likely to make enough profit and cash to repay the loan and interest. It also shows that the owner has thought carefully about the risks, which reduces the chance of failure and so the risk of the bank losing its money.
[8 marks]

11. A clothing company is considering two ways to increase added value: using more expensive, high-quality fabric so it can charge higher prices, or switching to a cheaper fabric supplier. Discuss which option it should choose. Justify your answer.

Model answerHigher-quality fabric could let the company build a premium brand and charge much higher prices, increasing added value if the price rise is greater than the extra material cost; however, costs rise and customers may not pay more if incomes are low. A cheaper supplier reduces material costs immediately, raising added value per unit without changing price; however, quality may fall, damaging reputation and future sales. Judgement: if the company targets high-income customers, the premium option is better because it builds long-term brand loyalty; if its customers are price-sensitive, cutting costs is safer provided quality checks are maintained. (Up to 2 marks knowledge, 2 application, 2 analysis, 2 evaluation.)

Quick Recap

  1. The economic problem is caused by unlimited wants and limited resources, which forces choices and creates opportunity cost.
  2. The four factors of production are land, labour, capital and enterprise, earning rent, wages, interest and profit.
  3. Specialisation and the division of labour raise efficiency but can cause boredom and dependency.
  4. Added value is selling price minus bought-in material costs, and can be raised through branding, service, quality or lower material costs.
  5. Business activity is classified into primary, secondary and tertiary sectors that are linked by the chain of production.
  6. Developed economies are dominated by the tertiary sector, while industrialising economies see secondary sector growth.
  7. Entrepreneurs take risks to start businesses, and a business plan helps them plan and obtain finance.
  8. A mixed economy contains both a profit-seeking private sector and a government-owned public sector.
