Chapter 01 · Commerce

Production and Trade

How human wants are satisfied through production, specialisation and exchange, and how commerce links producers to consumers through trade and aids to trade.

01

Overview

Every economy exists to satisfy human wants, and commerce is the part of economic activity that moves goods and services from the people who make them to the people who use them. This chapter introduces the chain of production, the three sectors of industry, the division of labour and the idea that trade and its aids (transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing and advertising) form the backbone of commerce. Almost every later chapter builds on these ideas, so examiners frequently test them through definitions, diagrams of the chain of distribution and short explanations of how commerce helps production. A secure understanding here makes the rest of the syllabus much easier.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain the meaning of production and why it exists to satisfy human wants and needs.
  • Distinguish between primary, secondary and tertiary production, giving examples of each.
  • Define commerce and describe how trade and the aids to trade fit within it.
  • Explain the division of labour and specialisation, with their advantages and disadvantages.
  • Describe the chain of production and the role of direct services and commercial services.
  • Explain how barter differs from exchange using money and why money is needed for specialisation.
  • Analyse how commerce helps producers, consumers and the economy as a whole.
02

Human Wants and the Purpose of Production

People have needs, such as food, clothing and shelter, which are essential for survival, and wants, such as mobile phones, holidays and branded goods, which improve the quality of life. Human wants are unlimited because as soon as one want is satisfied, another appears. The resources available to satisfy them, however, are limited. Production is the process of creating goods and services to satisfy these wants. In Commerce, production is used in a wide sense: it includes not only making physical goods in a factory but also every activity that helps get a product to the final consumer.

Production is only complete when the good or service reaches the person who wants it. A tonne of wheat in a field in Punjab has little value to a family in Karachi until it has been harvested, milled into flour, transported, stored, sold by a wholesaler and a retailer and bought by the family. Each stage adds value, and each person involved, from the farmer to the shopkeeper, is a producer. This is why a lorry driver, a banker or an insurance clerk is regarded as productive even though they do not make anything physical.

Goods can be classified as consumer goods, which are bought by households for their own use, and capital (producer) goods, which are used to make other goods, such as machinery, tools and factory buildings. Consumer goods may be durable, like furniture and cars, which give service over a long period, or non-durable, like food and soap, which are used up quickly. Services are intangible: they cannot be touched or stored, for example hairdressing, teaching and banking.

Key fact:Production = any activity that creates goods or services to satisfy human wants, and it is complete only when the product reaches the final consumer.
03

The Three Sectors of Production

Primary production (extractive industry) involves obtaining natural resources from the land or sea. Examples include farming, fishing, forestry, mining and quarrying, and drilling for oil and gas. Primary products are often raw materials that need further processing. In many developing countries a large share of the working population is still employed in primary production, especially agriculture.

Secondary production covers manufacturing and construction, where raw materials are changed into finished or semi-finished goods. Examples include textile mills turning cotton into cloth, car assembly plants, food processing, oil refining and the building of houses, roads and bridges. Construction is included because it turns materials such as cement, steel and bricks into a finished structure. Secondary production adds a great deal of value because the processed product is worth more than the raw materials used.

Tertiary production is the provision of services. It is divided into commercial services, which help the flow of goods (trade, transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing and advertising), and direct services, which are provided personally to consumers, such as those of doctors, teachers, police officers, lawyers, hairdressers and entertainers. As economies develop, the tertiary sector usually grows fastest, and in most industrialised countries it employs the majority of workers.

  • Primary: extracting natural resources, for example farming, fishing, mining, forestry.
  • Secondary: manufacturing and construction, for example sugar refining, car assembly, house building.
  • Tertiary (commercial services): trade and aids to trade, for example retailing, banking, insurance, transport.
  • Tertiary (direct services): personal services, for example doctors, teachers, police, hairdressers.
04

The Chain of Production

The chain of production shows the stages a product passes through from raw material to final consumer, linking all three sectors. A cotton shirt illustrates it well: a farmer grows cotton (primary), a ginning factory separates the fibre and a mill spins and weaves it into cloth, and a garment factory cuts and stitches the shirt (secondary), then transport firms, wholesalers, retailers, banks and insurers make sure the shirt reaches the customer (tertiary). At every link value is added, which is why the final price is much higher than the cost of the raw cotton.

The chain shows interdependence. Each stage relies on the others: the garment factory cannot operate without cloth, and the retailer has nothing to sell without the factory. A breakdown at any link, such as a transport strike or a crop failure, affects all later stages. Interdependence also exists between industries, because a manufacturer depends on banks for finance, on insurers for protection against risk and on advertisers to create demand.

Examiners often ask candidates to draw or complete a chain of production for a named product. A good answer names each stage, identifies the sector it belongs to and states how commercial services support the movement of goods between the stages, for example insurance covering the cloth while it is in transit to the garment factory.

05

Division of Labour and Specialisation

Specialisation means concentrating on producing a limited range of goods or services, or on one particular task. Division of labour is the form of specialisation in which the production of a good is split into many small tasks, each performed by a different worker. Adam Smith famously showed that in a pin factory, workers who each did one step produced vastly more pins than if each worker made whole pins alone. Specialisation can occur at the level of the individual, the firm, the region and the country.

The advantages include higher output per worker, because workers become skilled and fast through repetition; saving time, because workers do not move between tasks; the use of specialised machinery; easier and shorter training; and the chance for workers to choose jobs suited to their abilities. For the firm, costs per unit fall and quality becomes more consistent. For consumers, goods become cheaper and more plentiful.

There are also disadvantages. Repetitive work can be boring, lowering morale and increasing absenteeism. Workers lose craft skills and may find it hard to change jobs if demand falls, so structural unemployment can result. Interdependence means that a strike or breakdown in one section can halt the whole production line. Standardised products reduce variety for consumers. Most importantly for Commerce, specialisation creates a need for exchange: a worker who only fits car doors must trade their wages for everything else they need, which is why specialisation makes commerce essential.

Key fact:Specialisation leads to surpluses and shortages, and trade is needed to exchange surpluses for the goods people lack.
06

From Barter to Money

Barter is the direct exchange of goods and services without using money, for example swapping a goat for a bag of rice. Barter can work in a simple economy, but it has serious problems. There must be a double coincidence of wants: each party must want exactly what the other offers. It is hard to value goods against each other, it is difficult to divide large items like a cow into smaller parts, and wealth is hard to store because many goods perish. Barter also makes it impossible to agree on payment in the future.

Money solves these problems. It acts as a medium of exchange, so people sell goods for money and use the money to buy what they want, removing the need for a double coincidence of wants. It is a measure of value (unit of account), so every good can be given a price. It is a store of value, allowing saving, and a standard of deferred payment, allowing credit and loans. To perform these functions money must be acceptable, durable, portable, divisible, homogeneous, recognisable and limited in supply.

Without money, large-scale specialisation and modern commerce could not exist. Workers are paid wages in money, firms borrow and repay in money, and international trade is settled in currencies. Today money takes many forms, including notes and coins, bank deposits, cheques, debit and credit cards and electronic transfers made through mobile banking apps.

07

The Structure of Commerce: Trade and Aids to Trade

Commerce is the part of production concerned with the buying and selling of goods and services, together with all the activities that assist this process. It is divided into trade and aids to trade. Trade is the buying and selling itself. Home trade takes place within one country and consists of retail trade (selling in small quantities to final consumers) and wholesale trade (buying in bulk from producers and selling in smaller quantities to retailers). Foreign or international trade takes place between countries and consists of imports, exports and entrepot (re-export) trade.

The aids to trade are services that make trade possible or easier. Transport moves goods from where they are produced to where they are wanted, overcoming the barrier of distance. Warehousing stores goods until they are needed, overcoming the barrier of time. Banking provides finance and methods of payment, overcoming the problem of finance and payment. Insurance protects traders against risks such as fire, theft and loss at sea. Communication lets buyers and sellers exchange information quickly. Advertising informs consumers about products and persuades them to buy, overcoming the barrier of ignorance.

Each aid removes a particular obstacle, or hindrance, to trade. Candidates should be able to link each aid to the hindrance it removes, because this is one of the most frequently examined points in the syllabus.

  • Distance (place): overcome by transport.
  • Time: overcome by warehousing.
  • Finance and payment: overcome by banking.
  • Risk: overcome by insurance.
  • Ignorance or lack of information: overcome by advertising and communication.
  • Distance between buyer and seller (for orders and negotiation): overcome by communication.
08

The Importance of Commerce

Commerce benefits consumers by giving them access to a wide variety of goods from all over the world, at convenient places and times, often with credit facilities and after-sales service. Competition between traders keeps prices down and quality up. Consumers in a small town can buy fruit from another continent, electronics from East Asia and medicine made in Europe because of commerce.

Commerce benefits producers by finding markets for their output, providing finance, reducing risk through insurance and supplying information about consumer tastes. This allows firms to specialise and produce on a large scale, gaining economies of scale. For the country, commerce creates employment in retailing, transport, banking and other services, raises living standards, earns foreign exchange through exports and generates tax revenue for the government. It also encourages international understanding and cooperation through trade links.

Commerce also brings challenges, such as the risk of exploitation of consumers through misleading advertising or unfair prices, which is why governments pass consumer protection laws. Overall, however, a well-developed commercial system is a sign of a developed economy, and weak transport, banking and communication systems are a major barrier to growth in many poorer regions.

10

Worked Examples

Chain of production for a loaf of bread

Using a loaf of bread as an example, describe the chain of production and identify the sector of each stage. Show where commercial services are used.

  1. Primary stage: a farmer grows and harvests wheat. This is extractive production.
  2. Secondary stage: a flour mill grinds the wheat into flour, and a bakery mixes and bakes the flour into bread. Both are manufacturing.
  3. Tertiary stage: a wholesaler or distributor buys bread in bulk and a retailer sells single loaves to consumers.
  4. Commercial services throughout: lorries transport wheat to the mill and bread to shops; banks provide loans to the farmer and payment systems for all parties; insurers cover the mill against fire; the bakery advertises its brand; the wholesaler may store flour in a warehouse.
Wheat farming (primary) leads to milling and baking (secondary), then wholesaling and retailing (tertiary), with transport, banking, insurance, warehousing, communication and advertising supporting each link. Value is added at every stage.

Evaluating division of labour in a garment factory

A garment factory in Bangladesh changes from each worker making a complete shirt to each worker doing one task (cutting, stitching sleeves, sewing buttons, ironing, packing). Discuss whether this is beneficial. [6 marks]

  1. Identify benefits to the firm: output per worker rises because workers become faster and more skilled at one task; less time is wasted moving between tasks; specialised machines such as button-sewing machines can be used; unit costs fall.
  2. Identify benefits to workers and consumers: training is quicker and cheaper; shirts become cheaper and quality more consistent.
  3. Identify drawbacks: work becomes monotonous, which may reduce motivation and increase absenteeism; workers lose the skill of making a whole shirt and may struggle to find other work; if the stitching section stops, the whole line stops.
  4. Reach a judgement: for a firm producing large volumes for export, the cost savings are likely to outweigh the drawbacks, especially if job rotation is used to reduce boredom.
Division of labour is likely to be beneficial overall because it raises productivity and lowers unit costs, making the firm more competitive in export markets, but management should use job rotation and good working conditions to reduce boredom and the risk of stoppages.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Saying that only manufacturing is production. In Commerce, anyone who helps get goods to the consumer, including a transporter or banker, is productive.
  • Confusing commercial services with direct services. Banking and insurance are commercial services because they help trade; a doctor or teacher provides a direct service.
  • Treating specialisation and division of labour as identical. Division of labour is one type of specialisation, applied to tasks within the production of a single good.
  • Listing aids to trade without linking each one to the hindrance it removes. Always state, for example, that warehousing overcomes the problem of time.
  • Putting construction in the primary sector. Building is secondary production because it turns materials into a finished structure.
  • Describing barter problems vaguely. Name the specific problem, such as the lack of a double coincidence of wants or indivisibility of goods.

Exam tips

  • When asked for an example of each sector, choose clear examples linked to the same product, such as cotton, cloth and a clothes shop, to show understanding of the chain.
  • In 'explain' questions, give the point and then develop it with a consequence, for example 'specialised workers become skilled, so output per worker rises and unit costs fall'.
  • Learn the six aids to trade with a memory aid such as 'The Clever Boy Is Wearing Armour' for Transport, Communication, Banking, Insurance, Warehousing, Advertising.
  • For 'discuss' or 'evaluate' questions, always include both advantages and disadvantages and finish with a justified conclusion.
  • If a diagram of the structure of commerce is requested, draw a clear tree: Commerce splits into Trade and Aids to Trade; Trade splits into Home and Foreign; Home into Retail and Wholesale; Foreign into Import, Export and Entrepot.
  • Use local examples where possible, such as a named port, bank or supermarket, because they show application to a real context.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. Define production.

Model answerProduction is the creation of goods and services to satisfy human wants (1). It is complete only when the product reaches the final consumer (1).
[3 marks]

2. Give one example each of primary, secondary and tertiary production.

Model answerPrimary: fishing, farming, mining or forestry (1). Secondary: car manufacturing, sugar refining, construction (1). Tertiary: banking, retailing, transport, teaching (1).
[2 marks]

3. State the difference between commercial services and direct services.

Model answerCommercial services help the movement and exchange of goods, for example banking, insurance or transport (1). Direct services are personal services provided to individuals, for example doctors or teachers (1).
[4 marks]

4. Name four aids to trade.

Model answerAny four from transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing and advertising (1 mark each).
[4 marks]

5. Explain two problems of barter.

Model answerLack of double coincidence of wants: each trader must find someone who has what they want and wants what they have, which wastes time (2). Difficulty of valuing goods: there is no common measure, so it is hard to agree how many bags of rice equal one goat (2). Other acceptable points: indivisibility of large goods, difficulty storing wealth, no standard of deferred payment.
[4 marks]

6. State four functions of money.

Model answerMedium of exchange (1); measure of value or unit of account (1); store of value (1); standard of deferred payment (1).
[4 marks]

7. Explain how warehousing and insurance help trade.

Model answerWarehousing overcomes the barrier of time by storing goods between production and consumption, allowing seasonal goods to be available all year and stocks to be held to meet demand (2). Insurance overcomes the problem of risk by compensating traders for losses such as fire or theft, so traders are willing to hold stock and ship goods (2).
[5 marks]

8. Draw and label the structure of commerce.

Model answerCommerce divides into Trade and Aids to Trade (1). Trade divides into Home Trade and Foreign Trade (1). Home Trade divides into Retail and Wholesale (1). Foreign Trade divides into Import, Export and Entrepot (1). Aids to Trade: transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing, advertising (1).
[4 marks]

9. Explain why specialisation makes commerce necessary.

Model answerSpecialised workers and firms produce only one product or task, so they produce a surplus of it and lack everything else (2). They must exchange their surplus for other goods, which requires trade, money and aids to trade such as transport and banking to link producers with consumers (2).
[5 marks]

10. Using a named product, describe its chain of production.

Model answerFor example, furniture: forestry workers cut down trees (primary) (1); sawmills cut timber and a furniture factory makes tables (secondary) (2); wholesalers and furniture shops sell them to consumers (tertiary) (1); transport, banking, insurance and advertising assist throughout (1). Credit is given for correctly identifying each sector.
[8 marks]

11. Discuss the advantages and disadvantages of the division of labour to workers.

Model answerAdvantages: workers become skilled and efficient at one task (1), training is quick (1), they can choose work suited to their talents (1) and higher productivity may bring higher wages (1). Disadvantages: repetitive tasks are boring and reduce job satisfaction (1), workers lose general craft skills (1), they may become unemployed if machines replace them or demand falls (1). Conclusion: benefits are real but the monotony and risk of unemployment can be reduced by job rotation and training (1).
[8 marks]

12. Assess the importance of commerce to a developing country.

Model answerCommerce creates jobs in retail, transport, banking and insurance (1–2). It links producers such as farmers to wider markets, raising their incomes (1–2). Banks provide finance for investment and insurance reduces risk, encouraging enterprise (1–2). Exports earn foreign exchange to pay for imports of machinery (1–2). Weak commerce, such as poor roads or few banks, limits growth (1). Judgement: commerce is vital because without efficient trade and aids to trade, specialisation and economic development cannot happen (1–2).

Quick Recap

  1. Production creates goods and services to satisfy unlimited human wants and is complete only when products reach the consumer.
  2. Primary production extracts resources, secondary production manufactures and constructs, and tertiary production provides services.
  3. Tertiary production includes commercial services that help trade and direct services provided to individuals.
  4. Commerce consists of trade (home and foreign) and the aids to trade: transport, communication, banking, insurance, warehousing and advertising.
  5. Each aid to trade removes a hindrance: distance, time, finance, risk or lack of information.
  6. Specialisation and division of labour raise productivity but cause boredom, loss of skills and interdependence.
  7. Money replaced barter by acting as a medium of exchange, measure of value, store of value and standard of deferred payment.
  8. The chain of production shows how the three sectors are interdependent and how value is added at each stage.
