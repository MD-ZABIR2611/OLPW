Chapter Overview

What is it?

Electrolysis is the decomposition of an ionic compound (molten or in solution) by passing electricity through it. This chapter explains how ions behave under electric current, how to predict what forms at each electrode, and how to represent these processes using half-equations.

Why it Matters

Electrolysis is fundamental to modern industry. It is used to extract highly reactive metals (like aluminium), purify copper for electronics, and manufacture essential chemicals like chlorine and sodium hydroxide (brine electrolysis).

What You Will Learn

  • Difference between electrolytes and non-electrolytes.
  • Movement of ions to anode and cathode.
  • Writing half-equations for oxidation and reduction.
  • Predicting products using the reactivity series.
  • Industrial applications (Aluminium extraction, Copper purification, Brine).
  • Electroplating principles and setup.

03

Detailed Theory

01

Conductors & Electrolytes

Definition

Electrolyte: A molten or aqueous ionic compound that conducts electricity and is decomposed by it. (e.g., Molten NaCl, CuSO₄ solution).

Non-electrolyte: A substance that does not conduct electricity (no free-moving ions). (e.g., Solid NaCl, pure water, sugar solution).

Why do ionic compounds conduct?

In solid state, ions are locked in a lattice and cannot move. When melted or dissolved, the ionic bonds break, and ions become free to move and carry charge.

Exam Connection

Exams often ask why solid sodium chloride doesn't conduct but molten/aqueous does. Key phrase: "Ions are free to move".

02

The Electrolysis Setup

Apparatus

Requires a DC power source, an electrolyte, and two electrodes dipped into the electrolyte.

Cathode (Negative Electrode): Attracts cations (positive ions).
Anode (Positive Electrode): Attracts anions (negative ions).

Inert Electrodes: Do not react (e.g., Platinum, Carbon/Graphite). Reactive Electrodes: Take part in the reaction (e.g., Copper in purification).

03

Redox in Electrolysis

Oxidation & Reduction

Electrolysis is a redox process. Remember: OIL RIG (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain of electrons).

At Anode (+): Negative ions lose electrons. This is Oxidation.

At Cathode (-): Positive ions gain electrons. This is Reduction.

Half-Equations

Equations showing the movement of electrons at one electrode.

Cathode (Reduction):
Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)
2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)

Anode (Oxidation):
2Cl⁻(aq) → Cl₂(g) + 2e⁻
4OH⁻(aq) → O₂(g) + 2H₂O(l) + 4e⁻

04

Molten vs Aqueous Electrolysis

Molten Compounds

Only the metal ion and non-metal ion are present. The metal forms at the cathode, the non-metal forms at the anode.

e.g., Molten Lead(II) Bromide (PbBr₂):
Cathode: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb (molten grey metal)
Anode: 2Br⁻ → Br₂ + 2e⁻ (red-brown gas)

Aqueous Solutions (Competition)

Water is present, providing H⁺ and OH⁻ ions. Ions compete to discharge.

Cathode Rule:
If metal is above H in reactivity series → H₂ is discharged.
If metal is below H → Metal is discharged.

Anode Rule:
If halide (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻) is present in high conc. → Halogen is discharged.
If no halide (or dilute) → OH⁻ is discharged (O₂ formed).

05

Important Facts

Reactivity Metal Ion Product at Cathode (Aqueous) Reason
High (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ Hydrogen (H₂) Metal is more reactive than H. H⁺ is easier to discharge.
Low (Zn, Fe, Cu, Ag) Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, Ag⁺ The Metal (M) Metal is less reactive than H. Metal ion is easier to discharge.


Anode Discharge Rules

1. Halides present (Concentrated): If the solution has a high concentration of Cl⁻, Br⁻, or I⁻, the halogen is discharged (e.g., Cl₂ gas).

2. No halides / Dilute: If the solution contains sulfate (SO₄²⁻) or nitrate (NO₃⁻), or is very dilute, hydroxide (OH⁻) is discharged, producing Oxygen (O₂) gas.

Electrode Observations

Metals (Cathode): Often form a solid deposit. (e.g., Cu = brown/pink deposit, Pb = silvery deposit).

Gases (Both): Bubbles form.
H₂ = 'pop' with lit splint.
O₂ = relights glowing splint.
Cl₂ = pungent, bleaches damp litmus paper.
Br₂ = red-brown gas.

06

Equations & Half-Equations

CATHODE (Reduction - Gain of e⁻)
Mn+ + ne- → M

Examples:

Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu

2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂

Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al

ANODE (Oxidation - Loss of e⁻)
Xn- → X + ne-

Examples:

2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻

2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻

07

Worked Examples

CASE STUDY 01

BASIC / MOLTEN
Question: Predict the products and write half-equations for the electrolysis of molten magnesium chloride (MgCl₂).
Think: It is molten, so no water/competition. Mg²⁺ goes to cathode, Cl⁻ goes to anode.
Answer: Cathode: Magnesium (Mg). Anode: Chlorine (Cl₂).
Half-Equations:
Cathode (Reduction): Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg
Anode (Oxidation): 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻

CASE STUDY 02

INTERMEDIATE / AQUEOUS
Question: Predict products and write half-equations for the electrolysis of dilute copper(II) sulfate (CuSO₄) using inert electrodes.
Think Cathode: Cu is below H in reactivity series. Cu²⁺ discharges.
Think Anode: No halides present. OH⁻ discharges (from water), producing O₂.
Answer: Cathode: Copper. Anode: Oxygen.
Half-Equations:
Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu
Anode: 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻
Observation: Blue colour of solution fades (Cu²⁺ removed).

CASE STUDY 03

ADVANCED / BRINE
Question: Explain the electrolysis of concentrated aqueous sodium chloride (brine). Give products, half-equations, and the remaining solution.
Think Cathode: Na is above H. H⁺ discharges → H₂ gas.
Think Anode: Cl⁻ is present in high concentration → Cl₂ gas.
Half-Equations:
Cathode: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂
Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
Remaining Solution: Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions are left behind, forming sodium hydroxide (NaOH).
08

Industrial Chemistry Case Studies

CASE STUDY 01

Extraction of Aluminium

Electrolyte: Alumina (Al₂O₃) dissolved in molten cryolite (lowers m.p., saves energy).

Cathode (Steel): Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al (molten aluminium forms).

Anode (Carbon): 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻.

Catch: Oxygen reacts with the hot carbon anode to form CO₂. Anodes burn away and must be replaced regularly.

CASE STUDY 02

Purification of Copper

Setup: Anode = Impure copper. Cathode = Pure copper. Electrolyte = CuSO₄ + H₂SO₄.

Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ (impure copper dissolves).

Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (pure copper deposits).

Result: Impurities fall to the bottom as "anode sludge" (often contains valuable silver/gold).

CASE STUDY 03

Electroplating

Purpose: Coat a cheap metal with a more attractive, corrosion-resistant metal (e.g., silver plating).

Cathode: The object to be plated (e.g., a spoon).

Anode: The plating metal (e.g., silver).

Electrolyte: A solution of the plating metal ions (e.g., AgNO₃).

09

Laboratory Protocol

EXPERIMENT 01

Electrolysis of Copper(II) Sulfate (Inert Electrodes)


Objective: Observe the electrolysis of CuSO₄(aq) using carbon electrodes.

Apparatus: Beaker, CuSO₄ solution, 2 carbon rods, DC power supply, connecting wires, test tubes (for gas).

Method:
1. Pour CuSO₄ into beaker.
2. Connect carbon rods to DC supply.
3. Turn on power. Observe electrodes and solution.
4. Test gases if formed.

Variables:
Independent: Electrode material.
Dependent: Products at electrodes.

Safety: CuSO₄ is harmful. Wear goggles. Do not touch bare wires when power is on.

Observations:
- Cathode: Brown/pink deposit (Cu).
- Anode: Bubbles of colourless gas (O₂). Relights glowing splint.
- Solution: Blue colour fades.

Errors: Pieces of carbon breaking off and contaminating the deposit.

Exam Protocol

Predict

Meaning: State what will form using rules.

Write: "At the cathode, Hydrogen will form because sodium is above hydrogen in the reactivity series."

Mistake: Forgetting to check if the metal is above or below hydrogen.

Write Half-Eq

Meaning: Show ion turning to atom with electrons.

Write: 2Cl⁻ - 2e⁻ → Cl₂ (or 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻).

Mistake: Unbalanced charges or atoms. Forgetting state symbols.

Explain

Meaning: Give the scientific reason for a process.

Write: "Copper forms at the cathode because it is less reactive than hydrogen."

Mistake: Saying "because it is positive" instead of explaining the reactivity.

12

Error Database

Wrong

At the anode, positive ions move and gain electrons.

Correct

At the anode (+), negative ions (anions) move and lose electrons (oxidation).

Wrong

Solid lead(II) bromide conducts electricity because it has ions.

Correct

Solid PbBr₂ does NOT conduct. Ions are fixed in a lattice. It must be molten so ions are free to move.

Wrong

Electrolysis of dilute NaCl produces Sodium at the cathode.

Correct

Na is above H in reactivity. H⁺ discharges instead. Hydrogen gas (H₂) is produced.

Rapid Revision

Electrode Rules

  • Cathode (-): Cations go here. Reduction.
  • Anode (+): Anions go here. Oxidation.
  • OIL RIG: Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain.
  • Cathode Product: Metal if below H, else H₂.
  • Anode Product: Halogen if present, else O₂.

Industrial Facts

  • Aluminium: Cryolite lowers m.p. of Al₂O₃. Carbon anodes burn away.
  • Brine: Produces H₂ (cathode), Cl₂ (anode), NaOH (solution).
  • Copper Purification: Anode dissolves, Cathode grows.
  • Electroplating: Object is cathode, plating metal is anode.

Exam Simulation

Basic 3 Marks
1. Molten potassium iodide is electrolysed. Predict the products at the anode and cathode, and write the half-equation for the anode.
Mark Scheme

Cathode: Potassium (K) — 1 mark

Anode: Iodine (I₂) — 1 mark

Half-equation: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻ — 1 mark

Intermediate 4 Marks
2. Aqueous copper(II) chloride is electrolysed using inert electrodes. (a) Predict the products at both electrodes. (b) Write the half-equation at the cathode. (c) State one observation at the anode.
Mark Scheme

(a) Cathode: Copper. Anode: Chlorine. (2 marks)

(b) Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (1 mark)

(c) Bubbles of green-yellow gas / bleaches damp litmus paper. (1 mark)

Advanced 6 Marks
3. Explain why in the electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid, the volume of gas collected at the cathode is twice the volume of gas collected at the anode. Include half-equations in your answer.
Mark Scheme

Cathode produces H₂, Anode produces O₂. (2 marks)

Half-equations: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ AND 4OH⁻ → O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ (or 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻). (2 marks)

Explanation: To produce 1 mole of O₂, 4 moles of electrons are needed. 4 moles of electrons produce 2 moles of H₂. Therefore, H₂ volume is twice O₂ volume. (2 marks)

Mark Scheme Analysis

Common Mark Scheme Points


  • Always specify state symbols in half-equations if asked (e.g., (g), (aq), (s)).
  • When explaining Aluminium extraction, specifically mention cryolite and that it lowers the melting point to save energy/costs.
  • For Copper purification, always state that the anode is impure copper and the cathode is pure copper.
  • When explaining conductivity, use the exact phrase: "Ions are free to move".
