Chapter 17 · Commerce

Business Finance & Sources of Capital

Every firm needs capital to start and grow: money raised from within the business or from outside lenders and investors, with each source differing in cost, duration, risk and control surrendered.

Key Concepts

01

Capital is the money a business needs to start, run and expand; sources are classed as internal (generated within the firm) or external, and as short-term (repayable within one year), medium-term or long-term.

02

Internal sources include retained profits, the sale of surplus assets and tighter control of stock and debtors; they are cheap, need no outside approval and involve no loss of control, but the amount available is limited and only established firms have them.

03

Short-term external sources include a bank overdraft, which is flexible and charges interest only on the amount overdrawn; trade credit, which lets a firm take goods now and pay the supplier after 30-90 days; and debt factoring, the sale of unpaid invoices to a factor at a discount for immediate cash.

04

Long-term external sources include bank loans with fixed repayments and interest, hire purchase and leasing for machinery and vehicles, debentures (long-term loans paying fixed interest, repaid on maturity) and share capital — ordinary shares carry votes and variable dividends, preference shares carry a fixed dividend paid first but usually no vote.

05

Venture capitalists and business angels invest in risky start-ups in return for an equity stake; crowdfunding raises small sums from many people online; microfinance, pioneered by the Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, lends tiny amounts to poor entrepreneurs, and governments give grants to favoured activities.

06

The choice of source depends on the purpose (working capital or fixed assets), the amount and duration needed, the cost in interest or dividends, the gearing and risk, and whether owners will surrender control; financing long-term assets with an overdraft is a mismatch that creates liquidity crises.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Working capitalCurrent assets minus current liabilities: the funds available for the day-to-day running of a business.
Retained profitProfit kept in the business after tax and dividends for reinvestment rather than paid out to owners.
OverdraftA short-term bank facility allowing a firm to spend more than it holds in its account, with interest charged only on the amount overdrawn.
Trade creditA supplier's agreement letting a firm receive goods now and pay for them after an agreed period, usually 30-90 days.
DebentureA long-term loan to a company that pays a fixed rate of interest and is repayable on a set date.
Ordinary shareA unit of company ownership carrying voting rights and a dividend that varies with the profit made.
Preference shareA share paying a fixed dividend that is paid before any ordinary dividend, usually without voting rights.
Venture capitalFinance invested in high-risk, high-growth new businesses in return for an equity stake in the firm.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

For any source-of-finance question, first match the length of the finance to the life of the asset — never fund a factory with an overdraft — then compare cost, risk and loss of control, since each of these earns a separate mark.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

State two internal and two external sources of finance available to a business.

Model answerInternal: retained (ploughed-back) profits from previous years (1). Internal: sale of surplus assets such as unused machinery or buildings (1). External: a bank loan or overdraft (1). External: selling shares (share capital) or issuing debentures (1).
CHALLENGE

A company plans to build a new factory costing $5 million that will earn income for 20 years. Evaluate two long-term sources of finance it could use.

Model answerIssuing ordinary shares raises permanent capital with no repayment date and no interest, and the dividend rises only with profits, so it suits a risky 20-year project (1). However, it dilutes the original owners' control and dividends may be demanded even in bad years (1). Issuing debentures keeps ownership intact, gives tax-efficient fixed interest and a known maturity, suiting stable repayment (1). But interest must be paid even in loss-making years, and high gearing raises the risk of bankruptcy if trading fails (1). Overall shares suit high risk, debentures suit stable cash flow — many firms use a mixture (1).
