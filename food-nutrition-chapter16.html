Flour, Pastry, Cake and Bread Making

Every flour-based product is a controlled experiment in structure and aeration: how gluten is developed or limited, how gas is trapped, and how heat sets and browns the crumb.

Key Concepts

01

Bread needs strong flour and kneading: high-protein flour forms plenty of gluten, yeast ferments sugars to carbon dioxide which the elastic gluten stretches around, and oven heat sets the structure while browning the crust.

02

Aeration methods vary by product: rubbing in traps air for pastry, creaming traps air for cakes, whisking folds it in for sponges, and chemical raising agents release carbon dioxide in the heat of the oven.

03

Shortcrust pastry stays short because fat coats the flour particles, limiting gluten development; cold fat, minimal water and gentle handling keep it tender, and resting relaxes gluten so the pastry does not shrink in the oven.

04

In cakes the balance of sugar, fat, flour and egg controls texture: too much fat or sugar weakens the structure so the cake collapses or sinks, while too much flour makes it dry and heavy, and eggs set the crumb as their proteins coagulate.

05

Gelatinisation of starch — swelling and bursting of starch granules as they absorb water above about 60 °C — thickens sauces, sets the crumb of baked goods and is the setting mechanism in choux pastry and roux-based sauces.

06

Caramelisation of sugars above roughly 160 °C and Maillard browning between reducing sugars and proteins give baked goods colour and flavour, which is why pale pastry is brushed with egg or milk to promote browning.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Gluten developmentThe formation of an elastic gluten network when flour is mixed with water and worked, trapping gas and giving structure to bread and pastry.
AerationThe incorporation of air or gas into a mixture so that baked goods rise and have a light, open texture.
Rubbing-in methodA cake or pastry technique in which cold fat is rubbed into flour to coat the particles, limit gluten and trap air for a crumbly texture.
Creaming methodA cake-making method in which fat and sugar are beaten together to trap air before eggs and flour are added.
GelatinisationThe swelling and bursting of starch granules as they absorb water on heating, thickening sauces and setting the crumb of baked goods.
CaramelisationThe browning of sugars at high temperatures, which develops colour and a characteristic flavour in baked goods and confectionery.
Maillard reactionBrowning and flavour development from reactions between reducing sugars and amino acids at baking temperatures, e.g. on bread crusts.
Raising agentA substance that produces gas, usually carbon dioxide, to make mixtures rise, such as yeast or baking powder.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Tabulate flour-based products by structure source and aeration method: bread (strong gluten plus yeast), pastry (limited gluten plus trapped air), cakes (egg and creamed fat plus chemical agents); comparison tables earn marks quickly in explain-the-difference questions.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

For bread making, name the raising agent and the structural protein, and state two ways aeration is achieved in a Victoria sandwich cake.

Model answerRaising agent: yeast, which ferments sugars to release carbon dioxide (1). Structural protein: gluten, formed from gliadin and glutenin in the flour (1). Creaming the fat and sugar traps air (1). A chemical raising agent releases carbon dioxide in the oven heat (1).
CHALLENGE

Explain why pastry is handled lightly and rested while bread dough is kneaded, with reference to gluten.

Model answerPastry needs a short, crumbly texture, so cold fat coats the flour particles and limits the water reaching the proteins, restricting gluten development (1). Light handling avoids stretching the gluten that does form, keeping the pastry tender rather than elastic (1). Resting relaxes gluten strands, so the cooked pastry keeps its shape instead of shrinking (1). Bread needs the opposite: kneading develops a strong, elastic gluten network that stretches around the carbon dioxide from yeast, giving a risen, well-structured loaf (1). Under-kneaded bread has a weak network, so gas escapes and the loaf is dense and close-textured (1).
