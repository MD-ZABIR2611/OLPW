Plate Tectonics

The structure of the Earth, the movement of tectonic plates, the landforms and hazards produced at plate boundaries, and how people live with earthquakes and volcanoes.

01

Overview

The Earth's surface is broken into large moving slabs called tectonic plates, and almost all earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains occur where these plates meet. Tectonic hazards can kill hundreds of thousands of people in minutes, as in Haiti in 2010, yet millions choose to live close to active volcanoes and fault lines. In the exam you must be able to describe the global distribution of earthquakes and volcanoes, explain the processes at each type of plate boundary, and compare the causes, impacts and responses of named hazard events in countries at different levels of development.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Describe the internal structure of the Earth and explain why tectonic plates move.
  • Describe the global distribution of earthquakes, volcanoes and fold mountains.
  • Explain the processes at destructive, constructive, collision and conservative plate boundaries.
  • Describe the main features of volcanoes and earthquakes, including magnitude and measurement.
  • Explain why people continue to live in areas at risk from tectonic hazards.
  • Compare the causes, impacts and responses of earthquakes and eruptions in HICs and LICs.
  • Evaluate methods of prediction, protection and preparation to reduce tectonic risk.
02

Structure of the Earth and Plate Movement

The Earth has four main layers. At the centre is the solid inner core, made of iron and nickel, with temperatures of about 5,000-6,000 °C. Around it is the liquid outer core. The mantle, nearly 2,900 km thick, is made of hot silicate rock; its upper part, the asthenosphere, is semi-molten and able to flow slowly. The outermost layer is the crust, which is thin continental crust (about 30-70 km thick, older, less dense granite) or oceanic crust (about 6-10 km thick, younger, denser basalt).

The crust and the rigid top of the mantle together form the lithosphere, which is broken into seven major plates and many smaller ones. Plates move a few centimetres a year, roughly the speed fingernails grow. Movement is driven by convection currents in the mantle, as heat from the core causes magma to rise, spread and sink, dragging plates with it. Slab pull, where dense oceanic plate sinks at subduction zones and pulls the rest of the plate, and ridge push at spreading ridges are also important.

Key fact:Oceanic crust is thinner, younger and denser than continental crust, so it sinks (subducts) when the two collide.
03

Global Distribution of Tectonic Activity

Earthquakes and volcanoes are not randomly distributed; they occur in long, narrow belts along plate boundaries. The most important is the Pacific Ring of Fire, a horseshoe of boundaries around the Pacific Ocean running through New Zealand, Indonesia, the Philippines, Japan, Alaska and down the west coasts of North and South America. About 75% of the world's active volcanoes and around 90% of earthquakes occur here.

Other belts include the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, which runs through Iceland; the Alpine-Himalayan belt from southern Europe through Turkey, Iran and the Himalayas; and the East African Rift Valley. A few volcanoes, such as those in Hawaii, occur far from boundaries above hotspots, where a plume of magma rises through the middle of a plate. Earthquakes occur at all types of plate boundary, but volcanoes are found only at destructive and constructive boundaries and hotspots, not at conservative or collision boundaries.

04

Plate Boundaries

At a destructive (convergent) boundary, oceanic crust moves towards continental crust. The denser oceanic plate is forced down beneath the continental plate in a subduction zone, forming a deep ocean trench. Friction causes powerful earthquakes, and as the plate sinks it melts; the magma rises through cracks to form explosive composite volcanoes. The Nazca Plate subducting beneath the South American Plate created the Andes and the Peru-Chile Trench, while the Pacific Plate subducting beneath Japan caused the 2011 Tohoku earthquake.

At a constructive (divergent) boundary, plates move apart. Magma rises to fill the gap, forming new crust, gentle shield volcanoes and mid-ocean ridges; earthquakes are usually small and shallow. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the North American and Eurasian plates separate by about 2.5 cm per year, emerges above sea level in Iceland. At a collision boundary two continental plates meet; neither sinks, so the crust crumples upwards to form fold mountains. The Indian Plate colliding with the Eurasian Plate formed the Himalayas, including Mount Everest at 8,849 m.

At a conservative (transform) boundary, plates slide past each other in opposite directions or in the same direction at different speeds. Crust is neither created nor destroyed and there are no volcanoes, but plates stick and pressure builds until it is suddenly released as an earthquake. The San Andreas Fault in California, between the Pacific and North American plates, is the best-known example; it caused the 1906 San Francisco earthquake.

  • Destructive: ocean-continent, subduction, trench, composite volcanoes, strong earthquakes (Andes, Japan).
  • Constructive: plates diverge, shield volcanoes, ridges, small earthquakes (Iceland).
  • Collision: continent-continent, fold mountains, earthquakes, no volcanoes (Himalayas).
  • Conservative: plates slide past, earthquakes, no volcanoes (San Andreas Fault).
05

Volcanoes

A volcano is an opening in the Earth's crust through which magma, gases and ash erupt. Its main features are a magma chamber, a main vent, side vents, a crater and layers of lava and ash. Composite (strato) volcanoes, found at destructive boundaries, are steep-sided cones made of alternating layers of ash and viscous andesitic lava. Their thick, sticky, gas-rich lava causes explosive eruptions with pyroclastic flows, ash clouds and lahars; Mount St Helens (1980) and Mount Merapi in Indonesia are examples.

Shield volcanoes, found at constructive boundaries and hotspots, are broad with gentle slopes, formed by runny, low-silica basaltic lava that flows long distances. Eruptions are frequent but gentle, such as Mauna Loa and Kilauea in Hawaii. Volcanoes are classified as active (erupted recently and likely to erupt again), dormant (not erupted for a long time but could erupt) or extinct (not expected to erupt again).

  • Pyroclastic flow: fast-moving cloud of hot gas, ash and rock, over 100 km/h.
  • Lahar: volcanic mudflow of ash mixed with water from rain or melted snow.
  • Ash fall: collapses roofs, damages crops, disrupts aircraft.
  • Volcanic gases: sulphur dioxide and carbon dioxide can suffocate or cause acid rain.
06

Earthquakes

An earthquake is a sudden, violent shaking of the ground caused by the release of pressure that has built up along a plate boundary or fault. The point underground where the rocks break is the focus, and the point on the surface directly above it is the epicentre. Energy travels outwards as seismic waves, which are strongest near the epicentre. Shallow-focus earthquakes (less than 70 km deep) usually cause more damage than deep-focus ones because less energy is lost before reaching the surface.

Earthquakes are recorded by seismometers. Magnitude, the energy released, is measured on the Richter scale or the more modern moment magnitude scale. Both are logarithmic: each whole number increase represents ten times greater ground movement and about 32 times more energy. The Mercalli scale measures intensity, the observed effects and damage, from I to XII. Secondary hazards include tsunamis, landslides, liquefaction of soft sediment, fires from broken gas pipes and disease from contaminated water.

Key fact:An earthquake of magnitude 7 produces 10 times greater shaking and about 32 times more energy than one of magnitude 6.
07

Why People Live in Hazardous Areas

Hundreds of millions of people live near active plate boundaries. Volcanic soils weathered from lava and ash are very fertile, supporting intensive farming, as on the slopes of Mount Merapi in Java and Mount Etna in Sicily. Volcanic areas offer geothermal energy, as in Iceland where about 90% of homes are heated geothermally, and minerals such as sulphur, gold and copper. Volcanoes attract tourists, creating jobs.

Other reasons include the fact that many hazard zones contain major cities with jobs and services, such as Tokyo, Los Angeles and Istanbul; people may have family and cultural ties to the land; they may be too poor to move; eruptions and big earthquakes are infrequent, so people believe it will not happen to them; and modern technology, building design and warning systems in HICs make people feel safe.

08

Reducing the Impacts: Prediction, Protection and Preparation

Volcanic eruptions can often be predicted because they give warning signs: small earthquakes as magma rises, swelling of the volcano's surface measured by tiltmeters and GPS, increased gas emissions such as sulphur dioxide, and rising ground temperature detected by satellites. These allowed around 350,000 people to be evacuated before the 2010 Merapi eruption. Earthquakes cannot be accurately predicted, but hazard maps show high-risk areas and early warning systems can give seconds of warning; Japan's system automatically stops bullet trains.

Protection reduces damage. Earthquake-resistant buildings use deep foundations, cross-bracing, steel frames, rubber shock absorbers in the foundations (base isolation) and counterweights on the roof. Building codes, as in Japan, Chile and California, make these compulsory. Sea walls and tsunami barriers protect coasts. Preparation includes public education, earthquake drills (Japan holds Disaster Prevention Day on 1 September), emergency kits, evacuation routes and trained rescue teams. LICs often cannot afford these measures, so the same size earthquake kills far more people.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Tectonic plateA large rigid section of the lithosphere that moves slowly over the asthenosphere.
Convection currentThe circulation of heat within the mantle, where hot material rises and cooler material sinks, driving plate movement.
Destructive boundaryA plate margin where oceanic crust is subducted beneath continental crust, causing earthquakes and explosive volcanoes.
Constructive boundaryA plate margin where plates move apart and magma rises to form new crust.
Conservative boundaryA plate margin where plates slide past each other, causing earthquakes but no volcanoes.
Collision boundaryA plate margin where two continental plates meet and crumple upwards to form fold mountains.
Subduction zoneThe area where a denser oceanic plate sinks beneath another plate and melts.
FocusThe point below the Earth's surface where an earthquake originates.
EpicentreThe point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus of an earthquake.
MagnitudeThe amount of energy released by an earthquake, measured on the Richter or moment magnitude scale.
TsunamiA series of large ocean waves caused by the sudden displacement of water, usually by an undersea earthquake.
Pyroclastic flowA fast-moving, extremely hot cloud of gas, ash and rock fragments that flows down the side of a volcano.
LaharA volcanic mudflow formed when ash mixes with water from heavy rain or melting snow and ice.
HotspotAn area of volcanic activity away from plate boundaries, caused by a plume of hot magma rising from the mantle.

Worked Examples

Case study comparison: Haiti earthquake 2010 (LIC) and Tohoku earthquake, Japan 2011 (HIC)

Compare the impacts of, and responses to, earthquakes in an LIC and an HIC, and explain why the impacts were different. [7 marks]

  1. Haiti, 12 January 2010: magnitude 7.0, shallow focus of about 13 km, epicentre about 25 km from the capital Port-au-Prince on a conservative boundary between the Caribbean and North American plates.
  2. Haiti impacts: estimates of deaths range from about 100,000 to 316,000; around 1.5 million people made homeless; the presidential palace, main hospital, port and airport control tower were damaged; a cholera outbreak from October 2010 killed nearly 10,000 people.
  3. Haiti responses: dependence on international aid, with search teams and field hospitals from many countries; aid was slowed by the damaged port and blocked roads; hundreds of thousands still lived in tent camps a year later.
  4. Japan, 11 March 2011: magnitude 9.0-9.1 at a destructive boundary where the Pacific Plate subducts beneath the plate carrying northern Japan, about 70 km off the Tohoku coast.
  5. Japan impacts: most of the approximately 18,000 deaths were caused by a tsunami up to 40 m high rather than by shaking; the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant suffered meltdowns and over 150,000 people were evacuated; the World Bank estimated damage at about 235 billion US dollars, the costliest natural disaster recorded.
  6. Japan responses: early warning systems stopped trains and alerted residents; well-trained emergency services and 100,000 Self-Defence Force troops responded; strict building codes meant few buildings collapsed from shaking; reconstruction was largely funded by the government.
Haiti's weaker earthquake killed far more people because of poverty, poorly constructed concrete buildings, dense informal housing, weak government and lack of preparation, and its response relied on foreign aid. Japan's much stronger earthquake caused fewer deaths relative to its size because of strict building codes, warning systems and preparedness, but it caused far greater economic losses because of high-value infrastructure and the nuclear disaster.

Case study: Eruption of Mount Merapi, Indonesia, 2010

For a named volcanic eruption, explain its cause and describe its effects and the responses. [6 marks]

  1. Location and cause: Mount Merapi in central Java is a composite volcano on the destructive boundary where the Indo-Australian Plate subducts beneath the Eurasian Plate, producing viscous, gas-rich magma.
  2. Eruption: a series of explosive eruptions from late October to November 2010 produced pyroclastic flows that travelled over 15 km down the slopes.
  3. Primary effects: about 350 people died, many from burns caused by pyroclastic flows; villages and farmland on the fertile lower slopes were buried.
  4. Secondary effects: ash disrupted flights at Yogyakarta airport; lahars during the rainy season destroyed bridges and farmland; farmers lost crops and livestock.
  5. Responses: monitoring by Indonesian volcanologists detected increased earthquakes and ground swelling, and the exclusion zone was extended to 20 km, allowing around 350,000 people to be evacuated to temporary shelters, which saved thousands of lives.
The 2010 Merapi eruption was caused by subduction at a destructive boundary. It killed about 350 people and devastated farmland, but effective monitoring and a large evacuation greatly reduced the death toll.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Saying volcanoes occur at conservative or collision boundaries. Volcanoes form at destructive and constructive boundaries and hotspots only.
  • Confusing focus and epicentre. The focus is underground where the rock breaks; the epicentre is on the surface directly above.
  • Writing that the Richter scale is linear. It is logarithmic, so magnitude 8 is ten times the shaking of magnitude 7, not a little bigger.
  • Assuming that deaths depend only on magnitude. Development level, building quality, population density, depth of focus and preparedness are often more important.
  • Stating that oceanic crust is lighter. Oceanic crust is denser, which is why it subducts beneath continental crust.
  • Giving vague case studies such as there was an earthquake in Japan and many died. Include date, magnitude, plate boundary, deaths and specific responses.

Exam tips

  • Draw and label annotated cross-sections of plate boundaries; labelled diagrams can earn full marks for explaining processes.
  • In compare questions, use connectives such as whereas and in contrast, and compare like with like (deaths versus deaths, responses versus responses).
  • Separate primary effects (caused directly by shaking or eruption) from secondary effects (tsunami, fire, disease, economic loss).
  • Organise reasons for living near volcanoes into economic (soil, tourism, geothermal, minerals) and social (family ties, poverty, confidence in technology).
  • For distribution questions on a world map, name specific boundaries and places, such as the west coast of South America or the Ring of Fire.
  • Use the three Ps, prediction, protection and preparation, to structure answers about reducing tectonic risk.

12. To what extent can the impacts of earthquakes be reduced? Use examples.

Model answerLevel-marked. Quick Recap

  1. The Earth has an inner core, outer core, mantle and crust; plates of lithosphere move over the asthenosphere.
  2. Convection currents, slab pull and ridge push move plates a few centimetres per year.
  3. Earthquakes and volcanoes occur in belts along plate boundaries, especially the Pacific Ring of Fire.
  4. Destructive boundaries produce trenches, explosive volcanoes and strong earthquakes; constructive boundaries produce ridges and shield volcanoes.
  5. Collision boundaries form fold mountains such as the Himalayas; conservative boundaries such as the San Andreas Fault produce earthquakes only.
  6. Magnitude scales are logarithmic; the impacts of an earthquake depend strongly on development and preparation.
  7. People live near hazards for fertile soils, geothermal energy, tourism, jobs and family ties.
  8. Prediction, protection and preparation reduce risk, as shown by Japan's response in 2011 compared with Haiti in 2010.
