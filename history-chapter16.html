Chapter 16 · History

The USA 1919-1941

America rode a wave of mass production and easy credit through the roaring twenties, then crashed into the deepest depression of the century, which only the Second World War finally ended.

Key Concepts

01

The 1920s boom rested on America's position after the First World War as the world's leading creditor and industrial power, driven by mass production — Henry Ford's moving assembly line of 1913 cut the price of the Model T from $850 in 1908 to under $300 — by electrification, new industries such as cars, radios and cinema, persuasive advertising and hire purchase, which let consumers buy on instalments.

02

Social tensions of the decade: Prohibition (1920-1933, under the 18th Amendment) was widely ignored through speakeasies and was exploited by gangsters such as Al Capone in Chicago; women won the vote (19th Amendment, 1920) and the flapper symbolised changing dress and behaviour; the Immigration Act of 1924 restricted newcomers through national quotas; black Americans and farmers, hit by falling crop prices, shared little in the prosperity.

03

Weaknesses beneath the boom: overproduction in farming and industry, a maldistribution of wealth — by 1929 the richest 5 per cent took about a third of national income — and shares bought on the margin with borrowed money (a 10 per cent deposit) inflated a stock-market bubble; high tariffs, notably Smoot-Hawley in 1930, and war-debt disputes then strangled world trade.

04

The Wall Street Crash of October 1929: prices peaked in September; panic selling on 24 October (Black Thursday) and 29 October (Black Tuesday), when about 16 million shares changed hands, collapsed share values to around a fifth of their 1929 level by 1932, wiping out investors and the banks that had lent on shares.

05

The Depression: about 9 000 banks failed between 1930 and 1933, destroying savings; unemployment reached about 12-13 million — roughly one worker in four — by 1933; drought turned the Great Plains into the Dust Bowl, driving destitute Okie farmers west; Hoover's belief in voluntarism, the shanty towns called Hoovervilles and the violent dispersal of the Bonus Marchers (1932) discredited his presidency.

06

Roosevelt's New Deal from March 1933: a bank holiday restored confidence, Prohibition was repealed, and the alphabet agencies attacked the crisis — the CCC gave jobs to young men planting trees, the WPA built roads, schools and airports, the AAA paid farmers to cut production and raise prices (and was declared unconstitutional in 1936), the TVA brought dams and cheap electricity to the Tennessee Valley; the Social Security Act (1935) created pensions and unemployment insurance, and the Wagner Act (1935) protected trade unions; the Supreme Court fought back until the court-packing clash of 1937; unemployment fell to about 8 million by 1937, though the recession of 1937-38 showed recovery was incomplete — only the defence spending of the Second World War ended the Depression, and critics attacked the New Deal from the left (too little) and the right (too much state power).

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Assembly lineA moving production line, introduced by Henry Ford in 1913, which cut costs by dividing work into simple repeated tasks.
Hire purchaseA system of buying goods by paying in instalments while using them, which spread consumer demand in the 1920s.
ProhibitionThe constitutional ban (1920-1933) on making and selling alcoholic drinks in the USA, widely ignored and fuelling organised crime.
Buying on the marginPurchasing shares with a small deposit and borrowing the rest, which inflated the stock-market bubble of 1929.
Wall Street CrashThe collapse of US share prices in October 1929 that triggered the Great Depression.
Dust BowlThe drought-stricken Great Plains region of the 1930s whose failed farms produced dust storms and forced mass migration.
New DealPresident Franklin Roosevelt's programme of relief, recovery and reform launched in 1933 to fight the Great Depression.
Alphabet agenciesNew Deal bodies known by their initials, such as the CCC, WPA, AAA and TVA, which created jobs and regulated the economy.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

New Deal evaluation questions are best answered as three waves — relief (jobs and food), recovery (banks, farm prices, industry) and reform (Social Security, union rights) — then judge each against evidence such as the 8 million still unemployed in 1937.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Describe four causes of the economic boom of the 1920s in the USA.

Model answerMass production: Ford's assembly line made cars cheap, e.g. the Model T falling below $300, creating a huge new market (1). New industries and electrification: cars, radios, cinema and household appliances created demand for steel, rubber and oil (1). Advertising and hire purchase persuaded consumers to buy on credit, spreading demand (1). The First World War left the USA the world's leading creditor with intact factories, while Republican governments kept taxes and regulation low (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate the success of the New Deal in solving the problems of the Depression.

Model answerRelief worked quickly: the bank holiday restored confidence, and agencies such as the CCC and WPA gave jobs and income to millions (1). Recovery was real but partial: farm prices rose under the AAA and industry revived, yet about 8 million were still unemployed in 1937 and the recession of 1937-38 set recovery back (1). Reform was lasting: Social Security, union rights and deposit insurance still shape the USA today (1). Opposition limited it: the Supreme Court struck down the NRA and AAA, and conservatives feared state power while radicals demanded more, showing it pleased neither side fully (1). Overall the New Deal saved capitalism and eased the worst suffering, but full recovery came only with the defence spending of the Second World War (1).
