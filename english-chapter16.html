Directed Writing: Register, Form and Purpose

Directed writing is judged on both content and style. This chapter shows how to identify audience and purpose fast, choose the right form, and control register from formal to conversational.

Key Concepts

01

Register is the level of formality set by audience and purpose; it is controlled by vocabulary (purchase vs buy), contractions, sentence complexity and the use of first or third person.

02

Formal writing for unknown or senior audiences avoids slang, contractions and abbreviations, and uses complete, carefully structured sentences; informal writing to friends may use colloquialisms and a chatty tone.

03

Text type determines structure: a letter needs addresses, date and sign-off; a report uses headings and often passive constructions; an article needs a headline, byline and engaging opening; a speech addresses the audience directly and signals its ending.

04

Purpose drives selection: to persuade you choose emotive examples and rhetorical questions, to inform you prioritise clear facts, to advise you lay out options with a clear recommendation.

05

Every directed writing task implies bullet-point content in the question: each point must be covered, developed with your own detail, and reordered logically rather than answered one by one.

06

The opening sentence should perform a function — stating the purpose, engaging the reader, or setting context — and the ending should match: a call to action, a summary, or a polite close.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
RegisterThe level of formality of language, chosen to suit the audience, purpose and context of a piece of writing.
Formal languageStandard, impersonal wording without slang or contractions, used for professional, official or unfamiliar audiences.
ColloquialismAn informal word or phrase used in everyday speech, appropriate only in casual register.
ConventionAn expected feature of a text type, such as a headline in an article or a subject line in a formal letter.
AudienceThe intended readers or listeners of a text, whose knowledge, age and relationship to the writer shape the writing.
PurposeWhat a text is designed to achieve, such as to inform, persuade, advise, entertain or complain.
Rhetorical questionA question asked for effect that does not expect an answer, used to engage the reader and advance an argument.
Call to actionA closing statement that urges the reader or audience to do something specific, common in persuasive and speech writing.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Spend the first minute annotating the task: underline the audience, the purpose verb (persuade, advise, report) and every bullet point, then tick each bullet as you cover it — coverage of all bullet points is the single easiest content mark to secure or lose.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

List four features that make a piece of writing formal rather than informal.

Model answerAny four of: no contractions (1); standard vocabulary without slang or colloquialisms (1); complete, complex sentences (1); impersonal constructions avoiding excessive first person (1); full forms instead of abbreviations (1); polite, measured tone (1).
CHALLENGE

You must write a report to your school principal about a poorly attended charity event, and a text message to a friend about the same event. Explain how your language and structure would differ between the two.

Model answerRegister: the report uses formal, impersonal language and no contractions to suit a senior audience, while the text uses informal, colloquial wording and abbreviations for a friend (1). Structure: the report is organised under headings such as Attendance, Causes and Recommendations, often in passive constructions, whereas the text is short, chatty and chronological (1). Both still convey the key facts, but audience and purpose reshape every choice.
