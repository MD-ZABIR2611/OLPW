SECTION 01 // FOUNDATIONS

THEORY

DEFINITION 01

THE ATOM

An atom is the smallest particle of an element that can take part in a chemical reaction. Every atom contains a central nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by electrons arranged in shells. Atoms are electrically neutral — protons = electrons.

DEFINITION 02

THE ELEMENT

An element is a substance that cannot be split into anything simpler by chemical means. All atoms in an element have the same atomic number (same number of protons). 118 elements are arranged in the Periodic Table.

DEFINITION 03

THE COMPOUND

A compound is a substance containing two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio. The properties of a compound differ completely from its constituent elements. It can only be separated by chemical reactions — never physically.

DEFINITION 04

THE MIXTURE

A mixture contains two or more substances not chemically joined. The components keep their own properties and can be mixed in any ratio. Mixtures are separated by physical methods: filtration, distillation, chromatography, magnetism.

EXAM INTEL

Common trap: "air is a compound." WRONG. Air is a mixture — its composition varies and it separates by fractional distillation without any chemical change. Compounds have a fixed formula; mixtures do not.

SECTION 02 // DEFINITIONS DATABASE

KEY TERMS

TERM / T-01
Molecule

DEFINITION // A group of two or more atoms (same or different elements) held together by covalent bonds. e.g. O2, H2O, CO2.

TERM / T-02
Ion

DEFINITION // A charged particle formed when an atom gains or loses electrons.

TERM / T-03
Cation

DEFINITION // A positively charged ion, formed when a metal atom loses electrons. e.g. Na+, Mg2+.

TERM / T-04
Anion

DEFINITION // A negatively charged ion, formed when a non-metal atom gains electrons. e.g. Cl−, O2−.

TERM / T-05
Isotope

DEFINITION // Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.

TERM / T-06
Valence Electrons

DEFINITION // The electrons in the outermost shell. They determine an element's chemical properties and its group number.

TERM / T-07
Relative Atomic Mass, Ar

DEFINITION // The weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared to 1/12 the mass of one atom of carbon-12.

TERM / T-08
Lattice

DEFINITION // A regular, repeating three-dimensional arrangement of particles — ions in ionic solids, cations in metals.

EXAM INTEL

Definition marks are free marks — but only if word-perfect. "Different number of neutrons" scores nothing without "same number of protons." Always write both halves of the isotope definition.

EXAMPLE 01PARTICLE COUNT

QUESTION // Aluminium has atomic number 13 and mass number 27. State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons.

METHOD //

p = Z = 13
n = A − Z = 27 − 13 = 14
e = Z = 13 (atom is neutral)

ANSWER // 13 p, 14 n, 13 e. CHECK: 13 + 14 = 27 ✓

EXAM INTEL

Notation 2311Na: top number = mass number A, bottom = atomic number Z. In ions, electrons ≠ protons: subtract the charge. Al3+ has 13 − 3 = 10 electrons.

SECTION 04 // SAME ELEMENT, DIFFERENT MASS

ISOTOPES

DEFINITION

ISOTOPES

Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same protons, same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different mass numbers A). Written 35Cl and 37Cl.

  • Chemical properties — identical. Same electron configuration → same valence electrons → same reactions.
  • Physical properties — slightly different. Density, melting point, rate of diffusion differ with mass.
  • Some are radioactive. e.g. 14C used in radiocarbon dating; 60Co in radiotherapy.
FORMULA 02 // RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS

Ar = Σ (isotopic mass × % abundance) / 100

RELATIVE ISOTOPIC MASS
mass of one isotope compared to 1/12 of one atom of 12C.
Ar
the weighted mean mass of all isotopes, taking abundance into account.
EXAMPLE 02CHLORINE

QUESTION // Chlorine is 75% 35Cl and 25% 37Cl. Calculate Ar.

Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) / 100
   = (2625 + 925) / 100
   = 3550 / 100 = 35.5

EXPLANATION // Ar is always closer to the mass of the most abundant isotope. Chlorine's answer of 35.5 (not a whole number) is direct evidence that it is a mixture of isotopes.

IONS FORMED //

  • Positive ions (cations) — metals lose valence electrons: Na (2,8,1) → Na+ (2,8) + e−
  • Negative ions (anions) — non-metals gain electrons: Cl (2,8,7) + e− → Cl− (2,8,8)
  • Noble-gas configuration — the product is a full outer shell, identical to Ne or Ar. This stable octet is why ions form.

SECTION 09 // ELECTRON SHARING

COVALENT BONDING

A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons between two non-metal atoms. The electrostatic attraction between the shared pair and both nuclei holds the atoms together. One shared pair = single bond; two pairs = double bond; three pairs = triple bond (e.g. N≡N).

Dot-and-cross rules: draw outer shells only. H needs 2 electrons (duplet); C shares 4 pairs; O has 2 bonds + 2 lone pairs; halogens have 1 bond + 3 lone pairs. Dots vs crosses must be distinguishable — that is what carries the marks.

MODULE A // SIMPLE MOLECULAR

MOLECULAR SUBSTANCES

Strong covalent bonds within each molecule — but weak intermolecular forces between molecules.

  • Low m.p. / b.p. — only the weak intermolecular forces are overcome on melting/boiling; covalent bonds do not break.
  • Do not conduct — no mobile ions or electrons, in any state.
  • Usually soluble in organic solvents, insoluble in water (most).
  • Examples: H2O, CO2, NH3, CH4, I2.
MODULE B // GIANT COVALENT

DIAMOND · GRAPHITE · GRAPHENE

Huge networks of atoms joined by strong covalent bonds in every direction → very high m.p., extremely hard (except graphite's layers).

  • Diamond: each C bonded to 4 others, tetrahedral. Hardest natural substance; does not conduct — all 4 outer electrons are bonded, none free.
  • Graphite: each C bonded to 3 others in hexagonal layers → one delocalised electron per atom → conducts. Weak forces between layers → soft, slippery, pencil "lead", lubricant.
  • Graphene: a single graphite layer, one atom thick. Strongest known material; conducts electricity and heat.

SECTION 10 // CATIONS + ELECTRON SEA

METALLIC BONDING

DIAGRAM 09METALLIC LATTICE
FIXED CATIONS · FREE ELECTRONSFIG. 10-A
PROPERTIES — EXPLAINEDSTRUCTURE → PROPERTY
  • Structure: a giant lattice of positive cations surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons (outer electrons released from all atoms). Bonding = attraction between cations and the electron sea.
  • Conduct electricity + heat — delocalised electrons are mobile and carry charge/energy through the lattice.
  • Malleable + ductile — layers of cations slide over each other without breaking the attraction: the electron sea re-adjusts. (Ionic crystals shatter — metals bend.)
  • High m.p. (usually) — strong attraction between cations and electrons needs large energy to overcome.

SECTION 12 // LABORATORY PROCEDURE SHEET

PRACTICAL

EXPERIMENT 01

CONDUCTIVITY CLASSIFICATION

DURATION ≈ 30 MIN // LOW VOLTAGE
AIM

To classify substances as metallic, ionic or simple molecular by testing electrical conductivity in the solid and dissolved states — and to link results to structure and bonding.

APPARATUS

6 V power supply or battery · small lamp (or ammeter) · two carbon (graphite) electrodes · connecting wires · 100 cm³ beaker · washing water · samples: A copper strip, B solid NaCl, C NaCl solution, D sucrose solid, E sucrose solution, F graphite rod.

METHOD
  1. Set up the circuit: power supply → lamp → two electrodes with a gap between them.
  2. Close the circuit across the dry sample (solid) and record whether the lamp lights.
  3. Rinse the electrodes with distilled water and dry them.
  4. Repeat with each sample dissolved/suspended in distilled water.
  5. Test the distilled water alone as a control — lamp must stay off.
  6. Record all results and classify each substance.
OBSERVATIONS
SAMPLESOLIDAQ./MOLTENCLASS
CuLIT—Metallic
GraphiteLIT—Giant covalent
NaClOFFLITIonic
SucroseOFFOFFMolecular
RESULT

Conduction requires mobile charged particles: electrons (metals, graphite) or free-moving ions (NaCl when molten/dissolved). Solids with fixed ions or neutral molecules do not conduct.

SAFETY
  • Use low-voltage DC only — never mains.
  • Wear safety glasses; NaCl solution near electrodes may release small amounts of chlorine — ventilate.
  • Rinse electrodes between samples to avoid cross-contamination.
SOURCES OF ERROR
  • Electrode gap varies → lamp brightness is subjective.
  • Poor contact with dry solids gives false negatives.
  • Distilled water not perfectly pure — weak conduction possible.
IMPROVEMENTS
  • Replace the lamp with an ammeter for quantitative current readings.
  • Fix electrode separation with a clamp and ruler.
  • Repeat each test 3× and average.

"Solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity, but molten sodium chloride does. Explain." [3] — answer in the Exam Zone, Q04.

EXAM QUESTIONS

Q01MARKS: 03DIFFICULTY: BASIC

State the relative electrical charge and relative mass of a proton, a neutron and an electron. [3]

ANSWER

proton: charge +1, mass 1
neutron: charge 0, mass 1
electron: charge −1, mass 1/1840 (negligible)

MARK SCHEME
  • all three charges correct
  • proton + neutron mass = 1
  • electron mass negligible / 1/1840
Q02MARKS: 03DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

Chlorine exists as two isotopes: 35Cl (75%) and 37Cl (25%). (a) Define relative atomic mass. (b) Calculate the relative atomic mass of chlorine. [3]

ANSWER (A)

The weighted mean mass of an atom of the element compared to 1/12 the mass of one atom of carbon-12.

ANSWER (B) — WORKING

Ar = (35 × 75 + 37 × 25) / 100 = (2625 + 925) / 100 = 35.5

MARK SCHEME
  • definition: weighted mean / compared to 1/12 of C-12
  • correct substitution of both isotopes + abundances
  • answer 35.5
Q03MARKS: 04DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

Magnesium reacts with oxygen to form magnesium oxide. Describe the formation of the ions, state their electronic configurations, and write the balanced equation. [4]

ANSWER

Each Mg atom (2,8,2) loses 2 electrons → Mg2+ (2,8). Each O atom (2,6) gains 2 electrons → O2− (2,8). The oppositely charged ions are held by strong electrostatic attraction.

2Mg + O2 → 2MgO

MARK SCHEME
  • Mg loses 2 electrons → Mg2+
  • O gains 2 electrons → O2−
  • both configurations 2,8 (noble gas)
  • balanced equation 2Mg + O2 → 2MgO
Q04MARKS: 06DIFFICULTY: ADVANCED

Sodium chloride has a melting point of 801 °C. It does not conduct electricity as a solid, but conducts when molten or dissolved in water. Explain all three observations using structure and bonding. [6]

ANSWER

High m.p.: NaCl is a giant ionic lattice; every ion is attracted to several oppositely charged ions by strong electrostatic forces, so a very large amount of energy is needed to separate the ions.

Solid: the ions are fixed in position in the lattice — no mobile charged particles, so no conduction.

Molten/dissolved: the lattice breaks down and the ions become free to move; moving ions carry charge to the electrodes.

MARK SCHEME
  • giant lattice / strong electrostatic attraction between opposite ions
  • large energy needed to overcome these forces
  • solid: ions fixed / cannot move
  • no mobile charge carriers in the solid
  • molten/dissolved: ions free to move
  • mobile ions carry charge
Q05MARKS: 04DIFFICULTY: ADVANCED

Diamond and graphite are both giant covalent structures of carbon. Explain why graphite conducts electricity but diamond does not. [4]

ANSWER

Graphite: each carbon is bonded to only 3 other carbons, so one outer electron per atom is delocalised. These electrons are free to move through the structure and carry charge.

Diamond: each carbon is bonded to 4 others — all four outer electrons are used in covalent bonds. There are no free electrons or charged particles, so it cannot conduct.

MARK SCHEME
  • graphite: 3 bonds per carbon → delocalised electron
  • electrons mobile / free to move → carry charge
  • diamond: 4 bonds per carbon → no free electrons
  • no mobile charged particles → no conduction
Q06MARKS: 05DIFFICULTY: ADVANCED

Copper is used for electrical wiring. Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why copper conducts electricity and why it can be drawn into wires (ductile). [5]

ANSWER

Copper is a giant metallic lattice: positive Cu cations arranged regularly, surrounded by a sea of delocalised electrons.

Conduction: the delocalised electrons are free to move through the lattice and carry charge when a voltage is applied.

Ductility: the layers of cations can slide over one another when a force is applied; the attraction to the electron sea is non-directional, so the metallic bonding is not broken — the metal deforms instead of shattering.

MARK SCHEME
  • lattice of positive cations
  • surrounded by delocalised / free electrons
  • electrons mobile → carry charge
  • layers of cations can slide
  • bonding not broken when layers move → deforms, doesn't shatter
Q07MARKS: 03DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

Water boils at 100 °C even though the covalent bonds within its molecules are very strong. Explain this apparent contradiction. [3]

ANSWER

Water is a simple molecular substance. When it boils, only the weak intermolecular forces between molecules are overcome — the strong covalent O–H bonds within each molecule do not break.

MARK SCHEME
  • weak intermolecular forces between molecules
  • these are overcome on boiling (low energy needed)
  • covalent bonds within molecules remain intact
