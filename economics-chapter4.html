Price Determination and Elasticity

How demand and supply together set the equilibrium price, how that price changes when conditions change, and how price and supply elasticity measure the responsiveness of buyers and sellers.

01

Overview

Knowing the causes of shifts in demand and supply is only half the story; the real power of the model comes from putting the two curves together. This chapter shows how markets reach equilibrium, what happens when they are out of equilibrium, and how prices respond to changing conditions. It then introduces price elasticity of demand (PED) and price elasticity of supply (PES), which measure how strongly quantities react to price changes. Elasticity questions often involve calculations and link directly to business revenue decisions and government tax policy, so accuracy and clear explanation are essential.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain market equilibrium and identify the equilibrium price and quantity on a diagram.
  • Explain how excess demand and excess supply push a market back towards equilibrium.
  • Analyse the effect of shifts in demand and supply on equilibrium price and quantity.
  • Define, calculate and interpret price elasticity of demand, and explain its determinants.
  • Explain the relationship between PED and total revenue, and its significance for firms and governments.
  • Define, calculate and interpret price elasticity of supply, and explain its determinants.
02

Market Equilibrium

Market equilibrium occurs where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. On a diagram it is the point where the demand and supply curves intersect. The price at this point is the equilibrium price, sometimes called the market-clearing price, and the quantity is the equilibrium quantity. At equilibrium there is no tendency for price to change, because every buyer willing to pay that price can find a seller, and every seller willing to accept it can find a buyer.

If the price is above equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating excess supply, also called a surplus. Unsold stock builds up, so firms cut prices to clear it. As price falls, quantity demanded extends and quantity supplied contracts until equilibrium is restored. If the price is below equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating excess demand, or a shortage. Consumers compete for limited goods and firms realise they can charge more, so price rises until the shortage disappears.

Key fact:Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied. Excess supply pushes price down; excess demand pushes price up.
03

Changes in Equilibrium

When demand or supply shifts, the market moves to a new equilibrium. An increase in demand shifts the demand curve right; at the old price there is now excess demand, so price rises and quantity supplied extends until a new, higher equilibrium price and larger quantity are reached. A decrease in demand has the opposite effect, lowering both price and quantity.

An increase in supply shifts the supply curve right, creating excess supply at the old price; price falls and quantity demanded extends, giving a lower price and higher quantity. A decrease in supply raises price and lowers quantity. When both curves shift at once, the effect on one of the variables is certain and the other depends on the relative size of the shifts. For example, if demand and supply both increase, quantity definitely rises but price may rise, fall or stay the same.

  • Demand increases: price rises, quantity rises.
  • Demand decreases: price falls, quantity falls.
  • Supply increases: price falls, quantity rises.
  • Supply decreases: price rises, quantity falls.
04

Price Elasticity of Demand

Price elasticity of demand (PED) measures the responsiveness of quantity demanded to a change in price. It is calculated as the percentage change in quantity demanded divided by the percentage change in price. Because price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, PED is negative, but economists often discuss its size, ignoring the minus sign.

If PED is greater than 1 (ignoring the sign), demand is price elastic: quantity demanded changes by a larger percentage than price. If PED is less than 1, demand is price inelastic: quantity changes by a smaller percentage than price. If PED equals exactly 1, demand is unit elastic. Two extreme cases exist: perfectly inelastic demand (PED = 0), shown by a vertical demand curve, where quantity does not change at all; and perfectly elastic demand (PED = infinity), shown by a horizontal demand curve, where any price rise causes quantity demanded to fall to zero.

To calculate a percentage change, divide the change by the original value and multiply by 100. For example, if price rises from 4 dollars to 5 dollars, the percentage change is 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = 25 percent. If quantity demanded then falls from 200 to 150 units, the percentage change is −50 ÷ 200 × 100 = −25 percent, so PED = −25 ÷ 25 = −1, which is unit elastic.

Key fact:PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price.
05

Determinants of PED

The most important determinant is the number and closeness of substitutes. If a product has many close substitutes, consumers can easily switch when its price rises, so demand is elastic. A single brand of cola has elastic demand, whereas salt or petrol as a whole has few substitutes and inelastic demand. Linked to this is how widely the product is defined: demand for one brand of petrol is far more elastic than demand for petrol in general.

The proportion of income spent on the product also matters. Items that take a small share of income, such as matches or toothpaste, tend to have inelastic demand because a price rise hardly affects the budget. Expensive items such as cars or holidays tend to be more elastic. Whether the product is a necessity or a luxury, whether it is habit-forming, such as cigarettes, and the time period all play a role. Demand tends to be more elastic in the long run because consumers have more time to find alternatives, for example by buying a more fuel-efficient car after a rise in petrol prices.

  • Availability and closeness of substitutes
  • Proportion of income spent on the product
  • Necessity or luxury
  • Habit-forming or addictive nature
  • Time period considered
  • Breadth of definition of the product
06

PED and Total Revenue

Total revenue is price multiplied by quantity sold, and it equals total consumer spending on the product. PED tells a firm how its revenue will respond to a price change. When demand is price elastic, a price cut raises total revenue, because the percentage increase in quantity sold outweighs the percentage fall in price; a price rise reduces revenue. When demand is price inelastic, a price rise raises total revenue and a price cut reduces it. When demand is unit elastic, total revenue stays the same.

This has practical significance. A firm selling a product with inelastic demand, such as a patented medicine, can raise prices to increase revenue. Firms facing elastic demand, such as airlines competing on popular routes, may cut prices to win customers. Governments use PED when choosing which goods to tax: taxing products with inelastic demand, such as tobacco, fuel and alcohol, raises a lot of revenue because consumption falls only a little. However, if the aim is to reduce consumption of a harmful good, inelastic demand means a tax will be less effective at changing behaviour.

Key fact:Elastic: price and revenue move in opposite directions. Inelastic: price and revenue move in the same direction.
07

Price Elasticity of Supply

Price elasticity of supply (PES) measures the responsiveness of quantity supplied to a change in price. It is calculated as the percentage change in quantity supplied divided by the percentage change in price. PES is positive because price and quantity supplied move in the same direction. If PES is greater than 1, supply is elastic; if less than 1, supply is inelastic; if 0, supply is perfectly inelastic, shown by a vertical supply curve; and if infinite, supply is perfectly elastic, shown by a horizontal curve.

Supply is more elastic when firms have spare capacity, when they hold large stocks of finished goods or raw materials, when factors of production are mobile, and when production is quick. Supply is inelastic when production takes a long time, as with crops that must grow or buildings that must be constructed. Time is crucial: in the very short run, supply of fresh fish on a given day is almost fixed, but over months fishermen can buy more boats. Manufactured goods such as T-shirts usually have more elastic supply than agricultural products or specialist skilled services.

  • Spare capacity
  • Level of stocks (inventories)
  • Mobility of factors of production
  • Length of the production period
  • Time period considered
Key fact:PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price.
08

Why Elasticity Matters to Decision Makers

Elasticity helps many economic agents predict the effects of their decisions. Firms use PED to set prices and PES to plan how quickly they can respond to rising demand. Farmers with inelastic supply often see large price swings, because when demand rises they cannot quickly increase output, so prices rise sharply instead. This explains why agricultural prices are more volatile than prices of manufactured goods.

Governments use elasticity when predicting the effect of taxes and subsidies. A tax on a good with inelastic demand passes most of its burden to consumers through higher prices, while a tax on a good with elastic demand falls more heavily on producers. Workers and trade unions consider elasticity too: if demand for labour is inelastic, a wage rise will cause relatively few job losses. In every case, elasticity turns the direction of change predicted by demand and supply into an estimate of how large that change will be.

10

Worked Examples

Calculating PED and the effect on revenue

A café raises the price of a sandwich from 5 dollars to 6 dollars. Weekly sales fall from 400 to 360. Calculate PED, state whether demand is elastic or inelastic, and calculate the change in total revenue.

  1. Percentage change in price = (6 − 5) ÷ 5 × 100 = 20 percent.
  2. Percentage change in quantity demanded = (360 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = −10 percent.
  3. PED = −10 ÷ 20 = −0.5, so demand is price inelastic.
  4. Original revenue = 5 × 400 = 2,000 dollars. New revenue = 6 × 360 = 2,160 dollars.
  5. Revenue rises by 160 dollars, consistent with the rule that a price rise increases revenue when demand is inelastic.
PED = −0.5 (inelastic). Total revenue rises from 2,000 to 2,160 dollars, an increase of 160 dollars.

Calculating PES

The price of apples rises from 2.00 dollars to 2.50 dollars per kilogram, and the quantity supplied rises from 1,000 kg to 1,100 kg per week. Calculate PES and explain one reason for the result.

  1. Percentage change in price = 0.50 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = 25 percent.
  2. Percentage change in quantity supplied = 100 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 10 percent.
  3. PES = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4, which is less than 1, so supply is price inelastic.
  4. Reason: apple trees take years to grow and fruit must ripen, so growers cannot quickly increase output when prices rise.
PES = 0.4, meaning supply is inelastic, mainly because the production period for apples is long.

Model answer: explaining a price change

Using a demand and supply diagram, explain why the price of hotel rooms in a seaside town rises in summer. (4 marks)

  1. Draw demand D1 and supply S intersecting at equilibrium price P1 and quantity Q1.
  2. In summer, more tourists want to visit the seaside, so demand for hotel rooms increases and shifts right from D1 to D2 (1).
  3. At the original price P1 there is now excess demand (1), so hotels raise their prices.
  4. The new equilibrium is at a higher price P2 (1) and larger quantity Q2 as quantity supplied extends (1). Because the number of rooms is limited, supply is inelastic, so the price rise is large.
Seasonal demand shifts the demand curve right, creating excess demand at the old price, so price rises from P1 to P2 and quantity extends from Q1 to Q2; inelastic supply makes the price rise especially large.
11

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Calculating PED as percentage change in price over percentage change in quantity. Quantity always goes on top.
  • Using the new value rather than the original value when calculating percentage changes. Always divide by the original figure.
  • Saying elastic demand means quantity changes a lot. It means the percentage change in quantity is greater than the percentage change in price.
  • Believing a price rise always increases revenue. This is only true when demand is price inelastic.
  • Confusing a steep curve with perfectly inelastic demand. Only a vertical curve has zero elasticity.
  • Forgetting to explain the process of adjustment. Mention excess demand or excess supply at the old price before stating the new equilibrium.

Exam tips

  • Show every step of elasticity calculations, including the formula and percentage changes, as method marks are available.
  • Always interpret your elasticity answer: state whether it is elastic or inelastic and what that means.
  • When explaining a change in equilibrium, use the chain: shift in curve, excess demand or supply at old price, price changes, movement along the other curve, new equilibrium.
  • Link PED to real decisions: pricing by firms and the choice of goods to tax by governments.
  • Remember that PED is negative and PES is positive; include the sign when asked to calculate.
  • In evaluation questions, note that elasticity changes over time, usually becoming more elastic in the long run.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. Define equilibrium price.

Model answerThe equilibrium price is the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied (1), so the market clears with no shortage or surplus (1).
[2 marks]

2. State the formula for price elasticity of supply.

Model answerPES = percentage change in quantity supplied (1) divided by percentage change in price (1).
[3 marks]

3. Explain what happens in a market when the price is above the equilibrium price.

Model answerQuantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded, creating excess supply (1). Firms are left with unsold stock (1), so they reduce price, causing quantity demanded to extend and quantity supplied to contract until equilibrium is reached (1).
[3 marks]

4. The price of a bus ticket falls from 2 dollars to 1.60 dollars and the number of journeys rises from 5,000 to 5,500. Calculate PED.

Model answerPercentage change in price = −0.40 ÷ 2 × 100 = −20 percent (1). Percentage change in quantity = 500 ÷ 5,000 × 100 = 10 percent (1). PED = 10 ÷ −20 = −0.5 (1).
[3 marks]

5. Using your answer to the previous question, explain what happens to the bus company's total revenue.

Model answerDemand is price inelastic (1), so a price cut reduces total revenue (1). Revenue falls from 2 × 5,000 = 10,000 dollars to 1.60 × 5,500 = 8,800 dollars (1).
[4 marks]

6. Explain two determinants of price elasticity of demand.

Model answerAvailability of substitutes: if there are many close substitutes, consumers switch easily when price rises, so demand is elastic (2). Proportion of income: if the product takes a large share of income, a price change has a big effect on the budget, so demand is more elastic (2).
[3 marks]

7. Explain why the supply of agricultural products tends to be price inelastic.

Model answerCrops take a long time to grow, so farmers cannot quickly increase output when prices rise (1). Production also depends on weather and land availability, which farmers cannot easily change (1). Many agricultural products are perishable, so they cannot be stored as stocks to release when prices rise (1).
[4 marks]

8. Using a diagram, explain the effect of a rise in the cost of cocoa on the chocolate market.

Model answerCocoa is a raw material, so costs of production rise (1). Supply decreases and the supply curve shifts left from S1 to S2 (1). At the original price there is excess demand (1), so price rises from P1 to P2 and quantity falls from Q1 to Q2 (1).
[4 marks]

9. Explain why governments often impose taxes on products with price inelastic demand.

Model answerWhen demand is price inelastic, a tax raises price but quantity demanded falls by a smaller percentage (1). Consumers continue buying, so the tax revenue collected is large and reliable (1). Products such as tobacco and fuel are therefore good sources of revenue (1), though the tax will not greatly reduce consumption (1).
[4 marks]

10. Explain two factors that would make the supply of a manufactured good price elastic.

Model answerSpare capacity: if factories are not working at full output, firms can quickly produce more when price rises (2). Stocks: if firms hold large inventories of finished goods, they can release them onto the market immediately (2).
[8 marks]

11. Discuss whether knowledge of price elasticity of demand is useful to a firm deciding whether to raise its price.

Model answerUseful: if demand is inelastic, a price rise will increase total revenue because quantity falls by a smaller percentage (2); if demand is elastic, a price rise will reduce revenue, so the firm should avoid it or consider a price cut (2). Limitations: PED estimates are based on past data and may be inaccurate (1); PED changes over time as consumers find substitutes (1); competitors may react, for example by cutting their own prices (1). Conclusion: PED is a valuable guide to pricing, but the firm should combine it with information about costs, competitors and market conditions (1).

Quick Recap

  1. Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, at the intersection of the curves.
  2. Excess supply pushes price down and excess demand pushes price up until equilibrium is restored.
  3. Shifts in demand or supply create a new equilibrium price and quantity.
  4. PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price; it is elastic if greater than 1 and inelastic if less than 1.
  5. PED depends on substitutes, share of income, necessity, habit and time.
  6. With elastic demand, price and revenue move in opposite directions; with inelastic demand, they move together.
  7. PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price and depends on spare capacity, stocks, factor mobility and time.
  8. Firms, governments and workers use elasticity to predict the size of changes caused by price movements.
