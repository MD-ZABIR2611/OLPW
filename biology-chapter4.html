Enzymes

CORE
SECTION 01 — CHAPTER OVERVIEW

Biological Catalysts

Enzymes are specialized proteins that act as biological catalysts, speeding up chemical reactions in living organisms without being changed themselves. This chapter explains how they work and how environmental factors affect their activity.

SECTION 02 — LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Chapter Goals

  • Define enzyme, active site, substrate, and denaturation.
  • Explain the lock-and-key mechanism.
  • Interpret graphs showing the effect of temperature and pH on enzyme activity.
  • Explain why enzymes denature.
SECTION 03 — CORE THEORY

Enzyme Action

Enzymes are globular proteins. Their shape includes a specifically shaped region called the active site. The molecule that the enzyme acts upon is the substrate. Because the active site's shape matches exactly one specific substrate, enzymes are highly specific—a concept known as the Lock and Key model.

Factors Affecting Enzymes

Temperature: As temperature increases, kinetic energy increases, so substrates collide with the active site more frequently. However, above the optimum temperature, the chemical bonds in the enzyme vibrate so violently that they break. The active site changes shape permanently. The enzyme is denatured and can no longer bind to its substrate.

pH: Each enzyme has an optimum pH. Extreme acidity or alkalinity alters the charges on the amino acids, changing the active site shape and denaturing the enzyme.

SECTION 04 — KEY DEFINITIONS

Examination Definitions

ENZYME: A biological catalyst, usually a protein, that speeds up a chemical reaction without being changed.

ACTIVE SITE: The specifically shaped region of an enzyme where the substrate binds.

DENATURATION: The permanent change in the shape of an enzyme's active site, caused by high temperatures or extreme pH, preventing the substrate from binding.

SECTION 06 — PRACTICAL LAB

Effect of Temperature on Amylase

VARIABLES

Independent: Temperature (water baths at 20°C, 40°C, 60°C).

Dependent: Time taken for iodine to stop turning blue/black (starch digestion).

Control: Volume of amylase, volume of starch, pH (buffer).

CONCLUSION

As temperature increases, the rate of reaction increases until the optimum. After the optimum, the rate drops sharply because the amylase denatures.

SECTION 07 — EXAM TECHNIQUE

Describing Enzyme Graphs

When describing denaturation on a graph, never say the enzyme "dies" or "is killed". Say the active site changes shape, meaning the substrate no longer fits.

SECTION 08 — COMMON MISTAKES

Avoid These Errors

Mistake: Writing "enzymes are killed".

Correction: Enzymes are not alive. They are denatured.

Mistake: Using the words "optimum" and "maximum" interchangeably. Optimum is the ideal condition for enzyme activity.

SECTION 09 — QUICK REVISION

Fast Facts

  • Enzymes are biological catalysts (proteins).
  • They have a specific active site that matches a substrate (Lock and Key).
  • High temperatures and extreme pH cause denaturation (active site changes shape permanently).
  • Optimum temperature in humans is roughly 37°C.
SECTION 10 — EXAM QUESTIONS

Test Your Knowledge

Question 1 (Challenge)

A student heats an enzyme to 80°C and finds it no longer works. They cool it back to 37°C, but it still does not work. Explain why.

2 marks: At 80°C, the extreme heat broke the chemical bonds holding the enzyme's shape together. This permanently changed the shape of the active site. Cooling it down does not restore the original shape, so the substrate can no longer bind.
