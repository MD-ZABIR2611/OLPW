Climate Change and Sustainability

The causes and consequences of climate change, the meaning of sustainable development, and the evaluation of mitigation and adaptation strategies from personal, local, national and global perspectives.

01

Overview

Climate change is one of the defining global issues of the twenty-first century and a favourite theme for Global Perspectives sources. It affects every country, yet its causes and consequences are unequally distributed, making it ideal for exploring contrasting perspectives. Questions may ask you to explain the impacts of rising temperatures, evaluate an argument for a carbon tax, or judge whether individuals or governments bear most responsibility for action. This chapter gives you the key science, vocabulary and debates so you can analyse climate sources accurately and propose realistic, well-evaluated courses of action.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain the enhanced greenhouse effect and the main human causes of climate change.
  • Describe environmental, social and economic consequences of climate change at different scales.
  • Define sustainable development and explain its environmental, social and economic dimensions.
  • Distinguish between mitigation and adaptation and give examples of each.
  • Evaluate international agreements and national and individual courses of action.
  • Analyse different perspectives on climate responsibility and climate justice.
02

The Greenhouse Effect and Causes of Climate Change

The greenhouse effect is a natural process in which gases in the atmosphere, such as carbon dioxide, methane and water vapour, trap some of the heat radiated from the Earth's surface. Without it, the planet would be too cold for most life. Climate change refers to long-term shifts in temperature and weather patterns. Since the Industrial Revolution, human activities have increased concentrations of greenhouse gases, strengthening the effect and causing global warming. This is called the enhanced greenhouse effect.

The main human cause is the burning of fossil fuels, including coal, oil and natural gas, for electricity, transport, industry and heating, which releases carbon dioxide. Deforestation adds to the problem because trees absorb carbon dioxide, and cutting or burning them releases stored carbon. Agriculture releases methane from livestock and rice paddies and nitrous oxide from fertilisers. Industrial processes such as cement production also emit large amounts of carbon dioxide.

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), which assesses research from thousands of scientists, has concluded that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land. Global average temperatures are now roughly 1.1 to 1.3°C above pre-industrial levels, and 2024 was the first calendar year in which the average exceeded 1.5°C.

Key fact:Main greenhouse gases from human activity: carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄) and nitrous oxide (N₂O).
03

Consequences of Climate Change

Environmental consequences include rising sea levels caused by melting ice sheets and glaciers and the expansion of warming seawater, more frequent and intense heatwaves, changing rainfall patterns causing both droughts and floods, stronger tropical storms, ocean acidification that damages coral reefs, and loss of biodiversity as species struggle to adapt to changing habitats.

Social and economic consequences are severe. Crop failures threaten food security, especially in regions dependent on rain-fed farming. Water shortages increase in dry regions. Heatwaves and the spread of diseases such as malaria and dengue into new areas threaten health. Damage to homes and infrastructure costs billions, and some people are forced to move, becoming climate migrants. Low-lying island nations such as Tuvalu, Kiribati and the Maldives face threats to their very existence.

Consequences are unequal. Countries and people that have contributed least to emissions, such as low-income countries in Africa and small island states, are often the most vulnerable because they depend on agriculture, face greater exposure to hazards and have fewer resources to adapt. Within countries, poorer households, older people and outdoor workers are most at risk. This inequality is central to debates about climate justice.

  • Rising sea levels and coastal flooding.
  • More extreme weather: heatwaves, droughts, floods, storms.
  • Threats to food and water security.
  • Health risks from heat and spreading diseases.
  • Biodiversity loss and coral bleaching.
  • Displacement and climate migration.
04

Sustainable Development

The most widely used definition of sustainable development comes from the 1987 Brundtland Report: development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. It recognises that economic growth which destroys the environment or leaves people behind cannot continue indefinitely.

Sustainability has three interconnected dimensions, sometimes called the three pillars. Environmental sustainability means protecting natural resources and ecosystems. Social sustainability means meeting people's needs fairly, including health, education and human rights. Economic sustainability means supporting livelihoods and prosperity in ways that can be maintained over time. A truly sustainable course of action balances all three.

In 2015 the United Nations adopted 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) to be achieved by 2030. They include Goal 7 (affordable and clean energy), Goal 12 (responsible consumption and production) and Goal 13 (climate action). The SDGs show how climate change is linked to poverty, health, water and inequality, and they provide a useful framework for analysing any global issue.

Key fact:Brundtland definition: meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
05

Mitigation and Adaptation

Responses to climate change fall into two categories. Mitigation means reducing the causes of climate change, mainly by cutting greenhouse gas emissions or removing carbon from the atmosphere. Examples include switching to renewable energy such as solar, wind and hydroelectric power, improving energy efficiency, expanding public transport, electric vehicles, reforestation, reducing food waste and carbon pricing.

Adaptation means adjusting to the effects of climate change that are already happening or expected. Examples include building sea walls and flood defences, developing drought-resistant crops, improving water storage, designing buildings to stay cool, creating early warning systems for storms and relocating communities from high-risk areas. Bangladesh, for instance, has built cyclone shelters and early warning systems that have greatly reduced deaths from storms.

Both are necessary. Mitigation reduces the scale of future harm, while adaptation protects people from impacts that can no longer be avoided. However, adaptation can be expensive, and poorer countries often lack the funds. This is why international climate finance, in which wealthier countries help fund mitigation and adaptation in lower-income countries, is a major issue in negotiations.

  • Mitigation: renewable energy, efficiency, reforestation, carbon tax, public transport.
  • Adaptation: flood defences, drought-resistant crops, early warning systems, water storage.
06

International and National Action

Climate change is a global problem requiring international cooperation because greenhouse gases mix in the atmosphere regardless of where they are emitted. The United Nations holds annual Conferences of the Parties (COPs). The Kyoto Protocol of 1997 set binding emissions targets for developed countries. The Paris Agreement of 2015 committed almost every country to limit warming to well below 2°C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C, with each country setting its own targets, called Nationally Determined Contributions.

Supporters praise the Paris Agreement for including nearly all countries. Critics note that targets are not legally enforceable and that current national pledges are not enough to meet the 1.5°C goal. At COP27 in 2022, countries agreed to create a fund for loss and damage to help vulnerable countries cope with climate impacts, reflecting demands from developing nations for climate justice.

National governments use a range of policies: carbon taxes (Sweden has one of the highest), emissions trading schemes, subsidies for renewable energy, bans on new petrol and diesel cars, building regulations and protection of forests. Each policy has trade-offs. A carbon tax gives an incentive to cut emissions but can raise living costs for poorer households unless revenue is returned to them.

07

Perspectives and Responsibility

Perspectives on climate action differ sharply. Many developing countries argue that wealthy industrialised nations caused most historical emissions and should therefore make the biggest cuts and pay for adaptation elsewhere. This principle is known as common but differentiated responsibilities. Some developed countries point out that large emerging economies, such as China and India, are now among the biggest emitters and must also act strongly.

Fossil fuel industries and workers in coal or oil regions may fear job losses from rapid change, while renewable energy companies see opportunities. Young climate activists argue that older generations are leaving them with a damaged planet. Businesses may support action if it is gradual and predictable. Understanding these vested interests helps you evaluate sources on climate policy.

At a personal level, individuals can reduce their carbon footprint by using public transport, eating less meat, saving energy and reducing waste. Some argue individual action is essential and influences wider change; others argue that a small number of large companies and governments have far greater influence, so systemic change matters most. Strong answers usually conclude that both individual and collective action are needed.

Key fact:Climate justice: those least responsible for emissions often suffer most, so responsibility and costs should be shared fairly.

Worked Examples

Evaluating a proposal for a carbon tax

A government proposes a carbon tax on petrol, diesel and electricity generated from coal. Evaluate this course of action. [8 marks]

  1. Explain how it works: raising the price of high-carbon products gives consumers and businesses an incentive to switch to cleaner alternatives.
  2. Benefits: can reduce emissions efficiently; raises revenue that could fund renewable energy or public transport; examples such as Sweden show emissions can fall while the economy grows.
  3. Drawbacks: raises living costs, hitting poorer households hardest because they spend a larger share of income on fuel and energy (regressive effect).
  4. Drawbacks: may be politically unpopular, as shown by fuel-price protests in some countries; businesses may relocate to countries without the tax.
  5. Ways to improve: return revenue to households as rebates, introduce the tax gradually, invest in affordable alternatives.
  6. Judgement: effective if designed fairly.
A carbon tax can be an effective mitigation policy because it uses price incentives to cut emissions and raises funds for clean alternatives. However, it risks harming poorer households and provoking opposition. It is most likely to succeed if introduced gradually, with revenue returned to low-income families and investment in public transport and renewables.

Mitigation or adaptation?

Classify each action as mitigation or adaptation and explain one example of each: building a sea wall; installing solar panels; planting drought-resistant crops; banning new petrol cars.

  1. Sea wall: adaptation, because it protects against flooding caused by sea-level rise rather than reducing emissions.
  2. Solar panels: mitigation, because they generate electricity without burning fossil fuels.
  3. Drought-resistant crops: adaptation, because they help farmers cope with changing rainfall.
  4. Banning new petrol cars: mitigation, because it reduces future transport emissions.
Mitigation: solar panels and banning petrol cars. Adaptation: sea walls and drought-resistant crops. For example, solar panels reduce carbon dioxide emissions from electricity generation, while sea walls protect coastal homes from flooding that is already increasing.

Worked source evaluation

Source: A 2023 press release from a major oil company states: 'We are committed to net zero by 2050 and are investing in renewables. Oil and gas will remain essential to the world's energy needs for decades.' Evaluate the reliability of this source as evidence of the company's contribution to tackling climate change.

  1. Identify author and purpose: a press release from an oil company, designed to present the company positively.
  2. Vested interest: the company profits from oil and gas, so it has reasons to emphasise positive actions and argue fossil fuels remain necessary.
  3. Evidence: the release gives no figures on how much is invested in renewables compared with fossil fuels, so claims cannot be checked.
  4. Strength: it is a direct primary source of the company's stated position and is recent.
  5. Corroboration: compare with independent analysis of the company's spending and emissions.
The source is reliable as evidence of what the company claims and of the oil industry's perspective, but unreliable as evidence of its actual contribution because of its vested interest, promotional purpose and lack of verifiable figures. Independent data would be needed to judge its real impact.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the natural greenhouse effect with the enhanced greenhouse effect caused by humans.
  • Mixing up mitigation and adaptation; mitigation tackles causes, adaptation deals with effects.
  • Confusing climate change with the hole in the ozone layer, which is a separate issue.
  • Writing only about environmental impacts and ignoring social and economic consequences.
  • Assuming all countries share equal responsibility, without considering historical emissions and capacity to act.
  • Proposing solutions such as 'ban all fossil fuels now' without evaluating cost, feasibility and impacts on workers.

Exam tips

  • Use the terms mitigation and adaptation precisely and give one example of each in extended answers.
  • Consider environmental, social and economic dimensions of any climate policy to show sustainability thinking.
  • Refer to the Paris Agreement and the 1.5°C and 2°C goals when discussing international action.
  • Check sources on climate for vested interests, such as fossil fuel or renewable energy companies.
  • Discuss climate justice when comparing perspectives of developed and developing countries.
  • Balance individual and systemic responsibility in 'who should act' questions.

Practice Questions

1. Name two greenhouse gases released by human activity.

Model answerAny two: carbon dioxide (1); methane (1); nitrous oxide (1).
[2 marks]

2. State the Brundtland definition of sustainable development.

Model answerDevelopment that meets the needs of the present (1) without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (1).
[2 marks]

3. What is the difference between mitigation and adaptation?

Model answerMitigation reduces the causes of climate change, e.g. cutting emissions (1). Adaptation adjusts to its effects, e.g. flood defences (1).
[3 marks]

4. Identify three human activities that increase greenhouse gas emissions.

Model answerAny three: burning fossil fuels for energy or transport (1); deforestation (1); livestock farming (1); use of fertilisers (1); cement production (1).
[2 marks]

5. What were the main temperature goals of the Paris Agreement?

Model answerLimit global warming to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels (1) and pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C (1).
[3 marks]

6. Explain why deforestation contributes to climate change.

Model answerTrees absorb carbon dioxide through photosynthesis (1), so fewer trees means less CO₂ removed (1); burning or decomposing trees releases stored carbon into the atmosphere (1).
[4 marks]

7. Explain two consequences of climate change for food security.

Model answerDroughts and changing rainfall reduce crop yields (1), causing shortages and higher food prices (1). Flooding and storms destroy farmland and harvests (1), threatening farmers' livelihoods and food supply (1).
[4 marks]

8. Explain why low-income countries are often more vulnerable to climate change.

Model answerGreater dependence on agriculture, which is sensitive to weather (1); fewer resources to fund adaptation such as flood defences (1); weaker infrastructure and health systems (1); many are located in regions exposed to droughts, cyclones or sea-level rise (1).
[3 marks]

9. Explain what is meant by climate justice.

Model answerThe idea that climate action should be fair (1); those who contributed least to emissions often suffer most (1), so wealthier, high-emitting countries should take greater responsibility for cuts and funding (1).
[4 marks]

10. Explain one strength and one weakness of the Paris Agreement.

Model answerStrength: almost all countries signed (1), so it includes major emitters and developing nations (1). Weakness: national targets are not legally enforceable (1), and current pledges are insufficient to meet the 1.5°C goal (1).
[6 marks]

11. Evaluate how effective individual actions are in tackling climate change.

Model answerEffective: many individual changes add up, e.g. less meat, public transport (1); consumer choices pressure businesses (1); individuals influence governments by voting and campaigning (1). Limited: individual emissions are small compared with industry and energy systems (1); some people cannot afford alternatives (1); systemic change such as clean electricity is needed (1). Judgement: individual action matters most when combined with government and business action (1). Maximum 6.
[8 marks]

12. 'Rich countries should pay for poorer countries to adapt to climate change.' Discuss this claim, considering different perspectives.

Model answerFor: rich countries produced most historical emissions (1-2); poorer countries are most vulnerable and have least capacity (1); climate justice and the loss and damage fund agreed at COP27 support this (1-2). Against: taxpayers in rich countries may oppose paying (1); major emerging emitters such as China should also contribute (1-2); concerns about corruption and whether funds are used effectively (1). Perspectives of developing nations, donor governments, taxpayers, NGOs (1). Supported conclusion, e.g. rich countries should pay a larger share, with transparency and contributions from large current emitters (1-2). Maximum 8.

Quick Recap

  1. The enhanced greenhouse effect, caused mainly by burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture, is driving global warming.
  2. Consequences include sea-level rise, extreme weather, threats to food and water, health risks and displacement.
  3. Impacts are unequal: the least responsible countries and people are often the most vulnerable.
  4. Sustainable development meets present needs without compromising future generations and balances environment, society and economy.
  5. Mitigation reduces the causes of climate change; adaptation helps people cope with its effects.
  6. International action includes the Kyoto Protocol, the Paris Agreement and the loss and damage fund.
  7. Perspectives differ on responsibility, costs and whether individual or systemic change matters most.
