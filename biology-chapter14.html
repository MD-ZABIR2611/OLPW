Ecology, Ecosystems & Human Influences

CORE
SECTION 01 — CHAPTER OVERVIEW

The Web of Life

Ecology is the study of how organisms interact with each other and their physical environment. This final chapter covers food chains, nutrient cycles (carbon and nitrogen), population dynamics, and the major impacts humans have on the planet, such as pollution and climate change.

SECTION 02 — LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Chapter Goals

  • Define ecosystem, population, community, and habitat.
  • Construct and interpret food chains, food webs, and pyramids of biomass.
  • Explain the carbon and nitrogen cycles.
  • Discuss the effects of deforestation, pollution, and climate change.
  • Describe the process of eutrophication.
SECTION 03 — CORE THEORY

Ecosystems & Cycles

An ecosystem is all the living organisms and physical conditions in a habitat. A population is all the organisms of one species in a habitat. A community is all the populations in a habitat.

Energy Transfer

Energy enters a food chain via producers (plants) that absorb light for photosynthesis. Energy is passed to primary consumers (herbivores) and secondary consumers (carnivores). At each trophic level, 90% of energy is lost to the environment via respiration, movement, and heat. Only 10% is used for growth. This is why food chains rarely have more than 4 levels and why pyramids of biomass always shrink.

The Carbon Cycle

Carbon is removed from the atmosphere by plants during photosynthesis. It is passed through food chains. It is returned to the atmosphere by respiration (from plants and animals), combustion (burning fossil fuels), and decay (decomposers breaking down dead material).

Eutrophication

When excess fertilizers wash into rivers, they cause algal blooms. The algae block sunlight, causing underwater plants to die. Bacteria decompose the dead plants and algae, using up all the oxygen in the water. Fish suffocate and die.

SECTION 04 — KEY DEFINITIONS

Examination Definitions

ECOSYSTEM: A community of living organisms and their physical environment interacting as a system.

TROPHIC LEVEL: The position of an organism in a food chain (e.g., producer, primary consumer).

EUTROPHICATION: The process where excess nutrients in water cause rapid growth of algae, leading to oxygen depletion.

SECTION 06 — PRACTICAL LAB

Quadrat Sampling

AIM

To estimate the population size of a plant species in a field.

METHOD

1. Place a 1m² quadrat randomly on the ground.

2. Count the number of target organisms inside.

3. Repeat many times in different random locations.

4. Calculate the mean number per quadrat.

5. Multiply the mean by the total area of the field.

SECTION 07 — EXAM TECHNIQUE

Describing Eutrophication

For 4-5 marks, you must sequence the events perfectly: 1) Fertilizer enters water. 2) Algae grow rapidly. 3) Light is blocked, plants die. 4) Bacteria decompose dead matter. 5) Bacteria use up oxygen. 6) Fish die.

SECTION 08 — COMMON MISTAKES

Avoid These Errors

Mistake: Saying energy is "created" at the producer level.

Correction: Energy is "converted" or "absorbed" from sunlight. It is never created.

Mistake: Drawing a pyramid of numbers that doesn't look like a pyramid (e.g., one huge tree supporting 100 insects). Pyramids of numbers can be inverted; pyramids of biomass can never be inverted.

SECTION 09 — QUICK REVISION

Fast Facts

  • Only 10% of energy is passed to the next trophic level.
  • Carbon enters food chains via photosynthesis; exits via respiration/combustion.
  • Nitrogen gas cannot be used by plants; it must be fixed by bacteria into nitrates.
  • Deforestation increases atmospheric CO2 (less photosynthesis, more burning/decay).
SECTION 10 — EXAM QUESTIONS

Test Your Knowledge

Question 1 (Challenge)

Explain why a pyramid of biomass for a food chain is always pyramid-shaped, even if the pyramid of numbers is inverted.

3 marks: Biomass is the total dry mass of living material. One large producer (like a tree) contains a massive amount of biomass, which can support a larger number of smaller consumers (insects) whose total mass is less than the tree's. Energy is lost at each level (respiration/heat), so the total biomass must decrease at each step up the chain.
