Human Nutrition

CORE
SECTION 01 — CHAPTER OVERVIEW

The Digestive System

Humans require a balanced diet containing carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins, minerals, fibre, and water. This chapter covers the organs of the digestive system and how large, insoluble food molecules are broken down into small, soluble ones for absorption.

SECTION 02 — LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Chapter Goals

  • Identify the organs of the human digestive system.
  • Describe the functions of amylase, protease, and lipase.
  • Explain the role of bile in digestion.
  • Describe the adaptations of the small intestine (villi) for absorption.
SECTION 03 — CORE THEORY

Digestion & Absorption

Digestion occurs in several stages. Mechanical digestion (chewing, churning) breaks food into smaller pieces. Chemical digestion uses enzymes to break large molecules into soluble ones.

Key Digestive Enzymes

Amylase: Breaks down starch into simple sugars (glucose). Made in the salivary glands and pancreas.

Protease: Breaks down proteins into amino acids. Made in the stomach and pancreas.

Lipase: Breaks down lipids (fats) into fatty acids and glycerol. Made in the pancreas.

The Role of Bile

Bile is produced in the liver and stored in the gall bladder. It is released into the small intestine where it has two functions: it neutralizes the acidic stomach contents (providing the optimum pH for pancreatic enzymes), and it emulsifies fats (breaking large fat drops into smaller droplets to increase surface area for lipase).

Absorption

Absorption occurs in the small intestine. The inner wall is lined with millions of finger-like projections called villi, which drastically increase the surface area. They have thin walls (one cell thick) and a rich blood supply to absorb products rapidly into the bloodstream.

SECTION 04 — KEY DEFINITIONS

Examination Definitions

INGESTION: The taking of food into the mouth.

DIGESTION: The breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble ones.

ABSORPTION: The movement of digested food molecules through the wall of the intestine into the blood.

ASSIMILATION: The use of absorbed food molecules by body cells for energy or growth.

SECTION 06 — PRACTICAL LAB

Visking Tubing Model

AIM

To model digestion and absorption in the gut using Visking tubing.

SETUP

A piece of Visking tubing (partially permeable membrane) is tied at one end, filled with starch and amylase, and placed in a beaker of water. The water outside is tested for starch and glucose over time.

CONCLUSION

Glucose (small molecule) diffuses out into the water. Starch (large molecule) remains trapped inside the tubing.

SECTION 07 — EXAM TECHNIQUE

Explain vs Describe

If asked to "explain" how the small intestine is adapted for absorption, do not just list features. Connect them to the function.

Example: "The villi provide a large surface area, which increases the rate of absorption."

SECTION 08 — COMMON MISTAKES

Avoid These Errors

Mistake: Saying bile is an enzyme.

Correction: Bile is NOT an enzyme. It emulsifies fats and neutralizes acid. It does not chemically digest fats; lipase does that.

Mistake: Saying the stomach makes amylase.

Correction: The stomach makes protease (pepsin) and acid, which denatures any amylase swallowed.

SECTION 09 — QUICK REVISION

Fast Facts

  • Amylase: Starch → Glucose.
  • Protease: Protein → Amino Acids.
  • Lipase: Lipids → Fatty Acids + Glycerol.
  • Villi have a thin wall and large blood supply for rapid absorption.
  • Bile is made in the liver, stored in the gall bladder.
