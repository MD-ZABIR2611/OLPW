Chapter 16 · Islamiyat

Islamic Law and Jurisprudence (Usul al-Fiqh)

Sharia is not a single code but a method. This chapter explains the hierarchy of sources from which Islamic law is derived, the role of ijtihad, and the four great schools of Sunni jurisprudence.

Key Concepts

01

Fiqh is the human understanding of Sharia, derived through usul al-fiqh (principles of jurisprudence); its primary source is the Qur'an, binding in its clear rulings and values.

02

The Sunnah — the Prophet's sayings, actions and approvals recorded in Hadith — explains and specifies the Qur'an: prayer times, zakat rates and the details of hajj come from it.

03

Ijma (consensus) is the agreed opinion of qualified scholars of an age; it binds because the Prophet said his ummah would not agree upon error, and it preserved unity after revelation ended.

04

Qiyas is analogical reasoning: extending a ruling from an original case to a new case sharing the same effective cause (illah), e.g. extending the ban on wine to other intoxicants.

05

Ijtihad is the jurist's disciplined effort to derive a ruling; where texts are silent, tools such as maslaha (public welfare) and urf (custom) assist, and the gate of ijtihad remains open to qualified scholars.

06

Four Sunni schools (madhhabs) endure: Hanafi (Iraq, flexible reasoning), Maliki (Madinah, practice-centred), Shafi'i (emphasis on Hadith and precise rules) and Hanbali (most conservative); Muslims may follow any recognised school.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
ShariaThe divine guidance of Islam — God's law as revealed in the Qur'an and the Prophet's example.
FiqhThe human science of understanding and applying Sharia, produced by jurists from the recognised sources.
IjmaThe consensus of qualified scholars of a generation, accepted as a binding source of law after the Qur'an and Sunnah.
QiyasAnalogical reasoning in jurisprudence: extending a textual ruling to a new case that shares the same effective cause.
IjtihadThe disciplined intellectual effort of a qualified jurist to derive a ruling from the sources where no direct text exists.
MadhhabA recognised school of Sunni law with its own methodology, such as the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i or Hanbali school.
IllahThe effective cause shared by the original and new cases that justifies a ruling by qiyas, such as intoxication in the wine ruling.
MaslahaConsideration of public welfare or benefit used by jurists as a supporting principle when ruling on matters texts do not directly address.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Learn the sources in their hierarchy and always apply them in order in answers: name the Qur'anic principle, then how the Sunnah details it, before turning to ijma or qiyas — answers that jump straight to analogy without exhausting the higher sources lose the reasoning marks.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

List the four principal sources of Islamic law in order of authority and define any two of them.

Model answerQur'an (1); Sunnah (1); ijma, consensus of scholars (1); qiyas, analogical reasoning (1). Definitions: ijma is the agreed ruling of qualified scholars of an age, binding as a source (1); qiyas extends a ruling to a new case sharing the same effective cause (1).
CHALLENGE

A new intoxicating substance appears that did not exist in the Prophet's time. Using qiyas, explain how a jurist would rule on it, and discuss the strengths and limits of this method.

Model answerThe jurist takes the original case — wine, prohibited by text (1) — identifies its illah, the effective cause, as intoxication, and finds the new substance shares that cause, so the prohibition is extended by analogy (1). Strength: it lets revelation govern new realities consistently, closing loopholes merchants could exploit (1). Limit: results depend on how the illah is defined and on the jurist's competence, so rulings can differ between schools — hence the discipline of ijtihad and the need for learned consensus to check individual error (1).
