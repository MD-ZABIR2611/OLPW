Transport in Plants

CORE
SECTION 01 — CHAPTER OVERVIEW

Xylem & Phloem

Plants lack a pumping organ like the heart, yet they must transport water and minerals from the roots to the leaves, and sugars from the leaves to the rest of the plant. This chapter covers the vascular tissue system and the process of transpiration.

SECTION 02 — LEARNING OBJECTIVES

Chapter Goals

  • Explain the functions of xylem and phloem vessels.
  • Define transpiration and translocation.
  • Describe how environmental factors affect transpiration rate.
  • Explain the potometer practical.
SECTION 03 — CORE THEORY

Plant Transport Systems

Plants have two main transport tissues arranged in vascular bundles:

Xylem vs Phloem

Xylem: Transports water and mineral ions from roots to leaves. Made of dead cells with thick cellulose cell walls strengthened by waterproof lignin. The lignin kills the cells, leaving a hollow tube.

Phloem: Transports sucrose and amino acids from leaves to growing regions (translocation). Made of living cells arranged in columns.

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the leaves of a plant. Water evaporates from the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, then diffuses out through the stomata. This creates a "pull" that draws water up the xylem from the roots (the transpiration stream).

Factors Affecting Transpiration Rate

Temperature: Higher temp increases kinetic energy, speeding up evaporation.

Humidity: High humidity decreases the water potential gradient, slowing diffusion out of the leaf.

Wind speed: High wind blows away water vapour outside the leaf, maintaining a steep gradient.

Light intensity: Higher light causes stomata to open wider, allowing more water to escape.

SECTION 04 — KEY DEFINITIONS

Examination Definitions

TRANSPIRATION: The evaporation of water from the leaves of a plant, creating a tension that pulls water up through the xylem.

TRANSLOCATION: The movement of sucrose and amino acids in the phloem, from regions of production (sources) to regions of use/storage (sinks).

SECTION 06 — PRACTICAL LAB

Potometer Investigation

AIM

To measure the rate of water uptake by a plant (an estimate of transpiration rate).

METHOD

1. Cut a shoot underwater (to prevent air entering xylem).

2. Fit shoot into potometer underwater.

3. Introduce an air bubble into the capillary tube.

4. Time how long the bubble takes to move a set distance.

LIMITATIONS

The potometer measures water uptake, not just transpiration. Some water is used in photosynthesis or to keep cells turgid, so the rate of uptake is slightly higher than actual transpiration.

SECTION 07 — EXAM TECHNIQUE

Explaining Transpiration

Always explain transpiration step-by-step: Water evaporates from mesophyll cells → into air spaces → diffuses out through open stomata → lowers water potential in leaf → water pulled up xylem.

SECTION 08 — COMMON MISTAKES

Avoid These Errors

Mistake: Saying xylem cells are alive.

Correction: Xylem vessels are dead. They have no cytoplasm or end walls, forming a continuous hollow tube.

Mistake: Confusing transpiration with translocation.

SECTION 09 — QUICK REVISION

Fast Facts

  • Xylem: Up. Dead. Water + Minerals.
  • Phloem: Up and Down. Living. Sucrose + Amino acids.
  • Transpiration is highest in hot, dry, windy, bright conditions.
  • Potometers measure water uptake, not exact transpiration.
SECTION 10 — EXAM QUESTIONS

Test Your Knowledge

Question 1 (Standard)

Explain why an increase in wind speed increases the rate of transpiration.

2 marks: Wind blows away water vapour that has diffused out of the stomata. This maintains a steep water potential gradient between the inside of the leaf and the air outside, increasing the rate of diffusion/evaporation.
