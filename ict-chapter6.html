01

Overview

This chapter shows how the hardware and software you have studied are used in real organisations. IGCSE ICT exams are full of scenarios: a bank introducing online banking, a hospital using an expert system, a cinema taking online bookings or a car using satellite navigation. To answer well you need to describe the stages of each application in order and explain its advantages and disadvantages to the different people involved, such as the customer, the company and employees. Learning a clear sequence of steps for key applications, such as an ATM withdrawal or an online booking, will earn reliable marks.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Describe communication applications and computer modelling, giving advantages and disadvantages.
  • Explain how robots and computer control are used in manufacturing.
  • Describe school management systems and online booking systems, including the steps of a booking.
  • Describe banking applications including ATMs, electronic funds transfer and internet banking.
  • Explain how expert systems work and where they are used, including in medicine.
  • Describe retail applications such as EFTPOS and automatic stock control.
  • Describe recognition systems and satellite systems such as GPS and GIS.
02

Communication and Modelling Applications

ICT is used to produce communication media such as newsletters, posters, flyers, brochures, business cards, websites and multimedia presentations. Using desktop publishing and word processing software, organisations can produce professional material quickly, edit it easily, and print or publish it online to reach a large audience. Mobile phones and Voice over Internet Protocol (VoIP) allow cheap voice and video calls over the internet, but VoIP call quality depends on the internet connection and can suffer from echo or dropped calls.

A computer model is a representation of a real-life system using mathematical formulas and rules, usually built in a spreadsheet or specialist software. Models are used for personal finance, designing bridges and buildings, flood water management, traffic management and weather forecasting. The user can change input values and ask 'what if' questions to see the effect, for example what happens to a bridge if the wind speed increases. Models are cheaper and safer than building real prototypes, allow dangerous situations to be tested without risk, and allow time to be sped up or slowed down.

However, models are only as accurate as the data and formulas used to build them. If the model does not include all factors, or the assumptions are wrong, the results will be unreliable. Building a complex model can be expensive and requires expertise, and people may trust the results too much without checking them against real-world data.

Key fact:Models let you ask 'what if' questions safely and cheaply, but are only as good as the rules and data they contain.
03

Computer-Controlled Systems and Robotics

Robots are widely used in manufacturing, for example in car factories for welding, spray painting, fitting windscreens and assembling parts. Robots are programmed either by writing instructions or by a human guiding the robot arm through the movements, which are then stored and repeated. Sensors provide feedback, and actuators such as motors move the robot. Production control systems also use computers to monitor and adjust processes such as chemical manufacture, maintaining conditions like temperature and pressure within set limits.

Robots work 24 hours a day without breaks, produce consistent quality, are more accurate than humans and can work in dangerous environments such as with toxic paint fumes or high temperatures. This reduces labour costs and improves productivity. The disadvantages are high set-up and maintenance costs, loss of jobs for unskilled workers, difficulty coping with unusual situations that were not programmed, and the need for skilled technicians to program and maintain them. New jobs are created in robot maintenance and programming.

04

School Management and Booking Systems

School management systems store data about students and staff. Registration and attendance can be recorded using OMR registers, electronic registers on computers, swipe cards or biometric systems such as fingerprint scanners, which are fast, accurate and allow parents to be alerted automatically if a student is absent. The system also records student performance, such as test marks, and produces reports. Computer-aided learning (CAL) uses computers to deliver lessons and tests, letting students learn at their own pace with instant feedback, but it lacks the personal support a teacher gives.

Online booking systems are used for travel (flights, trains, hotels), concerts, cinemas and sporting events. They are real-time systems: as soon as a seat is booked, the database is updated so the same seat cannot be sold twice. A typical online booking follows set steps. The customer selects the event, date and number of tickets; the system checks the database and displays available seats; the customer chooses seats, which are temporarily locked; the customer enters personal and payment details; the payment is authorised by the bank; the database is updated to mark the seats as sold; and a confirmation email and e-ticket are sent.

Advantages for customers include booking at any time from anywhere, seeing seat availability instantly, comparing prices and receiving e-tickets. Advantages for the company include fewer staff, lower costs and a larger market. Disadvantages include the risk of fraud or hacking, the need for internet access, fewer personal interactions and the difficulty of resolving problems without speaking to someone.

  • Select event, date and number of tickets.
  • System searches the database and shows available seats.
  • Customer selects seats; they are temporarily held.
  • Customer enters details and pays; payment is authorised.
  • Database is updated to mark seats as booked.
  • Confirmation and e-ticket are sent by email.
05

Banking Applications

An automated teller machine (ATM) allows customers to withdraw cash, check balances, deposit money and pay bills. A cash withdrawal follows these steps: the customer inserts the card; the chip is read to identify the account; the customer enters their PIN, which is checked against the value stored on the chip or at the bank; the customer selects withdraw cash and enters the amount; the bank's computer checks there are enough funds; the account is updated; the card is returned; the cash is dispensed; and a receipt is printed if requested. If the PIN is entered incorrectly three times, the card is usually retained.

Electronic funds transfer (EFT) moves money between bank accounts electronically without cash changing hands, for example when employers pay salaries directly into employees' accounts or when you pay by card in a shop. Debit cards take money directly from the customer's current account, while credit cards borrow money from the card company, which is repaid later, possibly with interest. Cheques are still used in some countries and are processed using MICR.

Internet banking lets customers manage their accounts online: checking balances, transferring money, paying bills and setting up standing orders. Customers save time and travel, can bank 24/7 and can easily monitor spending. Banks save on branch and staff costs. However, customers need internet access, there is a risk of hacking and phishing, older people may find it difficult, and local branches may close, leading to job losses and less personal service.

Key fact:ATM order: insert card, read chip, enter PIN, check PIN, choose amount, check funds, update account, return card, dispense cash.
06

Expert Systems and Computers in Medicine

An expert system is a computer program that mimics the knowledge and decision-making of a human expert in a particular field. It consists of a user interface, a knowledge base containing facts, an inference engine that applies rules to the facts to reach conclusions, a rules base of IF-THEN style rules, and an explanation system that tells the user how a conclusion was reached. Expert systems are used for medical diagnosis, mineral prospecting, car engine fault diagnosis, financial planning, chess games and plant and animal identification.

To use a medical expert system, the doctor enters the patient's symptoms through the user interface, often answering yes or no questions. The inference engine compares the symptoms with the rules and facts in the knowledge base and produces a list of possible illnesses with probabilities and suggested treatments, and the explanation system justifies the conclusion. Expert systems provide consistent answers, never forget facts, can be used anywhere and speed up diagnosis, but they lack human common sense and emotional understanding, depend on the accuracy of the knowledge base and are expensive to set up.

Hospitals also use computers to store patient records, including personal details, medical history, allergies and prescriptions, so that authorised staff can access them quickly from any ward. Pharmacy records check that prescribed drugs will not react dangerously with other medication or allergies. 3D printers produce prosthetics, implants and models of organs to help plan surgery, and computers monitor patients' vital signs such as heart rate, blood pressure and temperature continuously, sounding alarms if readings go outside safe limits.

  • User interface: where the user enters data and sees results.
  • Knowledge base: stores the facts about the subject.
  • Rules base: stores the rules used for reasoning.
  • Inference engine: applies the rules to the facts to reach conclusions.
  • Explanation system: explains how the conclusion was reached.
07

Retail Applications

Supermarkets use point of sale (POS) terminals at checkouts. When a barcode is scanned, the product code is searched for in the stock database, the price and description are sent back to the checkout and shown on screen, and the stock level for that item is reduced by one. If the stock level falls below the reorder level, the system automatically orders more from the supplier. This is automatic stock control, which prevents shelves from being empty and avoids overstocking. Electronic funds transfer at point of sale (EFTPOS) lets customers pay by card, with the money transferred from their bank to the shop's account.

Internet shopping allows customers to browse and buy online at any time, compare prices easily and have goods delivered. Businesses can reach a global market and reduce costs by needing fewer shops and staff. However, customers cannot see or try goods before buying, delivery costs and delays may apply, there is a risk of fraud, and town-centre shops may close, leading to job losses and less social interaction.

08

Recognition and Satellite Systems

Recognition systems identify items or people automatically. OMR is used for multiple-choice tests and registers, OCR converts scanned documents into editable text, RFID tracks stock, animals and passports, and biometric systems identify people from fingerprints, faces or irises for passports, phone unlocking and school registration. Automatic number plate recognition (ANPR) uses cameras and OCR to read vehicle number plates for car park entry, congestion charging and speed enforcement. These systems are fast and accurate but raise privacy concerns about constant monitoring.

Satellite systems include GPS, GIS and media communication. The global positioning system (GPS) uses signals from at least four satellites orbiting the Earth; a receiver calculates its position from the time each signal takes to arrive. Satellite navigation (satnav) systems in cars use GPS with stored maps to give turn-by-turn directions, estimate arrival times and warn of traffic. A geographic information system (GIS) layers different data, such as roads, population and flood zones, onto maps for analysis and planning. Communication satellites relay television, phone and internet signals around the world. Satnavs reduce the stress of navigating unfamiliar areas, but maps can be out of date, signals can be lost in tunnels, and drivers may follow instructions blindly.

Key fact:GPS needs signals from several satellites; the receiver calculates position from the signal travel times.

Worked Examples

Automatic stock control in a supermarket

Describe how a supermarket computer system uses barcodes to keep stock levels up to date and reorder goods.

  1. The barcode on each item is scanned at the checkout and the product code is read.
  2. The product code is searched for in the stock file in the database.
  3. When the matching record is found, the price and description are sent to the checkout and printed on the receipt.
  4. The stock level in the record is reduced by the number of items sold.
  5. The new stock level is compared with the reorder level; if it is equal to or below it, an order is automatically sent to the supplier and a flag set so the item is not reordered twice.
  6. When new stock arrives, the stock level is updated and the flag is reset.
Scanning the barcode finds the product record, returns the price, reduces the stock level and triggers an automatic reorder when the level reaches the reorder level.

Using a medical expert system

Describe how a doctor would use an expert system to help diagnose a patient's illness. (4 marks)

  1. The doctor enters the patient's symptoms through the user interface, often answering on-screen questions.
  2. The inference engine searches the knowledge base using the rules base.
  3. The system produces possible diagnoses with probabilities or percentages.
  4. The explanation system shows the reasoning so the doctor can check it, and the doctor makes the final decision.
Symptoms entered via interface (1); inference engine compares with knowledge base (1) using rules (1); outputs possible illnesses with probabilities (1); explanation system shows reasoning (1). Max 4.

Building a flood model

A town council wants to use a computer model to plan flood defences. Explain why a model is used rather than waiting to observe real floods.

  1. Real floods are dangerous and unpredictable, so they cannot be tested safely.
  2. The model can change variables such as rainfall and river level to answer 'what if' questions.
  3. Many different defence designs can be tested quickly and cheaply before any money is spent building them.
  4. Time can be sped up so the effects of decades of rainfall can be predicted in minutes.
A model is safer, cheaper and faster, allows 'what if' testing of different scenarios and defences, and can predict long-term effects, although its accuracy depends on the data and rules used.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Missing out steps or getting them in the wrong order when describing an ATM withdrawal or online booking.
  • Saying the barcode contains the price. It contains the product code; the price is stored in the database.
  • Describing an expert system without naming its parts. Use the terms knowledge base, rules base, inference engine and explanation system.
  • Saying GPS satellites 'track' the car. The receiver in the car calculates its own position from satellite signals.
  • Only giving advantages for robots. Mention cost, job losses and inability to handle unexpected situations.
  • Confusing debit and credit cards. Debit takes money directly from your account; credit borrows money to be repaid later.

Exam tips

  • Learn key applications as numbered sequences and reproduce them in order; examiners award a mark for each correct step.
  • When asked for advantages, consider who benefits: the customer, the organisation or the employee, and state it.
  • In expert system questions, explain what each component does, not just its name.
  • For booking systems, mention that the database is updated immediately to prevent double booking.
  • In modelling questions, always use the phrase 'what if' and mention safety, cost and time.
  • Link recognition systems to their input devices, for example ANPR uses cameras and OCR software.

Model answerCheaper than building real prototypes (1); safer as dangerous conditions can be tested without risk (1); 'what if' questions can be tested easily (1); faster to test many designs (1). Max 2.

2. Give two advantages and two disadvantages of using robots in car manufacturing.

4. Explain how an online cinema booking system prevents the same seat being sold twice.

Model answerIt is a real-time system (1); when a seat is selected it is temporarily locked/held (1); once paid, the database is updated immediately to show the seat as booked (1) so other users see it as unavailable (1). Max 3.

Model answerUser interface, knowledge base, rules base, inference engine, explanation system (1 each).

7. Explain the difference between a debit card and a credit card.

Model answerA debit card takes money directly from the customer's bank account (1); a credit card borrows money from the card company (1) which must be paid back later, possibly with interest (1).

8. Describe how a GPS receiver in a car determines its position.

Model answerIt receives signals from several (at least three/four) satellites (1); each signal contains the time it was sent (1); the receiver calculates the distance to each satellite from the travel time (1) and uses these to work out its position (1). Max 3.

9. Give two advantages and two disadvantages to customers of internet banking.

Model answerAdvantages: available 24/7; saves travel time and cost; easy to monitor account; can bank from anywhere. Disadvantages: needs internet access; risk of hacking/phishing; less personal contact; may be difficult for older users; easier to overspend (1 each).

10. Evaluate the use of biometric registration systems in schools compared with paper registers.

Disadvantages: expensive to install; privacy concerns about storing fingerprints/face data; system failure stops registration; some parents may object. Paper: cheap but slow, easily lost and can be falsified. Conclusion (1 per point, max 6).

Model answerAny two: car park entry/payment; congestion charging; speed enforcement; tracking stolen vehicles; toll roads (1 each).

Quick Recap

  1. Computer models allow safe, cheap 'what if' testing, but depend on accurate formulas and data.
  2. Robots give consistent, accurate, continuous work in manufacturing but cost jobs and money to set up.
  3. Online booking systems are real-time, so the database is updated immediately to prevent double booking.
  4. ATMs, EFT and internet banking let customers manage money electronically, with convenience balanced against fraud risks.
  5. Expert systems use a knowledge base, rules base and inference engine to mimic human experts, for example in medical diagnosis.
  6. Retail systems use barcodes, POS terminals and automatic stock control to price goods and reorder stock.
  7. Recognition systems such as ANPR, RFID and biometrics identify items or people automatically.
  8. GPS calculates position from satellite signals, while GIS layers data onto maps for analysis.
