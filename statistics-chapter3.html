Questionnaires and Surveys

How to design clear, unbiased questionnaires, choose a survey method, run a pilot survey and deal with non-response and sensitive questions.

01

Overview

Questionnaires and surveys are the most common way of collecting primary data about people's opinions, habits and circumstances. A single badly worded question can ruin an otherwise careful investigation, because people may misunderstand it, refuse to answer it or be pushed towards a particular reply. In O-Level Statistics you are frequently given a poor question and asked to state what is wrong with it and to rewrite it, or asked to compare postal, telephone, online and face-to-face surveys. This chapter shows you the rules of good question design, how to write response boxes that work, why a pilot survey matters and how to calculate and interpret response rates.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Distinguish between open and closed questions and state when each is appropriate.
  • Identify faults in questions, including leading, ambiguous, vague and overly personal wording.
  • Design non-overlapping, exhaustive response boxes for a closed question.
  • Explain the purpose of a pilot survey.
  • Compare face-to-face interviews, telephone, postal and online surveys.
  • Calculate a response rate and explain how non-response can bias results.
02

What a Questionnaire Is For

A questionnaire is a structured set of questions designed to collect information from respondents in a consistent way. Because every respondent answers the same questions in the same order, the answers can be compared, counted and analysed. A questionnaire may be completed by the respondent alone, called self-completion, or read out by an interviewer who records the answers.

Before writing any questions, the investigator must be clear about the aim of the survey and the hypothesis being tested. Every question should help to answer that aim; unnecessary questions make the questionnaire longer, reduce the response rate and produce data that are never used. A good questionnaire is short, starts with easy and non-threatening questions, keeps related questions together and leaves any personal questions, such as age group or income band, until the end.

The layout also matters. Clear instructions such as 'tick one box' or 'tick all that apply' prevent confusion, and a brief introduction explaining the purpose of the survey and promising confidentiality encourages people to take part and to answer honestly.

03

Open and Closed Questions

An open question allows respondents to answer in their own words, for example 'What do you think of the school canteen?' Open questions give rich, detailed information and may reveal ideas the investigator had not thought of. However, the answers vary greatly, take longer to write and are difficult to group, count and analyse statistically.

A closed question gives a fixed set of possible answers, and the respondent chooses one, for example by ticking a box. Closed questions are quick to answer and the results are easy to tally and present in tables and charts. Their weakness is that the respondent may not find an option that fits exactly, which is why an 'Other (please state)' option is often added. Closed questions may use yes/no answers, multiple-choice options, rating scales such as 1 to 5, or ranking of several items.

For statistical analysis closed questions are generally preferred, but an open question in a pilot survey can help the investigator discover which options to include in the final closed version.

Key fact:Open questions = rich but hard to analyse. Closed questions = easy to analyse but may restrict answers.
04

Rules for Writing Good Questions

A good question is clear, short and means the same thing to every respondent. It should use simple language, avoid technical terms and ask about only one thing at a time. A question such as 'Do you like tea and coffee?' is a double question; someone who likes tea but not coffee cannot answer it. It should be split into two separate questions.

Questions must not be leading. A leading question suggests the answer the investigator wants, for example 'Don't you agree that the new sports hall is a waste of money?' The neutral version is 'What is your opinion of the new sports hall?' with balanced options such as very good, good, poor, very poor. Questions must also not be vague. 'Do you watch a lot of television?' is vague because 'a lot' means different things to different people; a better question asks how many hours were watched yesterday, with grouped response boxes.

Questions should specify a time frame where relevant ('in the last seven days'), avoid relying on memory over long periods and avoid being unnecessarily personal or embarrassing. If personal information such as age or income is essential, use grouped categories so that respondents do not have to give exact figures.

  • Avoid leading questions that suggest an answer.
  • Avoid ambiguous or vague words such as 'often', 'a lot' or 'regularly'.
  • Ask one thing per question – no double questions.
  • Give a clear time frame.
  • Use grouped categories for sensitive personal data.
05

Designing Response Boxes

Response boxes for closed questions must satisfy two rules. First, they must not overlap, so that every possible answer fits into exactly one box. Boxes labelled 0–2, 2–4 and 4–6 hours are faulty because a person who watched 2 hours could tick two boxes. Second, they must be exhaustive, covering every possible answer, which usually means including an open-ended box such as 'more than 6' at the top, and '0' or 'none' at the bottom.

For continuous variables such as time, clear inequality labels remove all doubt, for example: 0 ≤ t < 1, 1 ≤ t < 2, 2 ≤ t < 3, t ≥ 3 hours. For discrete variables, whole-number labels such as 0, 1–2, 3–4, 5 or more work well. The number of boxes should be manageable, usually between four and six.

Rating scales need balance: the same number of positive and negative options, possibly with a neutral midpoint. A scale of excellent, very good, good, poor is unbalanced because three options are positive and only one is negative, so results will be pushed towards the favourable end.

Key fact:Response boxes must be non-overlapping and cover all possible answers.
06

The Pilot Survey

A pilot survey is a small-scale trial of the questionnaire carried out on a few people before the main survey. Its purpose is to find problems while they can still be fixed cheaply. The pilot may reveal questions that are misunderstood, response boxes that do not cover common answers, a questionnaire that takes too long to complete, or instructions that are unclear.

A pilot survey also gives the investigator an idea of the likely response rate, how long each interview takes and how much the full survey will cost. Open questions in the pilot can suggest which categories to use for closed questions in the final version. Data from the pilot are usually not included in the final results, because the questions will have changed. In exam answers, the phrase 'to identify and correct problems with the questions before the main survey' earns the standard mark.

07

Methods of Conducting a Survey

Face-to-face interviews have a high response rate, allow the interviewer to explain unclear questions and allow follow-up questions. They are, however, expensive and slow, and interviewer bias can arise if the interviewer's tone, appearance or reactions influence answers. Respondents may also give answers they think are socially acceptable rather than honest ones.

Telephone surveys are cheaper than face-to-face interviews and can reach a wide area quickly, but people often refuse, not everyone can be reached at convenient times and the interviewer cannot show pictures or cards. Postal surveys are cheap and reach large numbers; respondents have time to think and may answer sensitive questions more honestly because no interviewer is present. The major weakness is a low response rate, and questions cannot be clarified.

Online surveys are very cheap, fast and automatically record the data. They exclude people without internet access and often rely on volunteers, so they suffer from self-selection bias. Choosing a method means balancing cost, speed, response rate and the likely bias.

  • Face-to-face: high response, can clarify; costly, interviewer bias.
  • Telephone: quick, wide coverage; refusals, no visual aids.
  • Postal: cheap, honest answers to sensitive questions; low response, no clarification.
  • Online: cheapest and fastest; excludes non-users, volunteer bias.
08

Response Rates, Non-response and Sensitive Questions

The response rate is the percentage of people in the sample who actually respond: response rate = (number of responses ÷ number in sample) × 100%. A postal survey might achieve only 20% to 30%. Non-response is a problem not just because the sample becomes smaller, but because the people who reply may differ from those who do not. People with strong opinions, more free time or a particular interest in the topic are more likely to reply, so the results may be biased.

Ways of improving response include sending reminders, offering a small incentive, providing a reply-paid envelope, keeping the questionnaire short and calling back at different times for interviews. When non-response occurs, the investigator should not simply replace the non-responders with anyone available, because this can add further bias.

Sensitive questions, such as about cheating, illegal activity or income, often receive dishonest answers. Anonymity helps. One clever approach is the random response technique: each respondent privately tosses a coin; on a head they answer the sensitive question truthfully, and on a tail they simply answer 'Yes'. Because the interviewer cannot tell which question was answered, people are more willing to be honest, and the true proportion can still be estimated using probability.

Key fact:Response rate = (responses ÷ number sampled) × 100%.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
QuestionnaireA structured list of questions used to collect information in the same way from each respondent.
RespondentA person who answers the questions in a survey.
Open questionA question that allows respondents to answer freely in their own words.
Closed questionA question with a fixed set of answers from which the respondent must choose.
Leading questionA question worded so that it encourages a particular answer.
Ambiguous questionA question that can be understood in more than one way.
Pilot surveyA small trial survey carried out before the main survey to test and improve the questionnaire and procedures.
Response rateThe percentage of people in a sample who return or complete the questionnaire.
Non-responseFailure of selected members of a sample to take part or answer, which may bias the results.
Interviewer biasDistortion of answers caused by the way an interviewer asks questions or reacts to replies.
Self-selection biasBias that arises when respondents choose themselves to take part, as in phone-in or online polls.

Worked Examples

Improving a faulty question

A student writes this question: 'How much do you spend on snacks? 0–1 dollar, 1–2 dollars, 2–5 dollars.' Give two criticisms and write an improved question.

  1. There is no time frame: it is unclear whether this means per day, per week or per month.
  2. The response boxes overlap: someone who spends exactly 1 dollar or 2 dollars could tick two boxes.
  3. The boxes are not exhaustive: there is no box for more than 5 dollars.
  4. Improved question: 'How much did you spend on snacks in the last 7 days? Tick one box: $0, more than $0 up to $2, more than $2 up to $5, more than $5 up to $10, more than $10.'
Criticisms: no time period; overlapping boxes; not all answers covered. The improved question gives a time frame and non-overlapping, exhaustive boxes.

Response rate and non-response bias

A council posts 500 questionnaires about a new bus route. 135 are returned, of which 81 support the route. (a) Calculate the response rate. (b) Calculate the percentage of respondents supporting the route. (c) Explain why the council should be cautious about concluding that most residents support it.

  1. (a) Response rate = (135 ÷ 500) × 100% = 27%.
  2. (b) Support among respondents = (81 ÷ 135) × 100% = 60%.
  3. (c) Only 27% responded, so 73% of the sample gave no view. People who would use the new route are likely to be more motivated to reply than those indifferent to it.
  4. In the worst case, if all non-responders opposed the route, support would be only 81 ÷ 500 = 16.2% of the sample.
(a) 27%. (b) 60%. (c) The low response rate may cause non-response bias because supporters are more likely to reply, so 60% may overestimate true support; true support in the sample could be anywhere from 16.2% (81 ÷ 500) to 89.2% ((81 + 365) ÷ 500).

Estimating a proportion with the random response technique

200 students are asked 'Have you ever copied homework?' Each tosses a fair coin in private: on a head they answer truthfully, on a tail they answer 'Yes'. There are 130 'Yes' answers. Estimate the proportion who have copied homework.

  1. Expected number of tails = ½ × 200 = 100, and all of these answer 'Yes'.
  2. So the number of 'Yes' answers from students who threw a head = 130 − 100 = 30.
  3. Expected number of heads = 100, so 30 out of 100 students answering truthfully said 'Yes'.
  4. Estimated proportion = 30 ÷ 100 = 0.3.
About 30% of students are estimated to have copied homework.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Rewriting a faulty question but leaving the response boxes overlapping; check every boundary value fits exactly one box.
  • Forgetting a box for the highest or lowest answers, such as 'none' or 'more than 10'.
  • Stating that a pilot survey is used 'to get more data'. Its purpose is to test and improve the questionnaire.
  • Criticising a question only as 'bad' or 'confusing' without saying exactly why, for example 'leading' or 'no time period given'.
  • Replacing a question with another leading question, such as changing 'Don't you agree the park is great?' to 'Do you agree that the park is excellent?'.
  • Assuming that a large number of returned questionnaires removes bias, even when the response rate is low.

Exam tips

  • When criticising a question, use the technical words: leading, ambiguous, vague, personal, overlapping, not exhaustive, no time frame.
  • Always write an improved question in full, including the response boxes, when the question asks for one.
  • Use inequality signs or 'more than 2 up to 5' style wording in response boxes to make boundaries unambiguous.
  • For survey method comparisons, mention response rate, cost, speed and bias for each method.
  • Quote a response rate as a percentage and comment on whether it is high enough to be reliable.
  • Remember the standard purposes of a pilot survey: check clarity of questions, check the time taken and estimate response rate.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. State one advantage and one disadvantage of an open question.

Model answerAdvantage: gives detailed answers / allows unexpected responses (1). Disadvantage: answers are difficult to group and analyse (1).
[2 marks]

2. Explain why the question 'Don't you think smoking should be banned in parks?' is unsuitable.

Model answerIt is a leading question (1) because its wording encourages the respondent to agree that smoking should be banned (1).
[2 marks]

3. Rewrite the question in the previous part so that it is suitable.

Model answer'Should smoking be banned in parks?' with response boxes: Yes / No / No opinion (or a balanced scale from strongly agree to strongly disagree).
[2 marks]

4. Give two faults in the response boxes 1–5, 5–10, 10–15 for the question 'How many text messages did you send yesterday?'

Model answerThe boxes overlap at 5 and 10 (1). There is no box for 0 or for more than 15 (1).
[2 marks]

5. What is a pilot survey and why is it carried out?

Model answerA small-scale trial of the survey before the main survey (1) to identify and correct problems such as unclear questions or unsuitable response boxes / to estimate time and response rate (1).
[2 marks]

6. A researcher sends 800 questionnaires and receives 176 replies. Calculate the response rate.

Model answer(176 ÷ 800) × 100% = 22%.
[2 marks]

7. Give two advantages of a face-to-face interview compared with a postal questionnaire.

Model answerAny two: higher response rate; interviewer can explain questions; follow-up questions possible; questionnaire completed fully; can use visual prompts.
[2 marks]

8. Explain why an online poll on a news website may give biased results.

Model answerRespondents select themselves, so only people who are interested or hold strong opinions take part (1). People without internet access or who do not visit that site are excluded, so the sample is not representative (1).
[3 marks]

9. Design a suitable question, with response boxes, to find out how long students spend on homework each weekday evening.

Model answer'On average, how many hours did you spend on homework each weekday evening last week? Tick one box: less than 1 hour; 1 hour or more but less than 2 hours; 2 hours or more but less than 3 hours; 3 hours or more.' (1 for time frame, 1 non-overlapping, 1 exhaustive.)
[3 marks]

10. 300 employees are asked 'Have you ever taken a day off work when not ill?' using the random response technique: each rolls a fair die privately; if it shows 1 or 2 they answer 'Yes' automatically, otherwise they answer truthfully. There are 140 'Yes' answers. Estimate the proportion who have taken a day off when not ill.

Model answerExpected automatic 'Yes' = (2/6) × 300 = 100 (1). 'Yes' from truthful answers = 140 − 100 = 40 (1). Number answering truthfully = (4/6) × 300 = 200, so proportion = 40 ÷ 200 = 0.2 (20%) (1).
[5 marks]

11. A sports club wants to find members' views on changing its opening hours. Describe how it should plan and carry out a questionnaire survey, referring to question design, piloting, sampling and response rate.

Model answerState the aim clearly and keep the questionnaire short and relevant (1). Use closed questions with clear, balanced, non-overlapping options, avoid leading wording, give a time frame and leave personal questions to the end (1). Carry out a pilot survey on a few members and amend unclear questions (1). Select a random or stratified random sample (e.g. by age group or membership type) from the membership list (1). Maximise the response rate with reminders, a deadline and a convenient return method, and report the response rate when presenting results (1).

14

Quick Recap

  1. A questionnaire should be short, relevant to the aim, logically ordered and have clear instructions.
  2. Open questions give detail but are hard to analyse; closed questions are easy to analyse but may restrict answers.
  3. Good questions are neutral, specific, ask one thing and include a time frame.
  4. Response boxes must not overlap and must cover every possible answer.
  5. A pilot survey tests the questionnaire so that problems can be corrected before the main survey.
  6. Each survey method trades off cost, speed, response rate and risk of bias.
  7. Response rate = responses ÷ number sampled × 100%, and low response can cause non-response bias.
  8. Anonymity and the random response technique encourage honest answers to sensitive questions.
