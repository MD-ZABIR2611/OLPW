Chapter 17 · Islamiyat

Later Islamic History: Umayyad and Abbasid Contributions

After the rightly guided era, the caliphate became empire — and civilisation. This chapter traces the Umayyad and Abbasid achievements in administration, science and culture that carried Islamic learning to three continents.

Key Concepts

01

The Umayyad caliphate (661–750), capital Damascus, made Arabic the language of administration, minted the first Islamic coinage and extended Muslim rule from Spain (al-Andalus) to the Indus under commanders such as Tariq ibn Ziyad and Muhammad bin Qasim.

02

Abd al-Malik's administration standardised Arabic coinage and the Dome of the Rock inscription; the Umayyads also built great desert palaces and the Umayyad Mosque of Damascus.

03

The Abbasid revolution of 750 moved the capital to Baghdad, built by al-Mansur as the City of Peace (Madinat al-Salam); under Harun al-Rashid and al-Ma'mun, Baghdad became the world's intellectual capital.

04

The House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma) in Baghdad sponsored the translation of Greek, Persian and Indian works into Arabic, while observatories and the bayt al-hikma tradition advanced algebra (al-Khwarizmi), medicine (Ibn Sina) and optics (Ibn al-Haytham).

05

Institutional legacies include the waqf (charitable endowment) funding hospitals, schools and public fountains, and the madrasa system of endowed colleges that spread across the Muslim world.

06

The period also saw internal strains — the mihna (inquisition) under al-Ma'mun, the rise of the Turks and the fragmentation into regional dynasties — showing that decline followed when knowledge and justice weakened.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Umayyad caliphateThe first hereditary caliphate, 661–750, ruling from Damascus and extending Muslim rule from Spain to India.
Abbasid caliphateThe caliphate that ruled from Baghdad from 750, presiding over the Islamic golden age of science and learning.
House of WisdomThe Baghdad academy (Bayt al-Hikma) that translated and advanced Greek, Persian and Indian scholarship.
WaqfA permanent charitable endowment in Islamic law, dedicating property or funds to religious, educational or public benefit.
MadrasaAn endowed college for the study of Islamic sciences, which spread across the Muslim world from the Abbasid era.
MihnaThe Abbasid inquisition under al-Ma'mun testing scholars on the createdness of the Qur'an, lasting about fifteen years.
Al-AndalusMuslim Spain, ruled by the Umayyads from 711, whose Cordoba became a leading centre of learning.
Golden ageThe Abbasid era of flourishing in science, philosophy and culture, roughly the 8th to 13th centuries.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Tie each contribution to its institution and its later effect: al-Khwarizmi to algebra and modern algorithms, Ibn Sina's Canon to medieval medicine, the waqf to today's endowments — examiners reward candidates who show these ages shaped the modern world, not merely that "science developed".

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Name the capitals of the Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates and one major contribution of each dynasty.

Model answerUmayyad — Damascus; contribution such as Arabic administration or rule from Spain to India (1). Abbasid — Baghdad; contribution such as the House of Wisdom, translation movement or algebra (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate the claim that the Abbasid House of Wisdom was the most important institution of the Islamic golden age.

Model answerFor: it created the translation movement that preserved Greek philosophy and science, fused it with Persian and Indian learning, and produced original advances — algebra, optics, the scientific method — which later reached Europe through Arabic texts (1). Against: it flourished because of supporting institutions — the waqf endowments that paid scholars, the madrasas that trained them, and the stability of Baghdad's trade wealth — so the House of Wisdom was a hub within a wider system, not a lone cause (1). Judgement: as a symbol and engine of synthesis it was supreme, but its achievements rested on the economic and charitable institutions of the age (1).
