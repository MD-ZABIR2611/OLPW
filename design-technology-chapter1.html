The Design Process

How designers move from an identified need to a tested, evaluated product using an iterative sequence of stages.

01

Overview

Every product you use, from a toothbrush to a bus shelter, began as a response to a need. The design process is the structured, repeatable method that designers use to turn that need into a successful product, and it underpins every paper and the coursework project in IGCSE and O-Level Design and Technology. Examiners expect you to name the stages, explain what happens at each one and show how the stages link together through feedback and iteration. Understanding the process well also makes your own design folio clearer, more logical and easier to mark.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Identify and describe the main stages of the design process in the correct order.
  • Explain the difference between a linear and an iterative design model and why iteration improves outcomes.
  • Write a clear design brief and distinguish it from a design specification.
  • Explain the roles of the client, the user and other stakeholders in a design project.
  • Describe how modelling, testing and evaluation feed back into development.
  • Apply the design process to a simple product and justify decisions at each stage.
02

What Is the Design Process?

The design process is a systematic approach to solving practical problems. Rather than jumping straight to making something, the designer works through a sequence of stages: identifying a need, writing a brief, researching, analysing the research, writing a specification, generating ideas, developing a chosen idea, planning and realising (making) the product, and finally testing and evaluating it. Each stage produces information that is used in the next stage, so the process builds a clear chain of reasoning from problem to solution.

The process exists because good products rarely happen by accident. A structured method reduces the risk of wasting time and materials, helps the designer consider the needs of real users, and creates a record of decisions that can be reviewed later. In industry this record is essential for quality control, legal protection and future improvement. In your coursework, the design folio is exactly this record, and examiners reward folios where each page clearly leads to the next.

It is important to understand that the stages are not rigid boxes. Professional designers move backwards and forwards between them constantly. A test result might send the designer back to research, or a manufacturing problem might force a change to the specification. The process is therefore best thought of as a framework that guides thinking rather than a strict checklist.

  • Identify the need or problem (situation).
  • Write the design brief.
  • Research and analyse the problem.
  • Write the design specification.
  • Generate a range of initial ideas.
  • Develop and refine the chosen idea, including modelling.
  • Plan and make (realise) the product.
  • Test and evaluate against the specification.
Key fact:Key idea: every stage of the design process produces evidence that justifies the next decision.
03

Linear and Iterative Models

A linear design model presents the stages as a straight line: one stage is completed before the next begins, and the process ends when the product is made. Linear models are easy to understand and useful for planning a project timetable, which is why they often appear in textbooks. However, they suggest that the designer never returns to an earlier stage, which is unrealistic. If a problem is discovered late, a purely linear approach offers no obvious way to fix it.

An iterative model, often drawn as a circle or spiral, recognises that design is a cycle of proposing, testing and improving. The designer produces an idea or model, tests it, evaluates the result and then refines the idea before testing again. Each loop is called an iteration. The spiral version shows that each loop moves the design closer to the final solution. Iterative design is used widely in modern industry, particularly in product and software development, because it catches problems early when they are cheap to fix.

In the exam you may be asked to compare the two approaches. A strong answer states that linear models are simple and help with time planning, while iterative models are more realistic, allow continuous improvement and involve the user more often. Most real projects combine both: a linear timetable overall, with iterative loops inside the development stage.

Key fact:Iteration: repeating a cycle of design, model, test and evaluate, with each cycle improving the solution.
04

Identifying Needs and Writing a Design Brief

Design begins with a situation, which is a description of the context in which a problem exists. For example: "Elderly people living alone often struggle to open tight jar lids because of reduced grip strength." From the situation, the designer identifies a need, which is the specific problem that a product could solve. Needs can come from observing people, from client requests, from market gaps or from changes in technology and legislation.

The design brief is a short, clear statement of what the designer intends to produce. It usually names the product type, the user and the main purpose, without describing the final solution in detail. A good brief for the situation above would be: "Design and make a hand-held device that allows an elderly person with limited grip strength to open screw-top jars safely in the kitchen." Notice that it does not say what material or mechanism to use, leaving room for creative ideas.

A brief that is too vague, such as "Make something for the kitchen", gives no direction. A brief that is too narrow, such as "Make a 150 mm aluminium lever with a rubber grip", has already decided the solution and prevents exploration. Examiners look for briefs that identify the user, the purpose and the context in one or two sentences.

05

Research, Analysis and the Specification

Once the brief is written, the designer gathers information. Research may include studying existing products, measuring users (anthropometrics), interviewing the client, investigating materials and processes, and checking relevant safety standards. The aim is not to collect as much information as possible but to collect information that will influence the design. Every piece of research should be followed by a short conclusion explaining what it means for the product.

The results of research are summarised in a design specification, a numbered list of requirements the product must meet. Specification points should be specific and measurable wherever possible, for example "The device must weigh less than 250 g" rather than "The device must be light". Each point should also include a justification, which links it back to the research. A good specification typically covers function, user, aesthetics, size, materials, manufacture, safety, cost, environment and maintenance.

The specification is vital because it becomes the checklist used to evaluate ideas and the final product. If a specification point cannot be tested, it is of limited value. When writing specifications in the exam, always make each point measurable and give a reason.

Key fact:Brief = what you will design and for whom. Specification = the measurable requirements it must meet, with reasons.
06

Generating and Developing Ideas

With a specification in place, the designer produces a range of initial ideas. These are usually quick freehand sketches with annotations explaining how each idea works and how it meets the specification. Variety is important: ideas should differ in form, function, materials and mechanism, not just in surface decoration. Techniques such as brainstorming, mood boards, adapting existing products and SCAMPER help to generate genuinely different concepts.

Ideas are then evaluated against the specification, often using a table or ranking system, and the best idea or a combination of ideas is chosen for development. Development involves refining the shape, size, materials, construction methods and finish. Designers make models from card, foam, clay or 3D prints to test proportions, ergonomics and mechanisms. Each model is a test that produces evidence for the next improvement.

By the end of development, the designer should have a final design with enough detail for someone else to manufacture it. This usually includes a working drawing with dimensions, a cutting list or parts list, and details of materials, components and finishes.

07

Planning, Realisation and Testing

Before making begins, the designer produces a plan of manufacture. This lists the stages in order, the tools and equipment for each stage, the time needed, quality control checks and safety precautions. A flow chart is a common way to present the plan, using standard symbols for start, process, decision (quality check) and end. Planning ensures materials are not wasted and that difficult operations are done in a sensible order.

Realisation is the making stage. The designer follows the plan, records any changes and explains why they were needed. Quality control checks such as measuring against the working drawing, checking joints for squareness and testing moving parts are carried out at key points. In the coursework, photographs of the making process with brief notes provide useful evidence.

Once complete, the product is tested in its intended environment by the intended user. Testing should be objective where possible, for example measuring the force needed to open a jar with and without the device, rather than simply asking whether the user likes it.

  • Oval or rounded box: start or end of the process.
  • Rectangle: a process or operation.
  • Diamond: a decision or quality control check.
  • Arrow: direction of flow; a loop back shows rework after a failed check.
08

Evaluation and Stakeholders

Evaluation is a critical judgement of how well the product meets the specification and the needs of the user. It is not the same as a description of what was made. A good evaluation goes through the specification point by point, states whether each has been met, gives evidence from testing, and suggests specific modifications where a point has not been fully met. It should also include the views of the user or client and third-party opinions.

Stakeholders are all the people affected by a product. The client commissions and pays for the design, the user operates it, and the manufacturer makes it. Others include retailers, maintenance workers and even people affected by its disposal. Considering all stakeholders helps the designer avoid problems such as a product that pleases the client but is difficult for the user or expensive to manufacture.

Evaluation also happens throughout the process, not only at the end. Ideas are evaluated against the specification, models are evaluated after testing, and the manufacturing plan is evaluated as making proceeds. This ongoing evaluation is what makes the process iterative.

Key fact:Evaluation must refer back to the specification and use test evidence, then propose realistic improvements.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Design processA structured sequence of stages used to move from an identified need to a tested and evaluated product.
SituationA description of the context or circumstances in which a design problem exists.

IterationA repeated cycle of designing, modelling, testing and evaluating in which each cycle improves the design.

Worked Examples

Writing a brief and specification for a phone stand

A student notices that people watching recipe videos in the kitchen prop their phones against jars, which often fall over. Write a design brief and four justified specification points.

  1. Identify the user, purpose and context from the situation: home cooks who need to view a phone hands-free in a kitchen.
  2. Write the brief without fixing the solution: "Design and make a stable stand that holds a smartphone at a comfortable viewing angle on a kitchen worktop."
  3. Specification 1 (size): The stand must hold phones from 65 mm to 80 mm wide, because research shows most current smartphones fall within this range.
  4. Specification 2 (function): The viewing angle must be adjustable between 50° and 75° so the screen can be seen by users of different heights standing at the worktop.
  5. Specification 3 (materials): All surfaces must be water-resistant and wipeable, because the kitchen environment involves splashes and grease.
  6. Specification 4 (stability): The stand must not tip over when the screen is tapped with a force of 5 N, because users will touch the screen while cooking.
The brief names the user, purpose and context. Each specification point is measurable and justified by research, so it can later be used to evaluate ideas and the final product.

Using iteration to improve a design

A first card model of a jar-opener lever slips on smooth lids during testing. Explain how an iterative approach would deal with this.

  1. Record the test result objectively: the model slipped on 4 out of 5 jar lids tested.
  2. Evaluate against the specification point "must grip lids from 50 mm to 90 mm diameter without slipping"; this point is not met.
  3. Return to research: investigate high-friction materials such as thermoplastic rubber and textured surfaces used on existing products.
  4. Modify the design by adding a serrated inner edge and a rubber lining, then make a second model.
  5. Test the second model on the same five lids and compare results.
The second iteration gripped all five lids, so the specification point is now met. The loop of test, evaluate, modify and retest is iteration, and it solved the problem before expensive final manufacture.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the brief with the specification. The brief is a short statement of intent; the specification is a detailed list of measurable requirements.
  • Writing vague specification points such as "it must look nice" or "it must be cheap". Always give a measurable value and a reason, for example "cost of materials must be under $8 so it can retail at $20".
  • Treating the design process as strictly linear. Show that you understand feedback loops and that testing can send the designer back to earlier stages.
  • Writing a description instead of an evaluation. Evaluation must judge performance against the specification using evidence.
  • Forgetting the user. Many answers focus on the product's appearance but ignore who will use it and how.
  • Producing initial ideas that are all variations of one concept. Ideas should differ in form, mechanism and materials.

Exam tips

  • When asked to list stages of the design process, use the correct order and standard terms: brief, research, analysis, specification, ideas, development, planning, making, testing, evaluation.
  • For "explain" questions, give a point and then a reason linked with words such as "because" or "so that".
  • In specification questions, include a number and a unit in every point where possible; examiners reward measurability.
  • If asked to compare linear and iterative models, give one advantage and one disadvantage of each rather than only describing them.
  • In evaluation questions, refer to specific test results and suggest a specific, realistic modification.
  • Use the words client, user and stakeholder precisely; they are not interchangeable.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. State the purpose of a design brief.

Model answerTo give a short, clear statement of what is to be designed, for whom and for what purpose (1), providing direction without stating the final solution (1).
[4 marks]

2. Explain the difference between a design brief and a design specification.

Model answerA brief is a short general statement of the problem and intended product (1). A specification is a detailed list of requirements (1) that are measurable and justified by research (1), used to evaluate ideas and the final product (1).
[6 marks]

3. List, in order, six stages of the design process.

Model answerAny six in order: identify need, design brief, research, analysis/specification, generate ideas, development, planning, making/realisation, testing, evaluation. 1 mark per correct stage in logical order, maximum 6.
[2 marks]

4. What is meant by iteration in design?

Model answerRepeating a cycle of designing, modelling, testing and evaluating (1) where each cycle improves the design based on the results of the previous one (1).
[2 marks]

5. Give two advantages of an iterative design approach over a linear approach.

Model answerProblems are found early when cheaper to fix (1); the design is continually improved through testing (1); users can be involved and give feedback throughout (1). Any two.
[2 marks]

6. Rewrite this specification point so it is measurable: "The lamp must be a good size."

Model answerFor example: "The lamp must be no more than 400 mm tall and have a base no wider than 150 mm (1) so that it fits on a standard bedside table (1)." Credit a measurable value with units and a justification.
[3 marks]

7. Explain the difference between a client and a user, using an example.

Model answerThe client commissions and pays for the product (1); the user actually operates it (1). Example: a school (client) orders playground equipment used by pupils (users) (1).
[3 marks]

8. State three pieces of information that should appear in a plan of manufacture.

Model answerSequence of stages (1); tools and equipment for each stage (1); time for each stage (1); safety precautions (1); quality control checks (1). Any three.
[3 marks]

9. Explain why models are made during the development stage.

Model answerTo test proportions, size and ergonomics before final manufacture (1); to check that mechanisms or moving parts work (1); to show the client and get feedback (1); cheap materials reduce the cost of mistakes (1). Any three with explanation.
[4 marks]

10. Describe what should be included in a good evaluation of a finished product.

Model answerReference to each specification point (1); whether each point has been met (1); evidence from testing, including measurements (1); views of the user/client or third parties (1); specific suggested modifications with reasons (1); comment on manufacture/cost (1). Any four.
[6 marks]

11. A designer is asked to design a bird feeder for a primary school garden. Identify three stakeholders and explain one need of each.

Model answerPupils (users): must be easy to refill at their height (1+1). Teachers/school (client): must be safe, low cost and durable (1+1). Birds: must allow easy access and keep food dry (1+1). Accept caretaker (easy to clean/maintain) or manufacturer (easy to make). 2 marks per stakeholder with need.
[6 marks]

12. Discuss why the design process should be seen as a cycle rather than a straight line.

Model answerReal design involves feedback: testing and evaluation reveal problems that require returning to earlier stages (1). Research may change the specification after ideas are generated (1). Modelling and testing lead to repeated refinements (1). Client feedback may alter the brief (1). A cycle encourages continual improvement even after launch (1). However, a linear view is useful for time planning (1). Balanced conclusion that the process is iterative within an overall timeline (1). Maximum 6.

Quick Recap

  1. The design process is a structured method that moves from a need to a tested product.
  2. A situation describes the context; the need is the specific problem to be solved.
  3. The design brief states what will be designed, for whom and why, without fixing the solution.
  4. The specification is a list of measurable, justified requirements used to judge ideas and the final product.
  5. Iterative models loop through design, model, test and evaluate, improving the solution each time.
  6. Planning with flow charts, quality checks and safety notes makes manufacture efficient and accurate.
  7. Evaluation judges the product against every specification point using evidence and suggests improvements.
  8. Stakeholders include the client, user, manufacturer and wider community, and all their needs matter.
