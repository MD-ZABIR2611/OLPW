Introduction to Nutrition

An overview of what nutrition is, the functions of food, the main nutrients and their roles, water and dietary fibre, dietary reference values, food guides and the factors that influence what people choose to eat.

01

Overview

Nutrition is the study of food and how the body uses it for energy, growth, repair and the regulation of body processes. Every later topic in Food and Nutrition, from digestion to meal planning, builds on the ideas introduced here: the classes of nutrients, what each one does, and how much of each a person needs. Examiners regularly ask candidates to name nutrients, state their functions and sources, and explain why a balanced diet matters for health. A clear, confident grasp of this foundation makes every other chapter easier and allows you to write precise, well-organised answers under exam conditions.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Define the terms food, nutrition, nutrient, diet and balanced diet.
  • State the main functions of food in the body: energy, growth and repair, protection and regulation.
  • Classify nutrients as macronutrients or micronutrients and give examples of each.
  • Explain the importance of water and dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharide) in the diet.
  • Describe dietary reference values and explain how they are used to plan diets.
  • Use a food guide such as the Eatwell Guide to explain how a balanced diet is achieved.
  • Discuss the physiological, social, economic and cultural factors that affect food choice.
02

Food, Nutrition and Diet

Food is any solid or liquid that, when eaten and absorbed, provides the body with materials for energy, growth, repair and the regulation of body processes. Nutrition is the whole process by which the body takes in food and uses it, including ingestion, digestion, absorption, assimilation and the removal of waste. A nutrient is a chemical substance found in food that performs a specific job in the body. The word diet simply means the food and drink a person usually consumes; it does not necessarily mean a weight-loss plan. In the exam, precise definitions like these often earn one mark each, so learn them word for word.

A balanced diet is one that provides all the nutrients in the correct proportions and amounts to meet the needs of an individual, together with enough water and dietary fibre. The key idea is that needs differ between people. A growing teenage boy who plays football needs more energy and protein than a sedentary elderly woman, while a pregnant woman needs extra folate and iron. A diet that is balanced for one person may therefore be unbalanced for another. When an exam question asks you to plan a balanced meal, always consider the age, gender, activity level and any special needs of the person named.

Food also has important non-nutritional functions. It satisfies hunger and gives a feeling of fullness, known as satiety. It plays a social role at family meals, celebrations, religious festivals and when welcoming guests. It gives psychological comfort and pleasure through colour, flavour, aroma and texture. Recognising these functions helps to explain why people do not always choose the most nutritious foods, and it is useful material for longer questions about food choice.

  • Energy: for movement, maintaining body temperature and basal metabolism.
  • Growth and repair: building new cells and replacing damaged tissue.
  • Protection: supporting the immune system and protecting organs.
  • Regulation: controlling body processes such as heartbeat, nerve impulses and metabolism.
Key fact:Balanced diet = all nutrients in the correct proportions and amounts for the individual's needs, plus water and fibre.
03

The Classes of Nutrients

There are five main classes of nutrients: proteins, carbohydrates, fats (lipids), vitamins and minerals. Water and dietary fibre are not usually classed as nutrients in the strict sense because they do not provide energy or become part of body tissue in the same way, but both are essential and must be included whenever you discuss a balanced diet. Many foods contain several nutrients at once; milk, for example, supplies protein, fat, the sugar lactose, calcium, vitamin A and riboflavin.

Nutrients are grouped by the amounts needed. Macronutrients are needed in large amounts, measured in grams per day, and include protein, carbohydrate and fat. These three also provide energy. Micronutrients are needed in very small amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms, and include vitamins and minerals. Although the quantities are tiny, deficiencies of micronutrients cause serious diseases such as rickets, scurvy, goitre and anaemia. A useful way to remember the pattern is that macronutrients mainly provide energy and building materials, while micronutrients mainly protect and regulate.

Each nutrient has a main function, and exam questions frequently ask you to link nutrient, function and source. Protein is needed for growth and repair of cells and for making enzymes, hormones and antibodies. Carbohydrate is the main source of energy. Fat provides concentrated energy, insulation, protection of vital organs and the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. Vitamins help to regulate body processes and protect against disease. Minerals build bones and teeth and help to regulate body fluids and nerve and muscle function.

  • Protein – growth and repair – meat, fish, eggs, milk, pulses, soya.
  • Carbohydrate – energy – bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, cereals, sugar.
  • Fat – concentrated energy, insulation, fat-soluble vitamins – butter, oil, meat, nuts.
  • Vitamins – protection and regulation – fruit, vegetables, dairy, oily fish.
  • Minerals – bones, teeth, blood, fluid balance – milk, red meat, green vegetables, salt.
04

Water in the Diet

Water makes up about 60 per cent of the body mass of an adult and even more in babies. It is the medium in which all chemical reactions in cells take place. Water forms the basis of blood plasma, lymph, digestive juices, saliva, urine and sweat. It transports nutrients to cells and carries waste products away to be excreted. It regulates body temperature, because the evaporation of sweat from the skin removes heat. It also lubricates joints and the eyes, and it helps to keep the contents of the large intestine soft so that faeces can pass easily, preventing constipation.

The body loses water constantly through urine, faeces, sweat and breathing, so it must be replaced every day. Adults are generally advised to drink around 6 to 8 glasses, or about 1.5 to 2 litres, of fluid a day, with more needed in hot climates, during exercise, during illness with fever or diarrhoea, and when breastfeeding. Some water also comes from food, especially fruit and vegetables, and a small amount is produced by the body during respiration. Early signs of dehydration include thirst, headache, tiredness, poor concentration and dark-coloured urine. Severe dehydration, especially in babies with diarrhoea, can be fatal.

Key fact:Water: transport, temperature control, lubrication, medium for reactions, part of body fluids, prevents constipation.
05

Dietary Fibre (Non-Starch Polysaccharide)

Dietary fibre, also called non-starch polysaccharide (NSP), is the part of plant foods that cannot be digested by human enzymes. It is found in the cell walls of plants, mainly as cellulose, and is present in wholegrain cereals, wholemeal bread, brown rice, pulses, fruit and vegetables, especially with their skins left on. Animal foods such as meat, fish, eggs and milk contain no fibre at all. This is an important point because candidates sometimes wrongly list meat as a source of fibre.

Although fibre passes through the digestive system largely unchanged, it performs vital functions. It absorbs water and adds bulk to the faeces, which stimulates peristalsis, the wave-like muscular contractions that move food along the intestine. Soft, bulky faeces pass easily and quickly, which helps to prevent constipation, diverticular disease and haemorrhoids, and is linked to a lower risk of bowel cancer. Fibre-rich foods are filling, so they help to control appetite and body weight. Soluble fibre, found in oats, beans and fruit, can help to lower blood cholesterol and slow the absorption of sugar into the blood.

Adults in the UK are advised to eat about 30 g of fibre a day, but most people eat far less. Simple ways to increase intake include choosing wholemeal bread and wholegrain breakfast cereals, using brown rice and wholewheat pasta, adding pulses to stews and soups, leaving the skins on potatoes and eating more fruit and vegetables. When fibre intake is increased, fluid intake must also increase, because fibre needs water to work properly.

Key fact:Fibre adds bulk, absorbs water and stimulates peristalsis; found only in plant foods.
06

Energy and Dietary Reference Values

Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal), where 1 kcal is equal to 4.184 kJ, usually rounded to 4.2 kJ. The energy-providing nutrients yield different amounts per gram: carbohydrate about 16 kJ (3.75 kcal), protein about 17 kJ (4 kcal), fat about 37 kJ (9 kcal) and alcohol about 29 kJ (7 kcal). Fat therefore provides more than twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate or protein, which is why high-fat foods are described as energy-dense. Vitamins, minerals, water and fibre provide no energy.

Dietary Reference Values (DRVs) are estimates of the amounts of energy and nutrients needed by different groups of healthy people in a population. They include the Estimated Average Requirement (EAR), which meets the needs of about half of a group; the Reference Nutrient Intake (RNI), which is enough for about 97.5 per cent of people in a group and is therefore generous enough for almost everyone; and the Lower Reference Nutrient Intake (LRNI), which is enough for only a small number of people with low needs. Anyone regularly eating less than the LRNI is very likely to be deficient.

DRVs are used by dietitians, caterers, schools and food manufacturers to plan and assess diets for groups of people. They are not targets for individuals, because every person's needs vary. Energy requirements are usually given as an EAR, since eating too much energy leads to weight gain, whereas protein, vitamin and mineral values are usually given as RNIs. On food labels, simplified figures called Reference Intakes are used to show what percentage of an average adult's daily needs a portion provides.

  • EAR: meets the needs of about 50% of a group; used for energy.
  • RNI: meets the needs of about 97.5% of a group; used for protein, vitamins and minerals.
  • LRNI: meets the needs of only about 2.5% of a group; intakes below this are inadequate for most people.
Key fact:Energy per gram: fat 37 kJ, protein 17 kJ, carbohydrate 16 kJ, alcohol 29 kJ.
07

Food Groups and the Eatwell Guide

Food guides help people choose a balanced diet without needing to calculate nutrients. The UK Eatwell Guide is a plate-shaped diagram divided into five main food groups, with the size of each segment showing the proportion of the diet it should make up. Fruit and vegetables form just over a third of the plate, and at least five portions a day are recommended. Potatoes, bread, rice, pasta and other starchy carbohydrates form just over another third, with wholegrain or higher-fibre versions preferred.

The smaller groups are beans, pulses, fish, eggs, meat and other proteins, of which two portions of fish a week, including one of oily fish, are advised; dairy and alternatives, choosing lower-fat and lower-sugar options; and oils and spreads, which should be unsaturated and used in small amounts. Foods high in fat, salt and sugar, such as cakes, crisps, sweets and sugary drinks, are placed outside the plate because they are not needed in the diet and should be eaten less often and in small amounts. The guide also recommends 6 to 8 glasses of fluid a day.

Other countries use different models, such as food pyramids or the three food groups of energy-giving, body-building and protective foods, which are common in many tropical countries. Whatever model is used, the principles are the same: base meals on starchy carbohydrates, eat plenty of fruit and vegetables, include moderate amounts of protein foods and dairy, and limit fat, sugar and salt. Using a food guide is especially helpful when planning meals for a family with a limited budget.

Key fact:Eatwell: fruit and vegetables + starchy carbohydrates together make up about two-thirds of the diet.
08

Factors Affecting Food Choice

People choose food for many reasons besides nutrition. Physiological factors include hunger, appetite, age, health conditions, allergies and intolerances. Economic factors include household income, the price of food and the amount of money available for food after other expenses. Families on a low income tend to buy cheaper, energy-dense foods and may struggle to afford fresh fruit, vegetables and lean meat. Availability also matters: seasonal produce, local agriculture and access to shops all affect what can be bought.

Social and cultural factors are equally powerful. Religion influences diet: for example, Muslims eat halal meat and avoid pork and alcohol, many Hindus do not eat beef and many are vegetarian, Jewish people may follow kosher rules, and many Christians traditionally avoid meat on certain days. Family traditions, peer pressure, advertising and the media, lifestyle and working hours, cooking skills and kitchen equipment all shape food choices. Ethical and environmental concerns, such as animal welfare, Fairtrade, food miles and sustainability, lead some people to become vegetarian or vegan or to buy local and organic produce.

In extended exam answers about food choice, aim to cover a range of factors and explain each one rather than simply listing it. For example, instead of writing 'money', write that a family with a low income may buy cheaper processed foods that are high in fat and sugar, which could increase the risk of obesity. Linking each factor to its effect on the diet or health is what gains the higher marks.

09

Malnutrition

Malnutrition means an incorrect or unbalanced intake of nutrients, and it includes both undernutrition and overnutrition. Undernutrition occurs when the diet provides too little energy or too little of one or more nutrients. Examples include kwashiorkor and marasmus in young children, iron-deficiency anaemia, rickets caused by lack of vitamin D and calcium, and night blindness caused by lack of vitamin A. Undernutrition may result from poverty, famine, war, illness that affects absorption, or poor knowledge of nutrition.

Overnutrition occurs when the diet provides too much energy or too much of certain nutrients. Excess energy is stored as fat, leading to overweight and obesity, which increase the risk of type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure and some cancers. Too much salt raises blood pressure, and too much sugar causes dental caries. It is possible for a person to be overweight and yet deficient in micronutrients, for instance someone who eats large amounts of processed snack foods but little fruit and vegetables. This shows that malnutrition is about balance, not just quantity.

Key fact:Malnutrition = undernutrition OR overnutrition; an obese person can still be malnourished.

Worked Examples

Calculating energy from a food label

A 100 g serving of breakfast cereal contains 70 g carbohydrate, 10 g protein and 4 g fat. Calculate the energy it provides in kJ and in kcal, and state which nutrient contributes most energy.

  1. Carbohydrate: 70 g × 16 kJ = 1120 kJ; 70 g × 3.75 kcal = 262.5 kcal.
  2. Protein: 10 g × 17 kJ = 170 kJ; 10 g × 4 kcal = 40 kcal.
  3. Fat: 4 g × 37 kJ = 148 kJ; 4 g × 9 kcal = 36 kcal.
  4. Total: 1120 + 170 + 148 = 1438 kJ; 262.5 + 40 + 36 = 338.5 kcal.
  5. Carbohydrate supplies 1120 ÷ 1438 × 100 ≈ 78% of the energy.
The serving provides about 1438 kJ (about 339 kcal). Carbohydrate contributes most of the energy (about 78%), even though fat gives the most energy per gram, because far more carbohydrate is present.

Applying the Eatwell Guide to a meal

A student's lunch is a white bread sandwich filled with ham and butter, a packet of crisps and a can of cola. Using the Eatwell Guide, evaluate the meal and suggest improvements.

  1. Identify the groups present: starchy carbohydrate (white bread), protein (ham), oils and spreads (butter), and foods high in fat, salt and sugar (crisps and cola).
  2. Identify the groups missing: there are no fruit or vegetables and no dairy food.
  3. Identify nutritional problems: low in fibre, vitamin C and calcium; high in salt (ham, crisps), saturated fat (butter, crisps) and free sugar (cola).
  4. Suggest swaps: wholemeal bread for fibre, lean chicken or egg instead of ham to reduce salt, lower-fat spread, add salad in the sandwich, replace crisps with an apple and cola with water or semi-skimmed milk.
The meal is unbalanced: it lacks fruit, vegetables and dairy, is low in fibre and vitamin C, and is high in salt, fat and sugar. A wholemeal chicken salad sandwich with lower-fat spread, an apple and a glass of semi-skimmed milk would follow the Eatwell Guide much more closely.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Writing that fibre is a source of energy or is found in meat. Fibre comes only from plant foods and provides no usable energy to humans.
  • Defining a balanced diet as 'eating a bit of everything'. Always mention correct amounts and proportions to meet individual needs.
  • Confusing RNI and EAR. Remember that the RNI covers almost everyone (97.5%) and the EAR covers about half.
  • Stating that carbohydrate provides the most energy per gram. Fat provides the most, about 37 kJ per gram.
  • Assuming malnutrition only means not having enough food. Overnutrition, such as obesity, is also a form of malnutrition.
  • Listing factors affecting food choice without explaining how each factor changes what people eat.

Exam tips

  • When asked for a function of a nutrient, be specific: 'growth and repair of body cells' scores better than 'good for the body'.
  • Learn the energy values per gram of fat, protein and carbohydrate; calculation questions appear frequently and are easy marks.
  • For 'suggest improvements' questions, give a named swap and explain the nutritional benefit, such as 'wholemeal bread instead of white to increase fibre'.
  • In food choice questions, use headings in your mind (economic, social, cultural, religious, health, environmental) to make sure you cover a range.
  • Always name a specific food when asked for a source, for example 'lentils' or 'sardines', rather than a vague group such as 'vegetables'.
  • Show your working in calculations with units at each step so that you can earn method marks even if the final answer is wrong.

Quick Recap

  1. Nutrition is the process by which the body takes in and uses food for energy, growth, repair and regulation.
  2. The five classes of nutrients are protein, carbohydrate, fat, vitamins and minerals; water and fibre are also essential.
  3. Macronutrients are needed in grams and provide energy; micronutrients are needed in milligrams or micrograms and protect and regulate.
  4. Water transports substances, controls temperature and lubricates; fibre adds bulk and prevents constipation.
  5. Fat gives 37 kJ per gram, protein 17 kJ and carbohydrate 16 kJ.
  6. DRVs such as the EAR and RNI are used to plan and assess diets for groups of people.
  7. The Eatwell Guide shows the proportions of each food group needed for a balanced diet.
  8. Food choice is influenced by economic, social, cultural, religious, physiological and ethical factors, and malnutrition includes both under- and overnutrition.
