Introduction to Global Perspectives

An overview of what IGCSE Global Perspectives is, the skills it assesses, how the three components work, and how to think about issues from personal, local, national and global viewpoints.

01

Overview

Global Perspectives is different from most IGCSE subjects because it is built around skills rather than a fixed body of facts. You are assessed on how well you research, analyse, evaluate, reflect, collaborate and communicate when exploring real-world issues such as poverty, climate change or digital technology. Understanding the structure of the course, the assessment objectives and the idea of a 'perspective' from the very beginning will help you use every later chapter effectively. Students who grasp early that the examiner rewards reasoning, balance and evidence, not memorised content, consistently score higher across all three components.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain what Global Perspectives is and why it is described as a skills-based course.
  • Identify the six core skills: research, analysis, evaluation, reflection, collaboration and communication.
  • Describe the three assessment components and the broad weighting of each.
  • Distinguish between personal, local, national and global perspectives on an issue.
  • Break an issue down into causes, consequences and possible courses of action.
  • Recognise the main command words used in Global Perspectives questions and what each one requires.
02

What Is Global Perspectives?

Cambridge IGCSE Global Perspectives (0457) is a cross-curricular course that asks you to investigate issues that affect people all over the world. Instead of learning a textbook of facts, you learn how to find information, judge whether it is reliable, compare different viewpoints and reach your own supported conclusions. The topics are chosen from a list of global themes such as Belief systems, Digital world, Disease and health, Sustainable living, Trade and aid, Poverty and inequality, and Conflict and peace.

The course is designed to prepare you for the kind of thinking needed at A Level, at university and in work. Employers and universities value people who can weigh up arguments, work in teams, explain ideas clearly and reflect honestly on their own learning. Global Perspectives trains these habits deliberately, and it rewards them directly in the mark schemes.

Because the subject is skills-based, the same abilities are tested whatever topic you study. A student who can evaluate a source about water shortages can also evaluate a source about social media or migration. This means your revision should focus on practising the skills repeatedly with different material, rather than trying to memorise every statistic you meet.

Key fact:Global Perspectives rewards how you think: research, analysis, evaluation, reflection, collaboration and communication.
03

The Core Skills and Assessment Objectives

Cambridge groups the skills into three assessment objectives. AO1 covers research, analysis and evaluation: finding relevant information, identifying causes and consequences, and judging the quality of arguments and evidence. AO2 covers reflection: thinking about how your own views have been influenced by what you have learned and by working with others. AO3 covers communication and collaboration: presenting ideas clearly and working effectively in a team.

Analysis means breaking an issue into its parts, for example identifying who is affected, what the causes are and what the consequences might be. Evaluation goes a step further: it means judging how strong, reliable or convincing something is, and explaining why. Many students describe when they are asked to evaluate, so learning the difference between these skills is one of the quickest ways to gain marks.

Reflection is often the least familiar skill. It is not simply saying what you did; it means considering what you learned, how your perspective changed, what went well or badly in a team, and what you would do differently next time. Good reflection is honest, specific and forward-looking.

  • Research: locating and selecting relevant, reliable information.
  • Analysis: identifying issues, causes, consequences, perspectives and links.
  • Evaluation: judging the reliability of sources and the strength of arguments.
  • Reflection: considering the impact of learning and collaboration on your own views.
  • Collaboration: working as part of a team to achieve a shared goal.
  • Communication: presenting information and arguments clearly and in a structured way.
04

The Three Assessment Components

The qualification has three components. Component 1 is a written examination, worth about 35 per cent, in which you answer questions based on a set of sources provided in an insert. You are tested on your ability to identify information, explain issues and consequences, evaluate the strength of arguments and suggest or justify courses of action. You do not need specialist knowledge, but you must read the sources carefully.

Component 2 is the Individual Report, also worth about 35 per cent. You research a question of your own choice linked to one of the global topics and write a report of 1,500 to 2,000 words. The report must explore different perspectives, evaluate sources, present a reasoned conclusion and include reflection on how your own view has developed. It is marked externally by Cambridge.

Component 3 is the Team Project, worth about 30 per cent. You work with other students to research a local problem connected to a global issue, plan and carry out an outcome or action, and then produce individual work reflecting on the process. Because details such as timings and word limits can change between syllabus versions, always check the current syllabus document your school is using.

Key fact:Approximate weightings: Written Exam 35%, Individual Report 35%, Team Project 30%.
05

Understanding Perspectives

A perspective is a particular way of looking at an issue, shaped by a person's experiences, values, culture, beliefs, interests and circumstances. A farmer, a government minister and an environmental campaigner may all look at the same plan to build a dam and reach very different conclusions. None of these views is automatically right or wrong; each is influenced by what matters to that person.

In Global Perspectives you are expected to consider issues at different scales. A personal perspective reflects your own views and experiences. A local perspective considers your community, town or region. A national perspective considers a country as a whole, often including government policy. A global perspective considers how the issue affects people and places around the world and how countries interact over it.

Considering several perspectives makes your work more balanced and more convincing. For example, a report on plastic pollution that only gives the view of an environmental charity is one-sided. A stronger report would also consider the plastics industry, consumers who rely on cheap packaging, governments trying to balance jobs with environmental protection, and communities in countries that receive exported plastic waste.

  • Personal: How does this issue affect me, and what do I think?
  • Local: How does it affect my community or area?
  • National: How does it affect my country, and what is the government doing?
  • Global: How does it affect the world, and how do countries cooperate or disagree?
06

Issues, Causes, Consequences and Courses of Action

A useful framework for analysing any global topic is to ask four questions. What is the issue? What are its causes? What are its consequences? What courses of action could be taken to resolve it? This framework appears throughout the written exam and gives structure to the Individual Report and the Team Project.

Causes and consequences are rarely simple. An issue such as youth unemployment might have economic causes (a recession), social causes (lack of training), political causes (poor government policy) and technological causes (automation replacing jobs). Its consequences could include poverty, crime, poor mental health and migration. Showing that you can see these different dimensions demonstrates strong analysis.

A course of action is a proposed response to an issue, such as a new law, a campaign, a technology or a change in personal behaviour. In the exam you may be asked to suggest or evaluate one. A good answer explains how the action would work, who would carry it out, what its benefits and drawbacks are, and whether it is realistic given cost, time and public support.

Key fact:Framework: Issue → Causes → Consequences → Courses of action → Evaluation of those actions.
07

Command Words and What They Require

Command words tell you exactly what kind of thinking the examiner wants. 'Identify' or 'state' require a short, precise answer with no explanation. 'Explain' requires you to give a reason and develop it, usually using words such as because, therefore or this leads to. 'Evaluate' requires you to judge strengths and weaknesses and reach a conclusion. 'Justify' requires you to support a choice or view with reasons and evidence.

Marks usually indicate how much to write. A one-mark 'identify' question needs only a phrase, while an eight- or twelve-mark 'evaluate' question needs several developed points, consideration of both sides and a supported judgement. Spending too long on low-mark questions and rushing high-mark questions is a very common way to lose marks.

Train yourself to underline the command word and the focus of each question before you write. If a question asks you to evaluate the strength of an argument, it is not asking whether you agree with the argument. If it asks for a consequence, it is not asking for a cause. Answering the question actually set is the single most important exam skill.

  • Identify / State: name or give a fact briefly.
  • Describe: say what something is like or what happens.
  • Explain: give reasons, showing how or why.
  • Compare: identify similarities and differences.
  • Evaluate: judge strengths and weaknesses, then conclude.
  • Justify: support a decision or opinion with reasons and evidence.
08

How to Study Global Perspectives Successfully

Good preparation involves regular practice with unfamiliar sources. Read news articles, reports from organisations such as the United Nations or the World Bank, and opinion pieces, and ask yourself: Who wrote this? What is their purpose? What evidence do they use? What perspective does it represent? Doing this for a few minutes every day builds the evaluation habit quickly.

Keep a research log or learning journal throughout the course. Record sources you read, notes on how reliable they are, discussions with classmates and moments when your opinion changed. This material is invaluable later when you need to write reflection for the Individual Report and Team Project, because reflection must be specific rather than vague.

Finally, broaden your general awareness of world issues. Although the exam does not require specialist knowledge, understanding basic ideas such as sustainable development, human rights, globalisation and inequality will help you interpret sources confidently and suggest realistic courses of action.

Worked Examples

Identifying perspectives on a new motorway

A government plans to build a motorway through farmland to connect two cities. Identify four different perspectives on this plan and explain one reason for each.

  1. Identify stakeholders at different scales: a local farmer, a local business owner, the national government and an environmental organisation.
  2. Farmer (local, personal): likely to oppose the plan because land will be taken, reducing income and disrupting a family way of life.
  3. Business owner (local): may support it because faster transport brings more customers and cheaper deliveries.
  4. National government: likely to support it because better infrastructure boosts economic growth and trade between cities.
  5. Environmental organisation (global): may oppose it because more road traffic increases carbon emissions and habitat loss.
Four valid perspectives are the farmer (against, loss of land and income), the business owner (for, more customers), the national government (for, economic growth) and an environmental group (against, emissions and habitat loss). Each view is shaped by the interests of the person or group holding it.

Applying the issue-cause-consequence framework

Use the framework of issue, causes, consequences and courses of action to analyse the problem of food waste in wealthy countries.

  1. Issue: large amounts of edible food are thrown away by households, shops and restaurants in high-income countries.
  2. Causes: confusing 'best before' labels, supermarkets rejecting imperfect produce, over-buying due to promotions, and low awareness among consumers.
  3. Consequences: wasted money, greenhouse gas emissions from landfill, wasted water and land used to grow the food, and a moral issue when people elsewhere go hungry.
  4. Courses of action: clearer date labelling, laws requiring supermarkets to donate surplus food, education campaigns and apps that sell leftover food cheaply.
  5. Evaluate one action: donation laws (as introduced in France) redirect surplus to charities, but they need enforcement and good storage and transport networks to work.
Food waste is caused mainly by labelling confusion, retail standards and consumer habits; it wastes resources and increases emissions. A combination of clearer labels, education and supermarket donation laws is likely to be most effective because it tackles causes at both the household and business level.

Answering a command-word question

Explain one consequence of rising sea levels for coastal communities. [2 marks]

  1. Notice the command word 'explain' and the focus 'consequence', so you need an effect plus a developed reason.
  2. State the consequence: increased flooding of homes and farmland.
  3. Develop it: salt water damages crops and soil, so families lose income and may be forced to migrate inland.
Rising sea levels increase coastal flooding (1), which contaminates farmland with salt water, destroying crops and livelihoods and forcing some families to move away (1).

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Treating Global Perspectives like a content subject and memorising facts; instead practise the skills of analysis and evaluation with many different sources.
  • Describing a source or argument when the question says evaluate; always make a judgement about strength or reliability and explain why.
  • Presenting only one viewpoint; strong answers consider at least two contrasting perspectives, ideally at different scales.
  • Confusing causes with consequences; check whether the question asks why something happens or what results from it.
  • Writing vague reflection such as 'I learned a lot'; say specifically what you learned and how it changed your view.
  • Ignoring the marks available; a two-mark question does not need a full page, and a twelve-mark question needs developed, balanced points.

Exam tips

  • Underline the command word and the key focus of every question before you start writing.
  • Use linking phrases such as 'because', 'this means that' and 'as a result' to turn simple points into developed explanations.
  • Refer to personal, local, national and global scales to show breadth of perspective.
  • Keep a learning journal throughout the course so you have specific evidence for reflection later.
  • When suggesting a course of action, always explain how it works and consider at least one limitation.
  • Divide your time in the written exam roughly in proportion to the marks for each question.

Practice Questions

[3 marks]

1. Name the three assessment objectives in IGCSE Global Perspectives.

Model answerAO1 Research, analysis and evaluation (1); AO2 Reflection (1); AO3 Communication and collaboration (1).
[2 marks]

2. What is meant by a 'perspective'?

Model answerA particular point of view on an issue (1) shaped by a person's or group's values, experiences, beliefs or circumstances (1).
[3 marks]

3. State the three components of IGCSE Global Perspectives.

Model answerWritten examination (1); Individual Report (1); Team Project (1).
[3 marks]

4. Explain the difference between analysis and evaluation.

Model answerAnalysis is breaking an issue into parts such as causes, consequences and perspectives (1). Evaluation is judging the strength, reliability or effectiveness of something (1) and reaching a supported conclusion (1).
[2 marks]

5. Give one example of a local perspective and one example of a national perspective on the issue of plastic waste.

Model answerLocal: a town council concerned about litter on its beaches harming tourism (1). National: a government considering a nationwide ban on single-use plastic bags to meet environmental targets (1).
[4 marks]

6. Explain why considering different perspectives improves a piece of research.

Model answerIt makes the work balanced rather than biased (1) because it shows the issue affects groups differently (1); it helps identify weaknesses in one-sided arguments (1) and leads to a more reasoned, credible conclusion (1).
[2 marks]

7. Identify two possible causes of youth unemployment.

Model answerAny two, e.g. economic recession reducing jobs (1); lack of skills or training (1); automation replacing workers (1); poor education system (1).
[4 marks]

8. Explain two consequences of youth unemployment for a country.

Model answerLower tax revenue and higher welfare spending (1) because fewer people are earning (1). Increased social problems such as crime or poor mental health (1) because young people lack income and purpose (1). Other valid: emigration of talent (brain drain) with development.
[2 marks]

9. What does the command word 'justify' require you to do?

Model answerSupport a decision, choice or opinion (1) with clear reasons and evidence (1).
[4 marks]

10. Explain what is meant by reflection in Global Perspectives and why it is valued.

Model answerReflection is thinking about what you have learned and how your views or skills have changed (1), including the influence of research and teamwork (1). It is valued because it helps you understand your own biases (1) and improve future work (1).
[6 marks]

11. A school wants to reduce the amount of energy it uses. Suggest a course of action and evaluate how effective it might be.

Model answerCourse of action: install motion-sensor lights and run an awareness campaign (1). How it works: lights switch off automatically in empty rooms and students learn to turn off devices (1). Strengths: cuts wasted electricity and bills (1); low running costs after installation (1). Limitations: upfront cost of sensors (1); campaigns may lose impact over time (1). Judgement: likely to be effective if combined with regular monitoring of energy use (1). Up to 6 marks for developed, balanced evaluation.
[8 marks]

12. 'Global issues are too big for individuals to make any difference.' Discuss this view with reference to different perspectives.

Model answerAgree: global issues such as climate change are caused by billions of people and large industries, so one person's actions are tiny (1-2); governments and international agreements have more power (1). Disagree: individual choices add up when many people change behaviour, e.g. reducing plastic use (1-2); individuals can influence governments through voting, campaigns and consumer pressure (1-2); local actions can inspire national change (1). Reference to personal, local, national and global perspectives (1-2). Supported conclusion, e.g. individuals matter most when their actions combine with collective and government action (1-2). Maximum 8 marks.

Quick Recap

  1. Global Perspectives is a skills-based course focusing on research, analysis, evaluation, reflection, collaboration and communication.
  2. The three assessment objectives are research, analysis and evaluation (AO1), reflection (AO2), and communication and collaboration (AO3).
  3. Assessment consists of a written exam, an Individual Report and a Team Project, weighted roughly 35%, 35% and 30%.
  4. A perspective is a viewpoint shaped by values, experiences and circumstances, and issues can be viewed at personal, local, national and global scales.
  5. Analysing issues through causes, consequences and courses of action gives structure to all your work.
  6. Command words such as identify, explain and evaluate tell you exactly what type of answer to give.
  7. Regular practice with unfamiliar sources and a learning journal are the best preparation for all components.
