Colour Theory

How colours are organised, mixed and combined, and how artists use colour relationships, temperature and symbolism to create harmony, contrast, depth and mood.

01

Overview

Colour is one of the most powerful tools available to an artist, yet it is also one of the easiest to misuse. Colour theory gives you a practical framework for mixing colours accurately, choosing combinations that work together and predicting the emotional effect of your choices. In the IGCSE and O-Level Art and Design components, confident colour handling is a clear sign of control of media under AO2, and discussing an artist's colour choices with correct terminology strengthens your critical analysis under AO1 and AO3. This chapter covers the colour wheel, colour properties, harmonies, temperature, optical effects and the expressive use of colour by real artists.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Construct a colour wheel showing primary, secondary and tertiary colours.
  • Define hue, tint, shade, tone, value and saturation, and mix each accurately.
  • Identify and use complementary, analogous, triadic and monochromatic colour schemes.
  • Explain colour temperature and how warm and cool colours affect space and mood.
  • Describe optical effects such as simultaneous contrast and optical mixing.
  • Analyse how artists such as Monet, Seurat, Matisse and Rothko use colour expressively.
02

The Colour Wheel

The traditional artist's colour wheel, used for mixing paint, is based on three primary colours: red, yellow and blue. Primaries cannot be mixed from other colours. Mixing two primaries produces a secondary colour: red and yellow make orange, yellow and blue make green, and blue and red make violet or purple. Mixing a primary with a neighbouring secondary produces a tertiary colour, such as yellow-green, blue-green, blue-violet, red-violet, red-orange and yellow-orange. Arranged in a circle, these twelve hues form the standard colour wheel.

In reality, no single set of three paints produces perfectly clean mixtures, because every pigment has a bias towards a neighbouring hue. Many artists therefore use a split-primary palette with a warm and a cool version of each primary, such as cadmium red and alizarin crimson, cadmium yellow and lemon yellow, and ultramarine and phthalo or cerulean blue. To mix a clean violet, you would choose a red and a blue that both lean towards violet, such as alizarin crimson and ultramarine, rather than an orange-leaning cadmium red.

It is also helpful to know that printers and screens work on different systems. Printing uses the subtractive primaries cyan, magenta and yellow plus black (CMYK), while screens use additive light primaries red, green and blue (RGB). This matters if you work with photography or digital media, where colour on screen may look different when printed.

  • Primary: red, yellow, blue (cannot be mixed).
  • Secondary: orange, green, violet (two primaries mixed).
  • Tertiary: a primary mixed with an adjacent secondary, such as blue-green.
  • Split-primary palette: a warm and a cool version of each primary for cleaner mixing.
Key fact:Mixing two colours that sit far apart on the wheel produces duller, browner mixtures.
03

Properties of Colour

Every colour can be described by three properties. Hue is the name of the colour family, such as red or blue-green. Value, also called tone, is how light or dark a colour is. Saturation, also called intensity or chroma, is how pure or vivid it is. A fire-engine red is highly saturated, while a brick red is less saturated. Being able to change each property separately is the key to sophisticated colour work.

Adding white to a hue creates a tint, which is lighter and often softer, such as pink from red. Adding black creates a shade, which is darker, although black can make colours look dirty, so many painters darken colours by adding a complementary or a darker neighbouring hue instead. Adding grey, or the complementary colour, creates a tone, which reduces saturation and produces subtle, muted colours. A value scale of a single hue, running from tint to shade, is an excellent sketchbook exercise.

Understanding value is crucial because the eye reads value before hue. A painting that has a strong range of values will read clearly even when colours are unusual, whereas one where all colours have similar values will look flat. A quick test is to photograph your work and convert it to greyscale on a phone; if the image loses its structure, you need more tonal contrast.

Key fact:Tint = hue + white; shade = hue + black; tone = hue + grey (or its complement).
04

Colour Harmonies and Schemes

Colour harmonies are combinations that are known to work well together. Complementary colours sit opposite each other on the wheel, such as red and green, blue and orange, and yellow and violet. Placed side by side, they create maximum contrast and make each other appear more intense; mixed together, they neutralise each other into greys and browns. A split-complementary scheme uses one colour plus the two neighbours of its complement, giving contrast with less tension.

Analogous colours are neighbours on the wheel, such as yellow, yellow-green and green. They create calm, unified harmony and are common in nature, for example in autumn leaves or seascapes. A triadic scheme uses three colours evenly spaced around the wheel, such as the three primaries, giving a lively, balanced contrast. A monochromatic scheme uses tints, tones and shades of a single hue and is excellent for developing tonal control and creating a strong, unified mood.

Picasso's Blue Period paintings, such as The Old Guitarist (1903-04), use an almost monochromatic blue palette to express poverty, loneliness and melancholy. By contrast, the Fauves, including André Derain in paintings such as Charing Cross Bridge (1906), used intense complementary contrasts in unnatural combinations to express energy and emotion rather than describe the scene accurately.

  • Complementary: opposites, for high contrast and vibrancy.
  • Analogous: neighbours, for harmony and calm.
  • Triadic: three evenly spaced hues, for balanced liveliness.
  • Monochromatic: one hue in many values, for unity and mood.
05

Colour Temperature, Space and Mood

Colours are often described as warm or cool. Reds, oranges and yellows are associated with fire and sunlight and appear warm; blues, greens and violets are associated with water, sky and shadow and appear cool. Temperature is also relative: a lemon yellow looks cool beside a cadmium yellow, and an ultramarine looks warmer than a cerulean blue. Skilled painters exploit these subtle shifts constantly.

Warm colours tend to advance towards the viewer, while cool colours recede. Landscape painters use this to create depth, placing warm, saturated colours in the foreground and cooler, paler colours in the distance, which combines colour temperature with atmospheric perspective. Many painters also use warm light and cool shadows, or the reverse, to give form a lively, convincing glow rather than simply darkening colours with black.

Colour also carries psychological and cultural associations. Red can suggest passion, danger or celebration; blue can suggest calm, sadness or authority. These meanings vary across cultures: white is associated with weddings in many Western countries but with mourning in parts of South and East Asia. Mark Rothko's large colour-field paintings, such as the dark maroons and blacks of the Seagram murals (1958-59), aimed to produce deep emotional and even spiritual responses through colour alone.

Key fact:Warm colours advance; cool colours recede.
06

Optical Colour Effects

Colours are never seen in isolation; each changes according to what surrounds it. This effect, called simultaneous contrast, was studied by the French chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul in the nineteenth century. A grey square looks slightly reddish on a green background and slightly greenish on a red one. Josef Albers explored this systematically in his Homage to the Square series and his book Interaction of Color (1963), showing that the same colour can look completely different depending on its neighbours.

Optical mixing occurs when small dots or strokes of separate colours are placed close together so that the eye blends them from a distance. Georges Seurat developed this into Pointillism in A Sunday Afternoon on the Island of La Grande Jatte (1884-86), building the painting from thousands of small dots of pure colour. The Impressionists, such as Claude Monet, used broken colour, placing unmixed strokes side by side to capture flickering light, as in his Water Lilies and Haystacks series.

Afterimages are another optical effect: stare at a bright red shape for thirty seconds and then look at a white wall, and you will see a green ghost image. Op artists like Bridget Riley used carefully controlled colour and pattern to create sensations of movement and vibration on a flat surface.

07

Practical Colour Mixing Techniques

Good colour mixing begins with a clean, organised palette. Lay out colours in the same order every time, usually following the colour wheel with white at one end, and keep a large clear area for mixing. Always add a small amount of a strong or dark colour to a larger amount of a weaker or light colour, rather than the other way round; a speck of phthalo blue can overwhelm a pool of white, whereas the reverse wastes paint and time.

Colour-matching exercises develop accuracy. Choose a small area of a photograph or natural object, mix a swatch and place it beside the source, then adjust hue, value and saturation one at a time. Ask yourself three questions: is it the right colour family, is it too light or too dark, and is it too bright or too dull? Recording successful mixes with the paint names in your sketchbook creates a useful reference and provides evidence of experimentation.

Try making a colour wheel, a tint-and-shade scale, a set of complementary neutral greys, and a painted still life restricted to a limited palette of three colours plus white. Limited palettes force you to mix rather than use colours straight from the tube, and they usually create more harmonious paintings because every colour is related.

  • Mix dark into light, strong into weak, in small increments.
  • Clean your brush between colours to avoid muddy mixes.
  • Test mixes on scrap paper and let them dry, as many paints change value when dry.
  • Label successful mixes with pigment names and proportions.

09

Worked Examples

Analysing colour in Monet's Impression, Sunrise (1872)

Explain how Claude Monet uses colour in Impression, Sunrise to capture light and atmosphere. (6 marks)

  1. Context: painted in 1872 at the port of Le Havre; its title gave the Impressionist movement its name when it was exhibited in 1874.
  2. Identify the dominant palette: cool, misty blues, greys and blue-greens across the harbour and sky.
  3. Identify the contrast: a small, intense orange sun and its broken reflection on the water, which is complementary to the surrounding blues.
  4. Explain the effect: the complementary contrast makes the sun appear to glow and vibrate even though, in greyscale, its value is almost identical to the sky.
  5. Technique: loose, broken brushstrokes of unblended colour suggest the flicker of light and the movement of water and mist.
  6. Conclude: Monet prioritises a fleeting impression of light and atmosphere over precise detail.
Monet sets a small, saturated orange sun against a cool, hazy blue-grey harbour. The complementary contrast makes the sun appear to glow, while loose, broken strokes of unblended colour capture the shimmering reflections and misty early-morning atmosphere, prioritising the effect of light over detail.

Mixing a monochromatic value scale

Produce a nine-step monochromatic scale in acrylic or gouache using ultramarine blue, white and a dark neutral, and explain its use.

  1. Draw nine equal boxes in a row, labelling the centre box as the pure hue.
  2. Paint the centre box with ultramarine straight from the tube.
  3. For tints, begin with a pool of white and add tiny amounts of ultramarine, painting boxes from the lightest (far left) towards the centre, checking each step is evenly spaced.
  4. For shades, add small amounts of a dark neutral mixed from ultramarine and burnt umber, rather than pure black, to keep the colour rich, painting towards the darkest box on the right.
  5. Let the scale dry and check it with half-closed eyes or a greyscale photo, repainting any step that jumps too much.
  6. Annotate the sketchbook page, explaining how this scale will be used to model form in a later painting.
The finished scale shows nine evenly graded values from a pale blue tint through pure ultramarine to a deep, rich shade, demonstrating control of value and providing a reference for painting three-dimensional form in a monochromatic scheme.

Comparing expressive colour: Matisse and Picasso

Compare the use of colour in Matisse's The Joy of Life (1905-06) and Picasso's The Old Guitarist (1903-04). (8 marks)

  1. Matisse: bright, saturated, unnatural colours including pinks, oranges, yellows and greens in flat areas, typical of Fauvism.
  2. Effect of Matisse's colour: creates a joyful, sensual, idyllic mood; colour is freed from describing reality.
  3. Picasso: an almost monochromatic palette of blues and blue-greens, with the figure's skin tinted a cold grey-blue.
  4. Effect of Picasso's colour: creates a cold, melancholy mood expressing poverty, blindness and isolation.
  5. Comparison: both artists use colour expressively rather than naturalistically, but Matisse uses contrast and warmth for joy while Picasso uses a cool, restricted harmony for sorrow.
Both artists use colour to express emotion rather than to copy nature. Matisse's saturated, warm and contrasting colours create exuberance and pleasure, whereas Picasso's restricted, cool blue monochrome creates a sombre, lonely atmosphere, showing how a palette choice can completely change the mood of a figurative painting.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Darkening every colour with black, which makes paintings look dull and dirty. Try adding a complementary or deeper related hue instead.
  • Mixing too many colours together, producing mud. Limit each mix to two or three pigments and clean brushes frequently.
  • Using colour straight from the tube without mixing, which looks crude and unrelated. Modify each colour to suit the light and mood.
  • Confusing tint, tone and shade. Remember white for tint, grey for tone and black for shade.
  • Ignoring value when choosing colours, leading to flat images. Check your work in greyscale.
  • Describing colour vaguely as 'nice' or 'bright'. Use terms such as saturated, muted, complementary, harmonious and warm.

Exam tips

  • When analysing an artwork, identify the colour scheme by name, then explain the mood or effect it creates.
  • Include colour-mixing experiments and swatches with annotation in your supporting studies to evidence AO2.
  • Use a limited palette for exam work; it saves time, creates harmony and demonstrates mixing skill.
  • Place the strongest complementary contrast at your focal point to draw the viewer's eye.
  • Make small colour studies before committing to a large final piece, testing at least two alternative palettes.
  • Refer to colour temperature when explaining how you created depth, for example 'cool blues push the hills back'.

3. Define tint, shade and tone.

Model answerTint is a hue mixed with white (1). Shade is a hue mixed with black or a darker colour (1). Tone is a hue mixed with grey or its complement, reducing saturation (1).

4. Explain what is meant by an analogous colour scheme and give one example.

Model answerColours next to each other on the colour wheel (1), which create harmony (1). Example: yellow, yellow-orange and orange, or blue, blue-green and green (1).
[3 marks]

5. Why do complementary colours appear more vivid when placed side by side?

Model answerThey are opposite on the colour wheel so they create maximum contrast (1). Through simultaneous contrast each colour intensifies the other (1), for example orange looks brighter next to blue (1).
[4 marks]

6. Describe how warm and cool colours can be used to create depth in a landscape.

Model answerWarm colours appear to advance so they can be used in the foreground (1). Cool colours appear to recede so they are used in the background (1). Distant areas are also made paler and less saturated, linking with atmospheric perspective (1), creating a convincing illusion of space (1).
[3 marks]

7. What is Pointillism and which artist developed it?

Model answerA technique of applying small dots of pure, unmixed colour (1) which blend optically in the viewer's eye from a distance (1). Developed by Georges Seurat, with Paul Signac (1).
[4 marks]

8. Explain why many painters prefer a split-primary palette.

Model answerEach pigment has a colour bias towards a neighbouring hue (1). Having a warm and a cool version of each primary allows the painter to choose two colours that both lean towards the desired secondary (1), giving cleaner, brighter mixes (1) and avoiding dull or muddy results (1).

Model answerRed: passion, anger, danger, luck or celebration in Chinese culture (1-2). Blue: calm, sadness, trust or authority (1-2). Associations differ between cultures, for example white as mourning in parts of Asia (1), so artists must consider audience to communicate intended meaning (1).

10. Explain how Josef Albers demonstrated the relative nature of colour.

Model answerIn Homage to the Square and Interaction of Color (1) Albers placed squares of colour within one another (1), showing that the same colour looks different depending on surrounding colours (1), proving colour perception is relative rather than fixed (1).

Quick Recap

  1. The painter's colour wheel is built from red, yellow and blue primaries, their secondaries and six tertiaries.
  2. Every colour has hue, value and saturation, which can be changed independently.
  3. Tints add white, shades add black or a darker hue, and tones add grey or a complement.
  4. Complementary, analogous, triadic and monochromatic schemes each create a different balance of harmony and contrast.
  5. Warm colours advance and cool colours recede, which helps create depth and mood.
  6. Simultaneous contrast and optical mixing show that colour perception depends on context.
  7. Artists such as Monet, Seurat, Matisse, Picasso and Rothko used colour to express light and emotion.
  8. Clean, careful mixing with a limited palette produces harmonious, controlled results.
