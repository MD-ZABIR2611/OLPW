Argumentative and Discursive Mastery

The difference between a competent essay and a top-band one is the quality of thinking. This chapter covers structuring arguments, handling counter-claims, evaluating evidence and closing with genuine judgement.

Key Concepts

01

A thesis is the clear, arguable position stated in the introduction; every paragraph should advance, qualify or defend it, so the essay reads as one sustained case.

02

Discursive essays explore an issue from multiple angles and may end undecided; argumentative essays defend a position — in both, each paragraph opens with a topic sentence linking back to the question.

03

A counter-argument anticipates the strongest opposing view, states it fairly, then rebuts it; conceding a limited truth before rebutting (concession) shows sophisticated thinking and earns evaluation credit.

04

Arguments are strengthened by evidence — statistics, examples, expert authority, anecdotes — and weakened by generalisation, false analogy, attacking the person rather than the argument (ad hominem), and false dilemmas.

05

Logical connectives (however, consequently, admittedly, on the other hand) signpost the relationships between ideas, making the reasoning easy to follow and the structure visible to the examiner.

06

A conclusion should weigh the arguments presented, reach a judgement tied to the precise wording of the question, and avoid introducing new material.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
ThesisThe central argument or position of an essay, stated clearly in the introduction and developed throughout.
Counter-argumentThe strongest opposing point to a position, presented and then answered within the essay.
RebuttalThe reasoning or evidence used to answer and overturn a counter-argument.
ConcessionAcknowledging a limited truth in the opposing view before arguing that it does not outweigh your case.
GeneralisationA sweeping claim made from too little evidence, such as "everyone knows", which weakens an argument.
Ad hominemAn attack on the person making an argument rather than on the argument itself, a common logical fallacy.
False dilemmaPresenting only two options when more exist, e.g. "either we ban it or society collapses", to force a choice.
Topic sentenceThe opening sentence of a paragraph stating its single main idea and linking it to the essay question.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Plan arguments as a two-column list: your points down the left, the strongest objection to each down the right. Writing the rebuttal before the point ensures your essay argues rather than merely asserts — examiners reward answers that engage the other side.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

What is a counter-argument, and why should an argumentative essay include one?

Model answerA counter-argument is the strongest opposing point to your position, presented fairly within your essay (1). Including one shows you have considered the issue fully, and rebutting it strengthens your own case and earns evaluation marks (1).
CHALLENGE

Discuss the view that "exams are the fairest way to assess students". Construct a balanced argument ending with a justified judgement.

Model answerFor: exams give every candidate identical conditions, test knowledge under pressure, and are hard to bias, so they are objective and comparable across the country (1). Against: they reward memory and speed over deep understanding, disadvantage anxious or ill students on a single day, and ignore coursework, creativity and collaboration — so other methods may measure ability more fairly (1). Judgement: exams are fair for comparing large groups but should carry less than total weight, combined with moderated coursework to capture abilities a single paper cannot (1).
