Chapter 18 · Food and Nutrition

Food Labelling, Storage and Legislation

The label is a legal document as well as a sales tool: date marks, ingredient lists, additives and nutrition tables are all controlled by food law designed to protect and inform consumers.

Key Concepts

01

Pre-packed food must show the name, ingredients in descending weight order, allergens emphasised, net quantity, date mark, storage conditions, the business name and address, and a nutrition declaration per 100 g or per portion.

02

Date marks mean different things: use-by dates are safety limits on highly perishable food such as cooked meat and soft cheese, while best-before dates are quality guides on foods such as tinned beans and dried pasta that remain safe after the date.

03

E-numbers identify permitted additives by function — colours (E100–199), preservatives (E200–299), antioxidants (E300–399) and emulsifiers or stabilisers (E400–499) — each assessed as safe at set limits before it may be used.

04

Packaging protects food from physical damage, moisture, oxygen and light, extends shelf life, carries information and supports marketing; materials such as glass, metals, plastics and laminates are chosen for barrier properties, cost and recyclability.

05

Weights and measures law requires pre-packed goods to meet stated average-quantity tolerances, and describing food falsely is an offence, enforced by trading standards officers who can prosecute misleading labels.

06

Safety duties extend through the supply chain: HACCP-based hazard control in manufacture, temperature control during transport and retail, allergen disclosure and traceability so any batch can be identified and recalled.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Use-by dateThe safety deadline on highly perishable foods; food must not be consumed or sold after this date.
Best-before dateA quality guide on longer-life foods indicating when the product may begin to lose flavour or texture but remain safe.
E-numberA code for a food additive approved for use within the European system, grouped by function such as colours, preservatives and emulsifiers.
Food additiveA substance added to food to preserve it, improve flavour, colour or texture, or aid processing, such as preservatives and emulsifiers.
Nutrition declarationThe mandatory table of energy and nutrient values per 100 g or per portion shown on pre-packed food labels.
Allergen labellingThe legal requirement to declare major allergens such as nuts, milk, egg, gluten and shellfish clearly within the ingredient list.
TraceabilityThe ability to follow a food through all production and distribution stages so that a batch can be identified and recalled.
HACCPHazard Analysis and Critical Control Points: a preventive system that identifies hazards in food production and monitors the points where they must be controlled.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Learn date marks as a comparison: use-by means safety, do not use after; best-before means quality, may be safe but past its best; vocabulary questions repeatedly test that single distinction, one mark for each side.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

State the difference between a use-by date and a best-before date, giving one example food for each.

Model answerUse-by is a safety deadline on highly perishable food, so it must not be used or sold after that date (1). Best-before is a quality guide on longer-life foods, which may remain safe after the date but lose flavour or texture (1). Example of a use-by food, e.g. cooked sliced meat or soft cheese (1). Example of a best-before food, e.g. tinned beans or dried pasta (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate the benefits and drawbacks of food additives and E-numbers for consumers and manufacturers, reaching a judgement.

Model answerBenefits: preservatives such as the E200 series extend shelf life and reduce food poisoning from moulds and bacteria (1). Colours, flavours and emulsifiers improve appearance, taste and texture, standardising quality and reducing waste (1). Antioxidants stop fats turning rancid, protecting flavour and nutrients (1). Drawbacks: some people are sensitive to particular additives, e.g. certain colourings linked to hyperactivity in children (1). Additive-heavy foods are often ultra-processed and high in fat, sugar or salt, so heavy reliance may worsen diet quality (1). Conclusion: within regulated limits additives are safe and bring real benefits, but they cannot rescue a poor-quality product, so clear labelling and informed choice matter most (1).
