Sources and Recording of Data

Covers the business documents that provide evidence of transactions, the books of prime entry in which they are first recorded, and how totals are posted to the divisions of the ledger.

01

Overview

Before a transaction can be entered in the ledger it must first be supported by a document and recorded in a book of prime entry. Invoices, credit notes, receipts and bank statements provide the evidence, while the sales, purchases and returns journals, the cash book and the general journal organise transactions of the same type in one place. Examiners frequently give you a source document and ask which book of prime entry it is recorded in, or ask you to write up a journal and post it to the ledger. Understanding this flow of information, from document to book of prime entry to ledger, is essential for every later topic, including control accounts and the correction of errors.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Identify the main business documents and explain the purpose of each.
  • Distinguish between trade discount and cash discount and show how each is treated.
  • Name the books of prime entry and state which transactions are recorded in each.
  • Write up the sales, purchases and returns journals from source documents.
  • Post the totals and individual entries from books of prime entry to the ledger.
  • Describe the divisions of the ledger and the accounts kept in each.
  • Use the general journal for non-routine transactions, including a narrative.
02

Source Documents

A source document is the original record of a transaction and provides evidence that it took place. Source documents are important because they give the details needed for recording (date, names, quantities, prices and amounts) and provide an audit trail that allows entries to be checked later. Businesses keep copies of documents they send out and file documents they receive.

The invoice is the most common source document. When goods are sold on credit, the seller sends a sales invoice to the buyer, stating what was supplied, the price, any trade discount and the amount owed. To the buyer, the same document is a purchase invoice. If goods are returned or were overcharged, the seller issues a credit note, which reduces the amount the customer owes. A debit note may be sent by a buyer to request a credit note, but it is not itself entered in the books.

At the end of each month the seller usually sends a statement of account to each credit customer, summarising invoices, credit notes and payments and showing the balance outstanding. A receipt is written evidence that money has been received. For bank transactions the business uses cheque counterfoils (stubs) as evidence of payments, paying-in slips as evidence of money deposited, and the bank statement to check its own cash book records.

  • Sales invoice: evidence of a credit sale; recorded in the sales journal.
  • Purchase invoice: evidence of a credit purchase; recorded in the purchases journal.
  • Credit note sent: evidence of goods returned by a customer; recorded in the sales returns journal.
  • Credit note received: evidence of goods returned to a supplier; recorded in the purchases returns journal.
  • Receipts, cheque counterfoils and paying-in slips: recorded in the cash book.
  • Petty cash vouchers: recorded in the petty cash book.
03

Trade Discount and Cash Discount

Trade discount is a reduction in the list price given by a supplier to another business, usually because it buys in bulk or is in the same trade. It is deducted on the invoice itself, and only the net amount after trade discount is recorded in the books. Trade discount never appears in any ledger account. For example, goods listed at $2,000 with 25% trade discount are recorded at $1,500.

Cash discount is an allowance given to a credit customer to encourage prompt payment within a stated period, for example '2% if paid within 14 days'. Because it depends on how quickly the customer pays, it is not known when the invoice is issued and is recorded only when payment is made. Cash discount allowed to customers is an expense of the seller; cash discount received from suppliers is income of the buyer. Both are recorded in the discount columns of the cash book and then transferred to discount allowed and discount received accounts in the nominal ledger.

If both discounts apply, trade discount is deducted first and cash discount is calculated on the net invoice amount. This ordering is a common source of examination errors.

Key fact:Trade discount: deducted on the invoice, never recorded. Cash discount: given for prompt payment, recorded in the cash book.
04

Books of Prime Entry

Books of prime entry (also called books of original entry) are where transactions are first recorded before being posted to the ledger. Grouping similar transactions together reduces the number of entries needed in the ledger, because the totals of a journal can be posted once a month rather than posting every item to the nominal ledger individually. It also divides the work so that different clerks can deal with different types of transaction.

There are seven books of prime entry in the syllabus. The sales journal records credit sales of goods. The purchases journal records credit purchases of goods for resale. The sales returns journal (returns inwards journal) records goods returned by customers. The purchases returns journal (returns outwards journal) records goods returned to suppliers. The cash book records all receipts and payments by cash and cheque. The petty cash book records small cash payments. The general journal records any transaction that does not belong in one of the other books.

Only credit transactions in goods for resale go in the sales and purchases journals. Cash sales go in the cash book, and the credit purchase of a non-current asset, such as a delivery van, goes in the general journal because the van is not bought for resale.

05

Writing Up the Sales and Purchases Journals

The sales journal is a simple list with columns for the date, the customer's name, the invoice number, a folio reference and the net amount. Each entry is the invoice total after trade discount. At the end of the month the column is totalled. The purchases journal has the same layout, listing suppliers and purchase invoice amounts.

Posting the sales journal follows a clear pattern. Each individual invoice is debited to the customer's personal account in the sales ledger, because the customer now owes the business money. The total of the journal is credited to the sales account in the nominal ledger, because the business has earned revenue. The purchases journal is posted the opposite way: each supplier's account in the purchases ledger is credited, and the total is debited to the purchases account.

The returns journals reverse these effects. The sales returns journal total is debited to the sales returns account, and each customer's account is credited. The purchases returns journal total is credited to the purchases returns account, and each supplier's account is debited.

  • Sales journal: debit each customer (sales ledger); credit sales account with the total.
  • Purchases journal: credit each supplier (purchases ledger); debit purchases account with the total.
  • Sales returns journal: credit each customer; debit sales returns account with the total.
  • Purchases returns journal: debit each supplier; credit purchases returns account with the total.
06

The Divisions of the Ledger

The ledger is the main book of account containing all the individual accounts of the business. In a business of any size it is divided into sections so that different staff can work on them and errors are easier to trace. The sales ledger contains the personal accounts of credit customers (trade receivables). The purchases ledger contains the personal accounts of credit suppliers (trade payables).

The nominal ledger, also called the general ledger, contains all other accounts. These include accounts for income and expenses, such as sales, purchases, rent, wages and discount allowed, and accounts for assets and liabilities, such as premises, motor vehicles and loans. Some businesses keep a private ledger for confidential accounts such as capital and drawings, but in examinations these are usually treated as part of the nominal ledger.

The cash book is both a book of prime entry and part of the ledger, because it contains the cash account and bank account themselves. Entries in the cash book are posted to the opposite side of the relevant personal or nominal account, completing the double entry.

  • Sales ledger: personal accounts of credit customers.
  • Purchases ledger: personal accounts of credit suppliers.
  • Nominal (general) ledger: income, expense, asset, liability, capital and drawings accounts.
  • Cash book: cash and bank accounts (a book of prime entry and part of the ledger).
07

The General Journal

The general journal records transactions that are not recorded in any other book of prime entry. Typical uses include opening entries when a business starts, the purchase or sale of non-current assets on credit, the correction of errors, writing off irrecoverable debts, year-end transfers to the income statement, and adjustments such as depreciation. Because these transactions are unusual, the journal provides a formal record and authorisation before the ledger is changed.

Each journal entry shows the date, the account to be debited (written first), the account to be credited (written below and slightly indented), the amounts in separate debit and credit columns, and a narrative. The narrative is a short explanation of why the entry is being made, for example 'Purchase of motor van on credit from Speedy Motors'. Examiners award marks for narratives, so do not leave them out unless the question specifically says they are not required.

The general journal helps to prevent fraud and errors because entries are documented and can be checked by a supervisor. It also provides a clear record that can be traced if questions arise later.

Key fact:Journal entry order: date, debit account and amount, credit account and amount, narrative.
08

The Flow of Information

The complete recording process follows a logical sequence. A transaction occurs and a source document is produced. The document is recorded in the appropriate book of prime entry. The entries are then posted to the ledger accounts using double entry. At the end of a period the ledger accounts are balanced and the balances are listed in a trial balance, which is then used to prepare the financial statements.

Following this sequence means every figure in the financial statements can be traced back to an original document, which is known as an audit trail. Folio references, the columns that record where an entry has been posted, make this trail easy to follow. For example, SL4 might mean page 4 of the sales ledger and GL12 page 12 of the general ledger.

  • Transaction → source document → book of prime entry → ledger → trial balance → financial statements.

Key Terms

InvoiceA document sent by a seller to a buyer giving details of goods sold on credit and the amount owed.

Worked Examples

Trade and cash discount on an invoice

On 3 May, Kaya Traders sold goods with a list price of $4,000 to Bello Stores, allowing 20% trade discount. Terms: 2.5% cash discount if paid within 10 days. Bello paid on 10 May. Calculate the invoice amount, the cash discount and the amount paid, and state the entries in Kaya's books.

  1. Step 1: Trade discount = 20% × $4,000 = $800. Invoice amount = $4,000 − $800 = $3,200. This is recorded in the sales journal.
  2. Step 2: Post the invoice: debit Bello Stores (sales ledger) $3,200; credit Sales account $3,200 (through the sales journal total).
  3. Step 3: Cash discount = 2.5% × $3,200 = $80. Amount paid = $3,200 − $80 = $3,120.
  4. Step 4: On 10 May, debit Bank $3,120 and debit Discount allowed $80; credit Bello Stores $3,200. Bello's account now has a nil balance.
Invoice $3,200; cash discount allowed $80; amount received $3,120. The $800 trade discount is never recorded in the ledger.

Writing up and posting the sales journal

During June, Patel & Co sold goods on credit: 4 June, A. Khan, list price $1,500 less 10% trade discount; 12 June, B. Lee, $980 net; 21 June, C. Mensah, list price $2,000 less 15% trade discount. Write up the sales journal and show the ledger postings.

  1. Step 1: Calculate net amounts. A. Khan: $1,500 − $150 = $1,350. B. Lee: $980. C. Mensah: $2,000 − $300 = $1,700.
  2. Step 2: Sales journal: 4 June A. Khan $1,350; 12 June B. Lee $980; 21 June C. Mensah $1,700. Total for June = $4,030.
  3. Step 3: Post individual invoices to the debit side of each customer's account in the sales ledger: A. Khan Dr $1,350; B. Lee Dr $980; C. Mensah Dr $1,700.
  4. Step 4: Post the total to the credit side of the Sales account in the nominal ledger: 30 June Cr 'Sundry receivables per sales journal' $4,030.
Sales journal total $4,030 is credited to Sales; the three customer accounts are debited with $1,350, $980 and $1,700, so total debits equal total credits.

Journal entry for a non-current asset bought on credit

On 15 July, Ahmed bought a delivery van costing $18,500 on credit from Fleet Motors. Prepare the general journal entry.

  1. Step 1: Identify the accounts. The van is a non-current asset, so Motor vehicles account increases (debit). Fleet Motors is now owed money, so its account increases as a liability (credit).
  2. Step 2: Record: 15 July — Dr Motor vehicles $18,500; Cr Fleet Motors $18,500.
  3. Step 3: Write the narrative: 'Purchase of delivery van on credit from Fleet Motors.'
Dr Motor vehicles $18,500, Cr Fleet Motors $18,500, with narrative. It is not entered in the purchases journal because the van is not goods for resale.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Recording trade discount in the ledger. Only the net amount after trade discount is recorded; trade discount never has its own account.
  • Calculating cash discount on the list price. Cash discount is calculated on the invoice amount after trade discount.
  • Entering the credit purchase of a non-current asset in the purchases journal. The purchases journal is only for goods for resale; use the general journal.
  • Recording cash sales in the sales journal. Cash sales go straight into the cash book.
  • Posting the sales journal total to the debit of the sales account. Sales is income and is credited; individual customers are debited.
  • Omitting narratives in general journal entries. Unless told otherwise, always include a brief narrative.

Exam tips

  • Learn a simple table matching each source document to its book of prime entry; this is tested almost every year in short questions.
  • When posting journals, remember the customer or supplier gets the individual entry and the nominal account gets the total.
  • Use the details column in ledger accounts to name the other account involved in the double entry, for example 'Sales' or 'Bank'.
  • Show discount calculations separately before the ledger entries to secure method marks.
  • If asked for the advantages of books of prime entry, mention fewer ledger entries, division of work and an audit trail.
  • Check which business's books you are recording in: a credit note is recorded in the sales returns journal by the seller but in the purchases returns journal by the buyer.

Practice Questions

[1 mark]

1. State the book of prime entry in which a copy of a sales invoice would be recorded.

Model answerSales journal (1).
[2 marks]

2. Distinguish between trade discount and cash discount.

Model answerTrade discount is a reduction in list price given to trade customers, deducted on the invoice and not recorded in the ledger (1). Cash discount is given to encourage prompt payment and is recorded in the cash book and ledger when payment is made (1).
[3 marks]

3. Name the source document that would be used to record each of the following: (a) goods returned by a customer; (b) money paid into the bank; (c) a small payment for postage.

Model answer(a) Credit note (copy sent) (1). (b) Paying-in slip (counterfoil) (1). (c) Petty cash voucher (1).
[4 marks]

4. Explain two advantages of using books of prime entry.

Model answerThey reduce the number of entries in the ledger because only totals are posted to nominal accounts (1)(1). They allow division of work so different staff can record different types of transaction (1)(1). Also accept: provide an audit trail of all transactions of one type.
[3 marks]

5. Goods with a list price of $6,000 are sold with 25% trade discount and 3% cash discount for payment within 7 days. The customer pays within 7 days. Calculate the amount received.

Model answerTrade discount $1,500; invoice amount $4,500 (1). Cash discount 3% × $4,500 = $135 (1). Amount received $4,500 − $135 = $4,365 (1).
[4 marks]

6. State which division of the ledger would contain each account: (a) Rent; (b) T. Noor, a credit customer; (c) Fixtures and fittings; (d) Harbour Supplies, a credit supplier.

Model answer(a) Nominal/general ledger (1). (b) Sales ledger (1). (c) Nominal/general ledger (1). (d) Purchases ledger (1).
[2 marks]

7. What is a narrative and why is it included in a journal entry?

Model answerA short written explanation below the journal entry (1) describing the reason for the transaction so it can be understood and checked later (1).
[2 marks]

8. Explain why a debit note is not recorded in the books of account.

Model answerA debit note is only a request for a credit note from the supplier (1). The transaction is recorded only when the supplier issues a credit note, which is the evidence that the amount owed has been reduced (1).
[4 marks]

9. The purchases journal of Zara Ltd for March shows: 5 March, Pine Ltd $840; 17 March, Oak Supplies $1,260; 28 March, Pine Ltd $500. Show how these would be posted to the ledger.

Model answerPurchases account (nominal ledger) debited with the total $2,600 (1)(1). Pine Ltd (purchases ledger) credited $840 and $500 (1). Oak Supplies (purchases ledger) credited $1,260 (1).
[7 marks]

10. Prepare journal entries, with narratives, for: (a) 1 Jan, opening entry: premises $80,000, equipment $12,000, bank $5,000, loan $30,000; (b) 9 Jan, office computer bought on credit from TechWorld $1,800.

Model answer(a) Dr Premises $80,000 (1), Dr Equipment $12,000, Dr Bank $5,000 (1); Cr Loan $30,000 (1), Cr Capital $67,000 (1); narrative 'Assets, liabilities and capital at start of business' (1). (b) Dr Office equipment $1,800, Cr TechWorld $1,800 (1); narrative 'Computer bought on credit' (1).
[6 marks]

11. Describe the flow of information from a transaction to the financial statements.

Model answerA transaction takes place and a source document is prepared (1). It is recorded in a book of prime entry (1). Entries are posted to ledger accounts using double entry (1). Accounts are balanced and a trial balance extracted (1). The financial statements are prepared from the trial balance (1). This provides an audit trail (1).
[6 marks]

12. A business owner says: 'I do not need source documents because I remember all my transactions.' Evaluate this view.

Model answerSource documents provide evidence that a transaction took place (1) and give accurate details such as dates, quantities and prices (1). They create an audit trail allowing entries to be checked and errors traced (1). They are required by tax authorities and auditors (1). They help prevent fraud and settle disputes with customers and suppliers (1). Memory is unreliable as the number of transactions grows (1). Conclusion: the owner's view is not sensible; documents are essential (1). Max 6.

14

Quick Recap

  1. Source documents such as invoices, credit notes, receipts and bank statements provide evidence of transactions.
  2. Trade discount is deducted on the invoice and never recorded; cash discount is recorded in the cash book when payment is made.
  3. The seven books of prime entry are the sales, purchases, sales returns and purchases returns journals, the cash book, the petty cash book and the general journal.
  4. Journal totals are posted to nominal ledger accounts while individual items are posted to personal accounts.
  5. The ledger is divided into the sales ledger, purchases ledger and nominal (general) ledger.
  6. The general journal records non-routine items such as opening entries, credit purchases of non-current assets and corrections, each with a narrative.
  7. Information flows from source document to book of prime entry to ledger to trial balance to financial statements.
