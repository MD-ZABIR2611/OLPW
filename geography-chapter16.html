Ecosystems & Natural Vegetation in Depth

Natural vegetation reflects climate, and each major ecosystem — equatorial rainforest, hot desert, savanna and boreal forest — shows distinctive adaptations that human activity is now threatening.

Key Concepts

01

An ecosystem is a natural unit in which living organisms interact with each other and with the non-living environment of climate, soil and water; energy enters as sunlight and passes along food chains from producers (plants) to consumers and decomposers, and a biome is a world-scale ecosystem such as tropical rainforest or desert.

02

Tropical rainforest (equatorial climate, over 1 800 mm of rain a year, no dry season, about 26-28°C all year) has a layered structure with emergents reaching 40-60 m, a continuous canopy, an undergrowth of shrubs, plus lianas and epiphytes; its biodiversity is the highest on Earth and nutrient cycling is rapid, with nutrients stored in the vegetation rather than the poor soils — so clearing it for logging, ranching or shifting cultivation destroys its fertility, as in the Amazon and Borneo.

03

Hot desert vegetation (under about 250 mm of unreliable rain) is sparse xerophytes with drought adaptations: deep or widespread shallow roots, small or no leaves, water-storing succulent stems, waxy or hairy skins, and seeds that lie dormant and germinate fast after rare rain, e.g. cacti and acacia.

04

Savanna (tropical grassland) is warm all year with a wet season and a four-to-seven-month dry season: tall grasses up to 3-4 m with scattered fire- and drought-resistant trees such as baobab and acacia; it supports vast herds of grazers such as zebra and wildebeest, and is maintained by seasonal fires, grazing and the seasonal rainfall.

05

Coniferous forest (taiga or boreal forest) forms a circumpolar belt across Canada, Scandinavia and Russia with cold winters and short summers; needle-like leaves reduce water loss and shed snow, the conical shape sheds snow, and the softwood timber feeds construction and paper industries.

06

Human activity — deforestation, overgrazing, farming, urban growth and climate change — destroys habitats, reduces biodiversity and can turn semi-arid land to desert (desertification); responses include national parks, sustainable logging, replanting, agroforestry and wildlife conservation.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
EcosystemA natural system in which living organisms interact with each other and with non-living components such as climate and soil.
Food chainThe transfer of energy through a sequence of organisms, from producers (plants) to consumers and decomposers.
XerophyteA plant adapted to survive in dry conditions, e.g. by storing water, having small leaves or deep roots.
EpiphyteA plant that grows on another plant for support rather than nutrients, common in rainforest canopies.
Drip-tip leafA pointed rainforest leaf that sheds heavy rainwater quickly, preventing rotting and algae growth.
BiodiversityThe variety of plant and animal species found in an area; tropical rainforests have the highest biodiversity on Earth.
DesertificationThe spread of desert-like conditions into dry semi-arid land, caused mainly by overgrazing, deforestation and poor farming.
Boreal forest (taiga)The great coniferous forest belt of the high latitudes of Canada, Scandinavia and Russia, with cold winters and short summers.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Adaptation questions demand linked pairs: each adaptation must be tied to the climate condition it beats, such as drip-tips shedding heavy rain or deep roots reaching groundwater, and quoting temperature and rainfall figures for the biome earns the first marks.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Describe how plants in the hot desert are adapted to survive drought.

Model answerSmall, needle-like or no leaves reduce the surface from which water is lost by transpiration (1). Thick, waxy or hairy skins (cuticles) reduce evaporation from the plant surface (1). Succulent stems or leaves store water for long dry periods, e.g. cacti (1). Long tap roots reach deep groundwater or widespread shallow roots absorb rare rainfall quickly, and seeds lie dormant until rain falls (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate the effects of deforestation on the tropical rainforest ecosystem.

Model answerCutting trees destroys habitats, so biodiversity falls and some species face extinction as food chains are broken (1). With no interception and roots to hold it, soil is eroded by heavy rain and leached of nutrients, silting rivers and degrading the land, making recovery slow (1). Deforestation releases stored carbon dioxide, adding to climate change, which can reduce rainfall and stress remaining forest (1). Local and national economies gain timber, farmland and jobs, which is why deforestation continues (1). Overall the short-term economic gains are outweighed by permanent ecological losses, which is why sustainable logging and replanting are recommended (1).
