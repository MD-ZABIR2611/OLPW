Double-Entry Bookkeeping

Explains the dual aspect of transactions, the debit and credit rules for each type of account, how to record common transactions in ledger accounts and how to balance accounts at the end of a period.

01

Overview

Double-entry bookkeeping is the heart of accounting. Every transaction affects two accounts: one account is debited and another is credited with the same amount. This simple rule, used by businesses for over five hundred years, keeps the accounting equation in balance and allows arithmetical accuracy to be checked through a trial balance. In the examination you will be asked to record transactions in ledger accounts, balance them and explain the entries. Almost every question in the paper assumes you can decide instantly which account to debit and which to credit, so this chapter deserves repeated practice until the rules become automatic.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain the dual aspect concept and why every transaction has two entries.
  • Apply the rules of debit and credit to assets, liabilities, capital, income, expenses and drawings.
  • Record cash and credit transactions, including returns and drawings, in ledger accounts.
  • Distinguish between personal and impersonal (real and nominal) accounts.
  • Balance ledger accounts and bring down balances correctly.
  • Interpret the meaning of a debit or credit balance on a ledger account.
02

The Dual Aspect of Transactions

Every business transaction has two aspects. When a business buys equipment by cheque, it gains an asset (equipment) and gives up another asset (money in the bank). When it buys goods on credit, it gains inventory and takes on a liability to the supplier. Double-entry bookkeeping records both aspects: one account receives a debit entry and another account receives a credit entry of exactly the same amount.

Because every debit has a matching credit, the total of all debit entries in the ledger must equal the total of all credit entries. This is what allows a trial balance to be prepared as a check on arithmetical accuracy. It also means the accounting equation stays in balance, since each transaction either changes two items on the same side of the equation or changes both sides equally.

Ledger accounts are traditionally drawn as T-accounts. The left-hand side is the debit side (Dr) and the right-hand side is the credit side (Cr). Each entry shows the date, the details (the name of the other account involved) and the amount. Writing the name of the other account in the details column is what makes the double entry traceable.

Key fact:Every debit entry must have an equal and opposite credit entry.
03

The Rules of Debit and Credit

The rules can be summarised by the type of account. Assets, expenses and drawings increase on the debit side and decrease on the credit side. Liabilities, capital and income increase on the credit side and decrease on the debit side. A popular memory aid is DEAD CLIC: Debit to increase Expenses, Assets and Drawings; Credit to increase Liabilities, Income and Capital.

These rules follow from the accounting equation. Assets are on the left of Assets = Capital + Liabilities, so they increase on the left (debit). Capital and liabilities are on the right, so they increase on the right (credit). Income increases profit and therefore capital, so it is credited. Expenses and drawings reduce capital, so they are debited.

A useful practical approach is to ask two questions for each transaction: which accounts are affected, and is each account increasing or decreasing? Once you know the type of each account, the rule tells you the side. For example, paying wages by cheque: Wages is an expense that increases (debit); Bank is an asset that decreases (credit).

  • Debit to increase: Assets, Expenses, Drawings (and purchases, sales returns).
  • Credit to increase: Liabilities, Capital, Income (and sales, purchases returns).
  • To decrease any account, enter the amount on the opposite side.
04

Recording Common Transactions

When goods for resale are bought, they are debited to the purchases account, not to an inventory account. Inventory is only recorded at the end of the year when it is counted. If the goods are bought for cash, credit cash or bank; if bought on credit, credit the supplier's account. When goods are sold, credit the sales account and debit cash, bank or the customer's account.

Returns are kept in separate accounts so that the owner can monitor them. Goods returned by customers (returns inwards) are debited to sales returns and credited to the customer. Goods returned to suppliers (returns outwards) are credited to purchases returns and debited to the supplier. Keeping returns separate gives better information than simply reducing sales or purchases.

Drawings may be cash or goods. Cash drawings are debited to drawings and credited to cash or bank. When the owner takes goods for personal use, debit drawings and credit purchases, because the goods were originally debited to purchases and are no longer available for resale. Capital introduced is debited to the asset received and credited to capital.

  • Credit purchase of goods: Dr Purchases, Cr Supplier.
  • Cash sale: Dr Cash, Cr Sales.
  • Credit sale: Dr Customer, Cr Sales.
  • Returns inwards: Dr Sales returns, Cr Customer.
  • Returns outwards: Dr Supplier, Cr Purchases returns.
  • Paid supplier by cheque: Dr Supplier, Cr Bank.
  • Received cheque from customer: Dr Bank, Cr Customer.
  • Owner takes goods: Dr Drawings, Cr Purchases.
05

Types of Account

Accounts can be classified as personal or impersonal. Personal accounts record transactions with people or organisations, such as credit customers (trade receivables), credit suppliers (trade payables) and the owner's capital account. Impersonal accounts are divided into real accounts and nominal accounts.

Real accounts record assets that the business owns, such as premises, machinery, motor vehicles, inventory and cash. Nominal accounts record income and expenses, such as sales, purchases, rent, wages, insurance, discount allowed and commission received. At the end of the year nominal account balances are transferred to the income statement, while real and personal account balances are carried forward to the next period and appear in the statement of financial position.

Understanding these categories helps you to decide where accounts are kept (sales ledger, purchases ledger or nominal ledger) and how their balances are treated at the year end.

06

Carriage and Other Expenses

Carriage is the cost of transporting goods. Carriage inwards is the cost of delivering goods purchased into the business. It is added to the cost of purchases in the trading section of the income statement because it is part of the cost of getting goods ready for sale. Carriage outwards is the cost of delivering goods to customers and is treated as a selling expense in the profit and loss section.

Both are debited to their own expense accounts and credited to cash or bank when paid. Keeping them separate is important because they are treated differently in the income statement. Other expenses such as rent, electricity, insurance, advertising and wages follow the same pattern: debit the expense account and credit the bank, cash or the person owed.

Income other than sales, such as rent received from subletting part of the premises or commission received, is credited to its own income account and debited to cash or bank.

Key fact:Carriage inwards: added to cost of sales. Carriage outwards: an expense in profit and loss.
07

Balancing Ledger Accounts

At the end of a period, usually a month or a year, each account is balanced to find the net amount. First, add up both sides. Second, find the difference and write it on the side with the smaller total as 'Balance c/d' (carried down). Third, total both sides, which will now be equal, and underline the totals on the same line. Finally, bring the balance down on the opposite side, dated the first day of the next period, as 'Balance b/d' (brought down).

The balance brought down tells you the nature of the account. A debit balance on a customer's account means the customer still owes money, a trade receivable. A credit balance on a supplier's account means the business still owes the supplier, a trade payable. A debit balance on the bank account means money is in the bank; a credit balance means an overdraft.

If an account has only one entry, it is not necessary to show a total; the single figure is itself the balance. If both sides are equal, the account is closed with the totals and no balance is brought down.

  • Add both sides and find the difference.
  • Enter the difference as Balance c/d on the smaller side.
  • Total both sides on the same line.
  • Bring down the balance on the opposite side as Balance b/d on the first day of the next period.
08

Interpreting Balances

Asset, expense and drawings accounts normally have debit balances. Liability, capital and income accounts normally have credit balances. When you meet an account with an unusual balance, think about what it means. A customer's account with a credit balance means the customer has overpaid or returned goods after paying, so the business owes them money. A bank account with a credit balance is an overdraft, a current liability.

Being able to interpret balances is tested in short questions such as 'Explain what the balance on 1 April on J. Singh's account represents.' A good answer names the type of balance and its meaning, for example 'A debit balance of $450, meaning J. Singh owes the business $450; J. Singh is a trade receivable.'

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Double entryThe system of recording every transaction as a debit in one account and an equal credit in another.
DebitThe left-hand side of a ledger account; used to record increases in assets, expenses and drawings.
CreditThe right-hand side of a ledger account; used to record increases in liabilities, capital and income.
Ledger accountA record of all transactions relating to one item, such as a customer, supplier, asset, expense or income.
Personal accountAn account for a person or organisation, such as a credit customer, credit supplier or the owner's capital.
Real accountAn account for an asset owned by the business, such as premises or motor vehicles.
Nominal accountAn account for an item of income or expense, such as sales, rent or wages.
PurchasesGoods bought for resale in the normal course of business.
Returns inwardsGoods returned to the business by customers; also called sales returns.
Returns outwardsGoods returned by the business to suppliers; also called purchases returns.
Carriage inwardsThe cost of transporting purchased goods into the business; added to cost of sales.
Carriage outwardsThe cost of delivering goods to customers; treated as an expense in the profit and loss section.
Balance c/dThe balancing figure entered on the smaller side of an account at the end of a period to make both sides equal.
Balance b/dThe balance brought down on the opposite side at the start of the next period, showing the net amount on the account.

Worked Examples

Recording a month of transactions

Nadia started a business on 1 April. Record the following in ledger accounts: 1 April, paid $20,000 into a business bank account as capital; 3 April, bought goods on credit from Kent Ltd $3,500; 6 April, sold goods for cash $900; 10 April, sold goods on credit to R. Das $1,600; 14 April, returned goods to Kent Ltd $300; 18 April, R. Das returned goods $200; 25 April, paid Kent Ltd $3,200 by cheque; 28 April, Nadia withdrew $500 cash for personal use.

  1. 1 April: Dr Bank $20,000; Cr Capital $20,000 (asset up, capital up).
  2. 3 April: Dr Purchases $3,500; Cr Kent Ltd $3,500 (expense up, liability up). 6 April: Dr Cash $900; Cr Sales $900.
  3. 10 April: Dr R. Das $1,600; Cr Sales $1,600. 14 April: Dr Kent Ltd $300; Cr Purchases returns $300.
  4. 18 April: Dr Sales returns $200; Cr R. Das $200. 25 April: Dr Kent Ltd $3,200; Cr Bank $3,200.
  5. 28 April: Dr Drawings $500; Cr Cash $500.
  6. Check Kent Ltd: credits $3,500; debits $300 + $3,200 = $3,500, so the account is closed with no balance. R. Das: debit $1,600 − credit $200 = debit balance $1,400.
Kent Ltd has been fully paid (nil balance); R. Das owes $1,400 (debit balance b/d on 1 May); bank has a debit balance of $16,800; cash has a debit balance of $400.

Balancing a customer's account

T. Owusu's account in the sales ledger shows: debits 1 May balance b/d $620, 8 May sales $1,450, 22 May sales $780; credits 12 May sales returns $150, 20 May bank $1,900. Balance the account at 31 May.

  1. Step 1: Total debits = $620 + $1,450 + $780 = $2,850.
  2. Step 2: Total credits = $150 + $1,900 = $2,050.
  3. Step 3: Difference = $2,850 − $2,050 = $800. Enter 31 May 'Balance c/d $800' on the credit side (the smaller side).
  4. Step 4: Both sides now total $2,850; write the totals on the same line and underline them.
  5. Step 5: Bring down 1 June 'Balance b/d $800' on the debit side.
Debit balance of $800 brought down on 1 June: T. Owusu owes the business $800 and is a trade receivable.

Goods taken by the owner and carriage

On 5 June, the owner Faisal took goods costing $250 from the business for his family. On 9 June he paid carriage inwards $120 and carriage outwards $80 in cash. State the double entry for each.

  1. Step 1: Goods taken for personal use reduce the goods available for resale and reduce capital. Dr Drawings $250; Cr Purchases $250.
  2. Step 2: Carriage inwards is an expense of buying goods. Dr Carriage inwards $120; Cr Cash $120.
  3. Step 3: Carriage outwards is a selling expense. Dr Carriage outwards $80; Cr Cash $80.
Dr Drawings $250 / Cr Purchases $250; Dr Carriage inwards $120 / Cr Cash $120; Dr Carriage outwards $80 / Cr Cash $80.
11

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Debiting an inventory account when goods for resale are bought. Use the purchases account; inventory is only recorded at the year end.
  • Crediting sales returns. Returns inwards reduce income, so the sales returns account is debited.
  • Crediting drawings when goods are taken by the owner. Drawings is always debited; purchases is credited.
  • Writing 'Balance b/d' on the same side as 'Balance c/d'. The balance is carried down on one side and brought down on the opposite side.
  • Writing the name of the account itself in the details column. The details column should show the other account in the double entry.
  • Treating carriage inwards as a selling expense. Carriage inwards belongs in cost of sales; carriage outwards is a profit and loss expense.

Exam tips

  • For every transaction, write down the two accounts, their types and whether each increases or decreases before deciding debit or credit.
  • Learn DEAD CLIC so that you can apply the rules quickly under time pressure.
  • Always date ledger entries and put the balance b/d on the first day of the next period.
  • Rule off totals on the same horizontal line even if one side has fewer entries.
  • When asked to explain a balance, state whether it is debit or credit, give the amount and say what it represents.
  • Check your work: after recording, the total of all debits you entered should equal the total of all credits.

Model answerDebit side (1).

2. State the double entry for goods bought on credit from Z. Hamid for $750.

Model answer(a) Dr Rent $600, Cr Bank $600 (1). (b) Dr Cash $150, Cr Commission received $150 (1). (c) Dr L. Ng $90, Cr Purchases returns $90 (1).

4. Distinguish between a real account and a nominal account, giving one example of each.

Model answerA real account records an asset, e.g. machinery (1)(1). A nominal account records income or expenses, e.g. wages (1)(1).
[2 marks]

5. Explain why goods taken by the owner for personal use are credited to the purchases account.

Model answerThe goods were originally debited to purchases when bought for resale (1). Since they will not be sold, the purchases figure must be reduced so that cost of sales is not overstated (1).
[2 marks]

6. A supplier's account shows a credit balance of $1,240 brought down. Explain what this means.

Model answerThe business owes the supplier $1,240 (1); the supplier is a trade payable/current liability (1).
[2 marks]

7. State the double entry when the owner pays her private car, valued at $9,000, into the business as additional capital.

Model answerDebit Motor vehicles $9,000 (1); Credit Capital $9,000 (1).
[4 marks]

8. Explain the difference between carriage inwards and carriage outwards and how each is treated in the income statement.

Model answerCarriage inwards is the cost of transporting goods purchased into the business (1); it is added to purchases in the cost of sales (1). Carriage outwards is the cost of delivering goods to customers (1); it is shown as an expense in the profit and loss section (1).

10. Record the following in the ledger of Sami, showing only the double entries: 2 Aug, bought equipment on credit from Delta Ltd $4,000; 7 Aug, sold goods on credit to P. Roy $1,300; 12 Aug, P. Roy returned goods $100; 20 Aug, P. Roy paid the balance by cheque; 30 Aug, paid Delta Ltd $4,000 by cheque.

Model answer2 Aug: Dr Equipment $4,000, Cr Delta Ltd $4,000 (1). 7 Aug: Dr P. Roy $1,300, Cr Sales $1,300 (1). 12 Aug: Dr Sales returns $100, Cr P. Roy $100 (1). 20 Aug: Dr Bank $1,200, Cr P. Roy $1,200 (1)(1). 30 Aug: Dr Delta Ltd $4,000, Cr Bank $4,000 (1).
[4 marks]

11. Explain how the double-entry system is related to the accounting equation.

Model answerEvery transaction affects two items and is recorded by an equal debit and credit (1). Debits increase assets, and credits increase capital and liabilities (1). So each transaction either changes two items on the same side of the equation by equal and opposite amounts, or changes both sides by the same amount (1). Therefore assets always equal capital plus liabilities (1).
[6 marks]

12. Discuss the benefits of keeping separate accounts for sales returns and purchases returns rather than adjusting the sales and purchases accounts directly.

Model answerSeparate accounts show the value of goods returned (1), allowing the owner to monitor levels of returns (1). High sales returns may indicate poor quality or wrong goods being supplied (1). High purchases returns may indicate problems with suppliers (1). Management can take action, such as changing supplier or improving quality control (1). Net sales and net purchases can still be calculated in the income statement (1).

14

Quick Recap

  1. Every transaction has two aspects, recorded as an equal debit and credit.
  2. Assets, expenses and drawings increase with a debit; liabilities, capital and income increase with a credit.
  3. Goods for resale are debited to purchases and sold goods are credited to sales; returns have separate accounts.
  4. Goods taken by the owner are debited to drawings and credited to purchases.
  5. Accounts are personal, real or nominal; nominal balances go to the income statement at the year end.
  6. Carriage inwards is part of cost of sales, while carriage outwards is a profit and loss expense.
  7. Balancing an account means carrying down the difference on the smaller side and bringing it down on the opposite side.
  8. A debit balance on a customer's account is a trade receivable; a credit balance on a supplier's account is a trade payable.
