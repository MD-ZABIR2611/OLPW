Key Concepts

01

Map skills: four- and six-figure grid references locate features precisely (a six-figure reference such as 427365 is read eastings-then-northings to the nearest 100 m); the scale converts map distance to ground distance (on a 1:25 000 map 1 cm = 250 m); direction is given by compass points or three-figure bearings measured clockwise from north.

02

Height and relief are shown by contours, spot heights, layer shading and triangulation pillars; a cross-section plots height along a straight line between two points, often with vertical exaggeration, and gradient = vertical interval divided by horizontal equivalent, e.g. a 100 m rise over 1 000 m = 1 in 10 (10%).

03

River valleys narrow and V-shaped with steep gradients upstream, where vertical erosion dominates, and widen with broader floodplains and meanders downstream as lateral erosion takes over; coasts show similar profile changes, and overlays (tracing-paper or GIS) compare maps of different dates or themes to show settlement growth, land-use change or flood risk.

04

The Paper 4 fieldwork enquiry runs as a chain: state an aim and a testable hypothesis (a null hypothesis predicts no relationship between variables); plan the data collection, mixing primary data measured by the student — river width, depth, velocity, beach profiles, pedestrian counts, questionnaires, weather readings — with secondary data from maps and records.

05

Sampling must be systematic and stated: random (every site has an equal chance), systematic (every 100 m along a river transect) or stratified (proportionate to sub-groups); typical equipment includes flow meters, ranging poles, clinometers, tape measures, quadrats and stopwatches, and velocity = distance travelled by a float divided by time, in m/s, with discharge = width × mean depth × mean velocity in m³/s.

06

Data are presented in tables, scatter graphs, bar and line graphs, cross-sections and annotated maps or photographs, then analysed for trends, anomalies and relationships — the conclusion states whether the hypothesis is accepted or rejected, and the evaluation identifies limitations such as small samples, bad weather and equipment error and suggests realistic improvements.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Six-figure grid referenceA precise map location reading the eastings and northings of a square and estimating tenths within it, e.g. 427365.
ContourA line joining points of equal height on a map; close contours indicate a steep slope.
Cross-sectionA side-view profile of relief along a line drawn on a map, made by plotting heights from the contours.
GradientThe steepness of a slope, calculated as vertical interval divided by horizontal distance, e.g. 1 in 10.
HypothesisA testable statement predicting a relationship between variables in a fieldwork enquiry.
Random samplingChoosing sample sites so that every possible site has an equal chance of selection.
DischargeThe volume of water flowing past a point in a river per second: width × mean depth × mean velocity, in m³/s.
EvaluationThe final stage of fieldwork, assessing the accuracy and limitations of the data and suggesting improvements.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Paper 4 rewards the enquiry sequence — aim, hypothesis, sampling method, data collection, presentation, conclusion, evaluation — so learn it as a chain, always state whether the hypothesis is accepted or rejected, and add one limitation plus one realistic improvement.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Describe how you would measure the velocity of a river at one site during fieldwork.

Model answerChoose a straight, uniform stretch and measure a fixed distance, e.g. 10 m, from upstream to downstream (1). Release a float at the upstream point and time its travel with a stopwatch (1). Repeat at least three times and calculate the average time (1). Velocity = distance ÷ time, e.g. 10 m ÷ 20 s = 0.5 m/s, and record the result in a table (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate how a fieldwork team could test the hypothesis that river velocity increases downstream.

Model answerSelect sites at regular intervals using systematic sampling along the river's course so results can be compared fairly (1). At each site measure width with a tape, depth at regular points across the channel with a metre rule, and velocity with a float and stopwatch over a fixed distance (1). Calculate mean depth and velocity, and discharge = width × mean depth × mean velocity, presenting results in a table and scatter graph of velocity against distance downstream (1). Analyse for a trend, marking anomalies such as a site where a tributary or obstruction slowed the float (1). Conclude by accepting or rejecting the hypothesis, then evaluate: limitations include few sites, wind affecting floats and operator reaction time, and improvements include more sites, a flow meter and repeating readings on another day (1).
