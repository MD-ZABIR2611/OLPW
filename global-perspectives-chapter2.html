Critical Thinking

How to analyse and evaluate arguments by identifying claims, reasons, evidence and conclusions, spotting assumptions, bias and flawed reasoning, and judging overall strength.

01

Overview

Critical thinking is the engine of Global Perspectives. Almost every question in the written exam asks you to break down an argument, judge the quality of its reasoning and evidence, or build a convincing argument of your own. Critical thinking does not mean being negative or criticising everything; it means thinking carefully and fairly before accepting a claim. Mastering the vocabulary of arguments and the common weaknesses in reasoning will give you a reliable method for tackling any source, on any topic, under exam conditions.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Identify the structure of an argument: claim, reasons, evidence and conclusion.
  • Distinguish between fact, opinion, prediction and value judgement.
  • Recognise assumptions, bias and vested interest in an argument.
  • Identify common flaws in reasoning such as generalisation, false cause and emotional appeal.
  • Evaluate the strength of an argument using clear criteria.
  • Construct a well-reasoned argument with a supported conclusion.
02

What Is an Argument?

In everyday life, an argument means a disagreement. In critical thinking, an argument is an attempt to persuade someone to accept a conclusion by giving reasons. For example: 'Cities should ban cars from their centres because air pollution causes thousands of early deaths each year, and car-free zones have reduced pollution in cities such as Oslo.' The conclusion is that cities should ban cars; the reason is the harm from pollution; and the evidence is the example of Oslo.

Every argument has a conclusion, which is the main point the author wants you to accept. Reasons are statements that support the conclusion. Evidence includes facts, statistics, examples, expert opinions or research findings that back up the reasons. Some arguments also contain intermediate conclusions, which are smaller conclusions drawn from reasons that then act as reasons for the main conclusion.

To find the conclusion, look for words such as therefore, so, thus, consequently or should. To find reasons, look for words such as because, since, as and due to. Asking 'What does the author want me to believe?' and then 'Why should I believe it?' is a quick way to map the structure of any argument.

Key fact:Argument = reasons (supported by evidence) leading to a conclusion.
03

Facts, Opinions, Predictions and Value Judgements

A fact is a statement that can be checked and proved true or false, such as 'The Paris Agreement was adopted in 2015.' An opinion is a personal belief or view that cannot be proved, such as 'The Paris Agreement is the most important treaty ever signed.' Opinions are not worthless; an opinion supported by strong reasons and evidence can be very persuasive. However, an argument built only on unsupported opinion is weak.

A prediction is a claim about the future, for example 'Global temperatures will rise by 2°C by 2060.' Predictions can be based on good evidence, such as scientific models, but they are always uncertain. A value judgement is a claim about what is good, bad, right or wrong, often using words such as should, ought, fair or unacceptable. Value judgements depend on a person's beliefs and are common in arguments about global issues.

Being able to classify statements quickly is valuable in the exam, because questions often ask you to identify a fact or an opinion in a source, or to explain why a statement is an opinion. When you explain, point to the feature that makes it an opinion, such as emotive or judgemental language, or the fact that it cannot be checked.

  • Fact: can be verified, e.g. 'Over half the world's population lives in urban areas.'
  • Opinion: a belief that cannot be proved, e.g. 'Cities are better places to live than villages.'
  • Prediction: a claim about the future, e.g. 'Electric cars will outsell petrol cars by 2035.'
  • Value judgement: a claim about right or wrong, e.g. 'It is unfair that rich countries emit more carbon.'
04

Assumptions and Hidden Reasoning

An assumption is an unstated idea that an argument depends on. The author takes it for granted and does not provide evidence for it. For example, the argument 'We should give every student a laptop, so exam results will improve' assumes that students will use the laptops for learning rather than for games or social media. If the assumption is false, the argument collapses.

Spotting assumptions is one of the most powerful ways to evaluate an argument. Ask yourself: what must be true for this conclusion to follow from these reasons? If you can identify a gap between the reasons and the conclusion, you have probably found an assumption. You can then evaluate whether the assumption is reasonable or questionable.

Assumptions are often linked to perspective. A writer from a wealthy country might assume that everyone has internet access, while a writer campaigning for economic growth might assume that growth always benefits the poor. Recognising these hidden beliefs helps you see why people with different backgrounds reach different conclusions.

Key fact:Test for an assumption: if it were false, would the conclusion still follow? If not, the argument depends on it.
05

Bias and Vested Interest

Bias is a tendency to present information in a one-sided way, favouring a particular viewpoint. It may be deliberate, as in advertising or political campaigns, or unconscious, when a writer is influenced by their own background without realising it. Signs of bias include selecting only evidence that supports one side, using emotive language, ignoring counter-arguments and exaggerating the benefits or harms of something.

A vested interest is when a person or organisation stands to gain or lose from people accepting an argument. A fast-food company funding research that claims sugar is harmless has a vested interest, as does a solar panel manufacturer claiming solar energy is the only solution to climate change. Having a vested interest does not automatically make a claim false, but it means you should look more carefully for supporting evidence from independent sources.

When you comment on bias in an exam, be specific. Do not simply say 'this source is biased'. Explain who the author is, what they might gain, and how this could affect the content, for example by leaving out negative information. Then consider whether other features, such as the use of verifiable statistics, reduce the impact of the bias.

06

Common Flaws in Reasoning

A flaw is a weakness in the logic that links reasons to a conclusion. Recognising flaws allows you to explain precisely why an argument is weak, which earns more credit than simply disagreeing. Several flaws appear repeatedly in articles, speeches and online posts about global issues.

A hasty generalisation draws a broad conclusion from too few examples, for example 'My cousin found a job easily after leaving school, so youth unemployment is not a problem.' A false cause, or confusing correlation with causation, assumes that because two things happen together one must cause the other. An appeal to emotion tries to persuade through feelings such as fear or guilt rather than evidence. A straw man misrepresents an opposing view to make it easier to attack.

Other flaws include the slippery slope, which claims that one small step will inevitably lead to extreme consequences without evidence, and the false dilemma, which presents only two options when many exist. An ad hominem attack criticises the person making an argument rather than the argument itself. Learning to name these flaws, and explain how they weaken an argument, is a mark of strong critical thinking.

  • Hasty generalisation: conclusion based on too few cases.
  • False cause: assuming correlation proves causation.
  • Appeal to emotion: using feelings instead of evidence.
  • Straw man: distorting an opponent's view.
  • Slippery slope: claiming one step leads inevitably to disaster.
  • False dilemma: offering only two options when others exist.
  • Ad hominem: attacking the person rather than the argument.
07

Evaluating the Strength of an Argument

To evaluate an argument, judge how well its reasons and evidence support its conclusion. Useful criteria include the quality and relevance of the evidence, whether the reasoning is logical, whether there are hidden assumptions, whether counter-arguments are considered, and whether the language is balanced or emotive. You should also consider the credibility of the author, including expertise and possible vested interest.

Strong arguments usually use evidence that is recent, specific, from reliable sources and relevant to the claim. They consider alternative views and explain why their conclusion is still more convincing. Weak arguments rely on anecdotes, unsupported opinions, exaggeration or flawed logic, and they ignore the other side. Most real arguments have both strengths and weaknesses, so your evaluation should be balanced.

Always finish your evaluation with a judgement. For example: 'Overall, the argument is fairly strong because it uses up-to-date statistics from the World Health Organization, but it is weakened by its assumption that all countries can afford the proposed vaccination programme.' A judgement that weighs strengths against weaknesses is what turns description into evaluation.

Key fact:Evaluate using: evidence, reasoning, assumptions, counter-arguments, language, author credibility, then judge.
08

Constructing Your Own Argument

Critical thinking is also about building your own arguments. Start with a clear position that answers the question. Support it with several reasons, each backed by evidence or an example. Link your reasons to the conclusion using connectives such as therefore, this shows that and as a result. A well-built argument is easy to follow because each step leads logically to the next.

Strong arguments acknowledge counter-arguments. Present the best opposing point fairly and then respond to it, either by showing it is weaker than your reasons or by accepting part of it and adjusting your conclusion. This shows the examiner that your view is considered rather than one-sided, and it is essential for top marks in extended answers.

Finish with a conclusion that follows directly from the reasoning you have given. Avoid introducing new points in the conclusion, and avoid sitting on the fence without explanation. It is acceptable to reach a qualified conclusion, such as 'this policy would be effective in cities but less so in rural areas', provided you explain why.

Key Terms

ClaimA statement put forward as true, which may or may not be supported by evidence.

EvidenceFacts, statistics, examples, expert views or research used to support reasons.

BiasA one-sided presentation that favours a particular view, often by selecting evidence or using emotive language.

Counter-argumentAn argument that opposes or challenges the main argument.

Worked Examples

Mapping the structure of an argument

Identify the conclusion, reasons and evidence in this argument: 'Governments should tax sugary drinks. Obesity rates among children have tripled in many countries since the 1970s, and sugary drinks are one of the biggest sources of added sugar in children's diets. After Mexico introduced a sugar tax in 2014, purchases of taxed drinks fell significantly.'

  1. Ask what the author wants you to believe: governments should tax sugary drinks. This is the conclusion, signalled by 'should'.
  2. Ask why: reason 1 is that child obesity has risen sharply; reason 2 is that sugary drinks are a major source of added sugar.
  3. Identify evidence: the statistic that obesity has tripled since the 1970s, and the Mexico example showing purchases fell after the tax.
  4. Identify an assumption: that reducing purchases of sugary drinks will reduce obesity, rather than people switching to other unhealthy foods.
Conclusion: governments should tax sugary drinks. Reasons: child obesity has risen and sugary drinks are a major source of sugar. Evidence: tripling of obesity rates and falling purchases in Mexico. Key assumption: lower purchases will lead to lower obesity.

Identifying and explaining a flaw

Explain the flaw in this argument: 'Countries with more mobile phones have higher life expectancy. Therefore, giving people mobile phones will make them live longer.'

  1. Notice that the argument links two things that occur together: phone ownership and life expectancy.
  2. Recognise the flaw: it assumes correlation proves causation (false cause).
  3. Explain an alternative explanation: wealthier countries can afford both more phones and better healthcare, nutrition and sanitation, so wealth is a third factor causing both.
  4. Judge the impact: because the link is not shown to be causal, the conclusion does not follow.
The argument commits a false cause flaw by assuming that because phone ownership and life expectancy are correlated, phones cause longer lives. A third factor, national wealth, probably explains both, so handing out phones would not by itself increase life expectancy.

Model answer: evaluating an argument

A campaign leaflet says: 'Fast fashion is destroying our planet! The fashion industry produces around 10% of global carbon emissions. Every time you buy a cheap T-shirt you are killing the Earth. Everyone must stop buying new clothes immediately.' Evaluate the strength of this argument. [6 marks]

  1. Strength: it uses a statistic about carbon emissions, which gives some factual support, although no source is given so it cannot be checked.
  2. Weakness: the language is highly emotive ('destroying', 'killing the Earth'), which appeals to emotion rather than reason.
  3. Weakness: the conclusion that everyone must stop buying new clothes is extreme and presents a false dilemma, ignoring options such as buying fewer, second-hand or sustainably produced clothes.
  4. Weakness: it assumes all individuals can afford alternatives and ignores the jobs of millions of garment workers in countries such as Bangladesh.
  5. Weakness: the author is a campaign group with a clear agenda and no counter-arguments are considered.
  6. Judgement: weigh these points to reach an overall conclusion.
The argument has some strength because it refers to a significant statistic on emissions, but it is weak overall. The statistic is unsourced, the language is emotive, the conclusion is an extreme false dilemma, and it ignores economic consequences for garment workers and consumers. A stronger argument would cite a named source and propose realistic alternatives.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Saying whether you agree with an argument when asked to evaluate its strength; judge the reasoning and evidence, not your personal opinion of the topic.
  • Writing 'the source is biased' without explaining who the author is, what they gain and how it affects the content.
  • Treating every statistic as strong evidence; check whether it is sourced, recent, relevant and representative.
  • Naming a flaw without explaining why it weakens the specific argument; always link the flaw to the conclusion.
  • Confusing a reason with the conclusion; ask what the author ultimately wants you to accept.
  • Ending an evaluation with no overall judgement; always state how strong the argument is and why.

Exam tips

  • Use the signal words: therefore and should often mark conclusions; because and since often mark reasons.
  • When evaluating, aim to give at least one strength and two weaknesses (or vice versa) before your judgement.
  • Quote short phrases from the source to show exactly where emotive language or a flaw occurs.
  • Ask 'What must be true for this to work?' to uncover assumptions quickly.
  • Use evaluative vocabulary such as convincing, credible, limited, unsupported, exaggerated and one-sided.
  • In your own arguments, always include and respond to at least one counter-argument.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. Define the term 'conclusion' in an argument.

Model answerThe main point or claim (1) that the author wants the reader to accept, supported by reasons (1).
[3 marks]

2. Is this statement a fact or an opinion? 'Renewable energy is the best solution to climate change.' Explain your answer.

Model answerOpinion (1). It is a judgement ('best') that cannot be proved true or false (1); others might argue for different solutions such as nuclear power or reducing consumption (1).
[2 marks]

3. What is an assumption?

Model answerAn unstated idea or belief (1) that an argument depends on and takes for granted without evidence (1).
[2 marks]

4. Identify the assumption in: 'Building more schools will end child labour.'

Model answerIt assumes children work because there are not enough schools (1), rather than because families need their income, so schools alone may not stop child labour (1).
[4 marks]

5. Explain what is meant by vested interest and why it matters when evaluating an argument.

Model answerA vested interest is when the author may gain or lose from the outcome (1), e.g. financially or politically (1). It matters because the author may exaggerate benefits or leave out opposing evidence (1), so claims should be checked against independent sources (1).
[3 marks]

6. Name and explain the flaw: 'My grandfather smoked every day and lived to 95, so smoking is not harmful.'

Model answerHasty generalisation or anecdotal evidence (1). One individual case (1) cannot outweigh large-scale medical research showing smoking increases disease risk (1).
[3 marks]

7. Explain the difference between correlation and causation.

Model answerCorrelation is when two things change or occur together (1). Causation is when one thing directly makes the other happen (1). Correlation does not prove causation because a third factor or coincidence could explain the link (1).
[2 marks]

8. Give two features that make an argument strong.

Model answerAny two: uses reliable, relevant and up-to-date evidence (1); logical reasoning where the conclusion follows from reasons (1); considers and responds to counter-arguments (1); written by a credible, independent expert (1); balanced, non-emotive language (1).
[4 marks]

9. Explain two ways an author might show bias.

Model answerSelecting only evidence that supports their view (1), so the reader gets a misleading picture (1). Using emotive or loaded language (1) to persuade through feelings rather than facts (1). Other valid: ignoring counter-arguments, exaggeration.
[4 marks]

10. Explain why a false dilemma weakens an argument, using an example.

Model answerA false dilemma presents only two options when more exist (1). Example: 'Either we ban all cars or the planet will be destroyed' (1). It weakens the argument because it ignores alternatives such as electric vehicles or better public transport (1), so the conclusion is not justified (1).
[6 marks]

11. Read the claim: 'Social media is making teenagers depressed. A survey of 50 students at my school found that those who used social media most were unhappiest.' Evaluate the strength of this argument.

Model answerStrengths: provides some evidence from a survey (1); social media's impact on wellbeing is a recognised concern (1). Weaknesses: small, unrepresentative sample of one school (1); correlation does not prove causation, as unhappy students may use social media more (1); no information on how happiness was measured (1); depression is a medical condition not the same as unhappiness (1). Judgement: weak argument overall because evidence is limited and the causal claim is not established (1). Maximum 6.
[8 marks]

12. 'Tourism does more harm than good to developing countries.' Construct a reasoned argument either for or against this claim, including a counter-argument.

Model answerClear position stated (1). Reasons with evidence, e.g. against the claim: tourism creates jobs and foreign currency (1-2), funds conservation through park fees (1-2), and supports local businesses (1). Counter-argument fairly presented: profits may go to foreign companies, environmental damage and cultural change (1-2). Response to counter-argument showing why the position remains stronger, e.g. community-based tourism keeps profits local (1-2). Logical conclusion following from reasons (1). Maximum 8.

Quick Recap

  1. An argument uses reasons and evidence to persuade you to accept a conclusion.
  2. Facts can be verified, opinions cannot, predictions concern the future, and value judgements concern right and wrong.
  3. Assumptions are unstated ideas an argument depends on; if they are false the argument fails.
  4. Bias and vested interest can distort an argument, so always explain how and why they affect the content.
  5. Common flaws include hasty generalisation, false cause, appeal to emotion, straw man, slippery slope and false dilemma.
  6. Evaluate arguments using evidence, logic, assumptions, counter-arguments, language and author credibility, then give a judgement.
  7. Build your own arguments with a clear position, supported reasons, a counter-argument and a conclusion that follows logically.
