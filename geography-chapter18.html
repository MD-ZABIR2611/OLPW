Development Indicators, Aid & Human Welfare in Depth

Development is about quality of life, not just income: indicators such as the HDI measure human welfare, and aid is one controversial tool for improving it.

Key Concepts

01

Development means improvement in the quality of life and standard of living — income, health, education and security — not merely growth of output; it is measured with economic indicators such as GDP or GNI per capita and energy consumption, and social indicators such as life expectancy, infant mortality, literacy, access to safe water and daily calorie intake.

02

The Human Development Index (HDI) combines income (GNI per capita), life expectancy at birth, and education (mean and expected years of schooling) into a single score from 0 to 1, ranking countries more fairly than income alone because rich but unequal countries score lower than their wealth suggests.

03

Indicators must be read critically: GDP per capita hides the distribution of income and the informal (hidden) economy, averages conceal regional and gender inequality, and converting incomes at purchasing power parity (PPP) adjusts for price differences between countries to make comparisons fairer.

04

Aid takes several forms: emergency (relief) aid after disasters; development aid for long-term projects such as dams, roads, schools and vaccination programmes; bilateral aid flows government-to-government; multilateral aid flows through bodies such as the UN, World Bank and IMF; non-governmental organisations (NGOs) such as Oxfam work directly with communities; tied aid requires the receiver to spend the money on donor-country goods and services.

05

Supporters argue aid saves lives, funds infrastructure, health and education, relieves debt and can kick-start growth; critics reply that it creates dependency, is often lost to corruption, tied aid raises costs and favours donor exporters, and aid is volatile — some argue trade and good governance matter more, summed up as trade not aid.

06

Human welfare improves through wider strategies: universal education (especially of girls), healthcare and clean water, microfinance for small entrepreneurs, fair trade prices for primary products, and sustainable development; the Millennium Development Goals (2000-2015) and the Sustainable Development Goals (2015-2030) set global targets such as ending extreme poverty and achieving universal primary education, though rapid population growth can outstrip the gains.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
DevelopmentThe improvement in people's quality of life and standard of living, including income, health, education and security, not merely economic growth.
GDP per capita (PPP)Total economic output per person converted at purchasing power parity to allow fair comparison between countries with different prices.
Human Development IndexA composite measure of development combining income, life expectancy and years of schooling, scored from 0 to 1.
Infant mortality rateThe number of deaths of children under one year old per 1 000 live births per year.
Literacy rateThe percentage of the adult population able to read and write.
Bilateral aidAid given directly from one country's government to another, often for a specific project.
Multilateral aidAid given through international organisations such as the UN, World Bank or IMF, funded by many countries.
Tied aidAid granted on condition that the receiving country spends it on goods and services from the donor country.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

For development data questions, quote figures from the resource first, compare two countries, then explain what the indicator does not show — distribution, informal economy, quality of life — because state, compare, then criticise is exactly how the mark scheme climbs from low to high marks.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

State four indicators used to measure development and say what each one shows.

Model answerGDP or GNI per capita: average economic output or income per person (1). Life expectancy: the average number of years a newborn can expect to live, showing healthcare and living standards (1). Infant mortality rate: deaths of children under one year per 1 000 live births, showing health provision (1). Literacy rate or school enrolment: the proportion able to read and write or in education, showing human capital development (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate two arguments for and two arguments against giving aid to developing countries.

Model answerFor: emergency and medical aid save lives after disasters and through vaccination and clean-water projects, which no poor government could fund alone (1). Development aid builds roads, schools and power supplies that raise productivity and growth for the future (1). Against: aid can create dependency, be diverted by corruption, and tied aid forces the receiver to buy overpriced donor goods, reducing its real value (1). Aid can be volatile and may distort local markets, while critics argue trade access, fair prices and good governance help more in the long run (1). Overall, aid helps most when it is untied, well-governed and aimed at health, education and infrastructure rather than endless consumption (1).
