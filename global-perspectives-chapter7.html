Poverty and Inequality

01

Overview

Poverty and inequality are among the most important and most debated global issues, and they connect to almost every other topic in the syllabus, from health and education to migration and conflict. Although extreme poverty fell dramatically in recent decades, hundreds of millions of people still live without enough income for basic needs, and inequality within and between countries remains high. In the exam, this topic frequently appears in sources that ask you to explain causes and consequences or evaluate proposed solutions. Understanding key measures and contrasting perspectives will help you tackle these questions confidently.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Distinguish between absolute poverty, relative poverty and inequality.
  • Describe how poverty and inequality are measured, including the international poverty line, the Gini coefficient and the Human Development Index.
  • Explain economic, social, political and environmental causes of poverty.
  • Explain the consequences of poverty and inequality for individuals, communities and countries.
  • Evaluate courses of action to reduce poverty, including aid, cash transfers, microfinance and education.
  • Compare perspectives of different stakeholders on how poverty should be tackled.
02

Defining Poverty and Inequality

Absolute poverty means lacking the resources needed to meet basic needs such as food, clean water, shelter, sanitation, healthcare and education. The World Bank measures extreme poverty using an international poverty line; in 2025 this was updated to 3.00 US dollars per person per day, adjusted for differences in prices between countries, replacing the earlier line of 2.15 dollars. Hundreds of millions of people, many in sub-Saharan Africa, live below this line.

Relative poverty means having much less than most people in your own society, so that you cannot participate fully in normal life. It is usually defined as living on less than a certain percentage of the median income in a country, often 50 or 60 per cent. A family in a wealthy country may not face starvation yet still be relatively poor if they cannot afford adequate heating, internet access or school trips.

Inequality refers to the unequal distribution of income, wealth or opportunities between people or groups. It exists between countries, for example between high-income and low-income nations, and within countries, between rich and poor, urban and rural areas, men and women, or different ethnic groups. A country can grow richer overall while inequality rises, if the gains go mainly to those who are already wealthy.

Key fact:Absolute poverty = cannot meet basic needs; relative poverty = far below the typical living standard in your society.
03

Measuring Poverty and Development

Income-based measures such as the international poverty line are simple to compare across countries, but they ignore other aspects of deprivation. The Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI), developed by the United Nations Development Programme and Oxford University, measures poverty across health, education and living standards using indicators such as child mortality, years of schooling, access to clean water, electricity and cooking fuel. It shows that people can be deprived in many ways even when their income is above the poverty line.

The Human Development Index (HDI) combines life expectancy, education (expected and mean years of schooling) and income per person into a score between 0 and 1. It gives a broader view of development than income alone. Countries such as Norway and Switzerland score very highly, while countries affected by conflict and low incomes score lowest.

Inequality is often measured with the Gini coefficient, which ranges from 0 (perfect equality, everyone has the same income) to 1 (perfect inequality, one person has everything). It is sometimes expressed as a figure from 0 to 100. South Africa has one of the highest Gini coefficients in the world, while many Nordic countries have among the lowest. Another simple measure compares the share of income received by the richest 10 per cent with the share received by the poorest 40 per cent.

  • International poverty line: income-based measure of extreme poverty.
  • Multidimensional Poverty Index: deprivation in health, education and living standards.
  • Human Development Index: life expectancy, education and income combined.
  • Gini coefficient: income inequality from 0 (equal) to 1 (unequal).
04

Causes of Poverty

Poverty has many interconnected causes. Economic causes include low wages, unemployment, lack of access to credit, dependence on a few low-value exports such as raw agricultural products, and high levels of national debt that limit government spending on services. Global trade rules and falling commodity prices can also reduce the incomes of farmers in low-income countries.

Social causes include limited access to education and healthcare, discrimination based on gender, caste, ethnicity or disability, and large family sizes in places without social security. Political causes include corruption, weak governance, poor infrastructure, and conflict, which destroys homes, businesses and services. Many of the world's poorest people live in fragile or conflict-affected states.

Environmental causes are increasingly important. Droughts, floods and storms destroy crops and property, and climate change is making such events more frequent in many regions. Poor people are especially vulnerable because they often depend on farming, live in hazard-prone areas and have little savings or insurance to recover. These causes interact to create a poverty cycle that is hard to escape.

05

The Poverty Cycle and Consequences

The poverty cycle describes how poverty can be passed from one generation to the next. Low income leads to poor nutrition and limited healthcare, which leads to illness and low school attendance. Children who leave school early have fewer skills and earn low wages as adults, so their own children are also likely to grow up in poverty. Breaking any link in the cycle, for example by keeping children in school, can help families escape.

For individuals, poverty causes hunger, illness, stress, shorter life expectancy and limited opportunities. For communities, it can lead to poor housing, crime, lack of investment and migration of young people seeking work elsewhere. For countries, widespread poverty means lower tax revenue, a less productive workforce, pressure on public services and sometimes political instability.

High inequality has its own consequences. Research by organisations such as the International Monetary Fund suggests that very high inequality can slow economic growth, reduce social mobility and weaken trust in institutions. Unequal societies often experience more social tension and protest, as seen when rising living costs affect poor households far more than wealthy ones.

Key fact:Poverty cycle: low income → poor health and education → low skills → low income for the next generation.
06

Courses of Action

International aid is money, goods or expertise given by governments, organisations or individuals to help other countries. Emergency aid responds to disasters, while development aid funds long-term projects such as schools, clinics and water supply. Supporters argue aid saves lives and builds infrastructure; critics argue it can create dependency, be lost to corruption or be tied to the donor's interests, for example requiring recipients to buy goods from the donor country.

Cash transfers give money directly to poor households. Conditional cash transfer programmes, such as Bolsa Família in Brazil, pay families if they meet conditions such as keeping children in school and attending health check-ups. Studies suggest these programmes reduced poverty and improved school attendance. Unconditional transfers trust families to decide how to spend money and are simpler to run, although some critics worry about misuse.

Microfinance provides small loans, savings and insurance to people who cannot access normal banks, often to start small businesses. The Grameen Bank in Bangladesh, founded by Muhammad Yunus, who shared the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize, is the best-known example. Microfinance has helped many women gain income and independence, but research shows mixed results: some borrowers struggle with repayments and high interest rates. Other courses of action include investing in education, fair trade schemes, progressive taxation, minimum wages and debt relief.

  • Aid: emergency or long-term; risks include dependency and corruption.
  • Conditional cash transfers: money linked to schooling or health conditions.
  • Microfinance: small loans for small businesses; mixed evidence of impact.
  • Education: builds skills and breaks the poverty cycle, but results take time.
  • Fair trade: guaranteed minimum prices for producers.
  • Progressive taxation and welfare: redistribute income to reduce inequality.
07

Perspectives on Poverty and Inequality

Different stakeholders see poverty differently. Some economists and business leaders emphasise economic growth, free markets and trade as the most powerful ways to reduce poverty, pointing to the huge falls in extreme poverty in China and India as their economies grew. Others argue that growth alone is not enough, because benefits can be unevenly shared, and that governments must redistribute income through taxes, welfare and public services.

NGOs often focus on the rights and voices of people living in poverty, arguing that solutions should be designed with communities rather than imposed on them. Governments of low-income countries may call for fairer trade rules, debt relief and more climate finance from wealthy nations. Donor governments may emphasise good governance and accountability as conditions for support.

At a personal level, people's views are shaped by values. Some believe individuals are mainly responsible for their own circumstances, while others stress structural causes such as discrimination and unequal opportunities. The United Nations Sustainable Development Goals include ending poverty in all its forms (Goal 1) and reducing inequality within and among countries (Goal 10), showing broad international agreement on the aims, if not always on the methods.

Key fact:SDG 1: No Poverty. SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities.

Worked Examples

Evaluating a claim about microfinance

Source: 'Microfinance is the answer to global poverty. Thousands of women in Bangladesh have started businesses with small loans and lifted their families out of poverty.' Evaluate the strength of this argument. [6 marks]

  1. Identify the conclusion: microfinance is the answer to global poverty.
  2. Strength: it gives an example from Bangladesh, where microfinance is well established, and highlights women's empowerment, which is supported by some research.
  3. Weakness: 'thousands' is vague and unsourced, so the scale cannot be checked.
  4. Weakness: hasty generalisation from one country to 'global poverty', ignoring different contexts.
  5. Weakness: it ignores evidence that some borrowers fall into debt because of high interest rates, and that loans do not solve lack of markets, infrastructure or healthcare.
  6. Judgement: weigh up and conclude.
The argument is weak overall. It offers a relevant example but uses vague, unsourced evidence, overgeneralises from one country, and presents microfinance as a single solution despite mixed evidence. A more convincing argument would present microfinance as one tool among several, supported by named research.

Explaining the poverty cycle

Explain how poor health can keep a family in poverty. [4 marks]

  1. Start with the link: poor families may lack clean water, sanitation and nutritious food, increasing illness.
  2. Develop the consequence for adults: illness means adults cannot work, so income falls and money is spent on medicine.
  3. Develop the consequence for children: sick children miss school or leave early to care for relatives, reducing future skills.
  4. Link back to the cycle: lower skills mean low wages in adulthood, so poverty passes to the next generation.
Poverty limits access to clean water and nutrition, causing illness (1). Sick adults lose income and spend savings on treatment (1). Children miss school through illness or caring duties (1), so they gain fewer skills and earn low wages as adults, continuing the cycle (1).

Comparing courses of action

A government in a low-income country has funds for one programme: building new schools or a conditional cash transfer scheme. Which should it choose? Justify your answer.

  1. Consider schools: build long-term human capital, but many poor children miss school because families need their labour or cannot afford uniforms, so new buildings alone may not raise attendance.
  2. Consider cash transfers: give families income now and are conditional on school attendance and health checks, tackling both immediate poverty and the cause of low attendance.
  3. Evidence: programmes such as Bolsa Família are associated with reduced poverty and improved attendance.
  4. Limitations of cash transfers: require reliable administration to identify families and prevent fraud, and do not work if schools are overcrowded or far away.
  5. Reach a justified judgement, possibly conditional on context.
The cash transfer scheme is likely to be more effective where schools already exist but attendance is low, because it tackles the financial barriers that keep children out of school while reducing immediate poverty. If there are no schools nearby, investment in schools must come first. The best choice therefore depends on local conditions.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Confusing absolute and relative poverty; remember relative poverty is measured against the average in a particular society.
  • Treating poverty as having one cause; explain how economic, social, political and environmental factors interact.
  • Assuming aid is always good or always bad; evaluate the type of aid and how it is used.
  • Using out-of-date poverty line figures without noting that they are revised over time.
  • Describing a solution without evaluating its limitations, costs or suitability in different contexts.
  • Presenting only the perspective of wealthy donor countries and ignoring people living in poverty.

Exam tips

  • Learn two or three measures (poverty line, HDI, Gini) and one limitation of each.
  • Use the poverty cycle to develop explanations of consequences into chains of reasoning.
  • Refer to named examples such as Bolsa Família or the Grameen Bank to support evaluation.
  • When evaluating solutions, consider cost, scale, speed, sustainability and who controls them.
  • Show different scales: household, community, national government and international organisations.
  • Link poverty to other topics such as health, education, climate change and migration for richer analysis.

Quick Recap

  1. Absolute poverty means lacking basic needs; relative poverty means being far below the average in your society.
  2. Extreme poverty is measured by the World Bank's international poverty line, revised to 3.00 dollars a day in 2025.
  3. The HDI and MPI give broader views of development and deprivation; the Gini coefficient measures inequality.
  4. Poverty has interacting economic, social, political and environmental causes.
  5. The poverty cycle passes disadvantage between generations through poor health, education and income.
  6. Courses of action include aid, cash transfers, microfinance, education, fair trade and redistribution, each with limitations.
  7. Stakeholders disagree on whether growth, redistribution or community-led solutions matter most.
