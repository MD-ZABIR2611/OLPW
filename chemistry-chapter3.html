Chapter Overview

What is it?

This chapter explores the microscopic building blocks of matter. It defines atoms, elements, and compounds, delves into the subatomic structure (protons, neutrons, electrons), and explains how atoms interact through ionic, covalent, and metallic bonding to form the materials we see.

Why It Matters

You cannot understand chemical reactions without understanding what is reacting. This chapter provides the foundational rules for chemistry. Mastering bonding and structure allows you to predict macroscopic properties like melting point, electrical conductivity, and strength—core skills for IGCSE/O-Level exams.

Detailed Theory

01

The Atom & Subatomic Particles

Definition: An atom is the smallest particle of a chemical element that can take part in a chemical reaction.

Explanation: Atoms are not solid spheres; they possess an internal structure. The center of the atom is the nucleus, which contains protons (positive) and neutrons (neutral). Orbiting the nucleus in electron shells are electrons (negative). The nucleus is very small compared to the overall size of the atom.

Why it matters: The number of protons dictates what element the atom is. Changing the number of neutrons creates isotopes, while changing electrons creates ions.

02

Atomic Number, Mass Number & Isotopes

Atomic Number (Z): The number of protons in the nucleus. In a neutral atom, Protons = Electrons.

Mass Number (A): The sum of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.

Isotopes: Atoms of the same element (same atomic number) that have different numbers of neutrons (different mass number). Example: Carbon-12 and Carbon-14.

Relative Atomic Mass ($A_r$): The weighted average mass of an element's isotopes compared to 1/12th of Carbon-12. It is rarely a whole number because isotopes exist in nature in different abundances.

03

Electronic Configuration

Explanation: Electrons orbit the nucleus in distinct energy levels called shells. The 1st shell holds max 2 electrons; the 2nd holds max 8; the 3rd holds max 8 (for the first 20 elements).

Valence Electrons: The electrons in the outermost shell. They determine how an atom reacts and bonds.

Why it happens: Atoms are most stable when they have a full outer shell (like Noble Gases). Atoms will lose, gain, or share electrons to achieve this stable state.

04

Ions & The Periodic Table

Ion: A charged particle formed when an atom gains or loses electrons.

  • Cation: Positive ion (loses electrons). E.g., Na → Na⁺ + e⁻
  • Anion: Negative ion (gains electrons). E.g., Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻

Periodic Table Structure:

  • Periods: Horizontal rows. Period number = number of electron shells.
  • Groups: Vertical columns. Group number = number of valence electrons. (Group 1 = 1 valence e⁻; Group 8 = full shell).
05

Ionic Bonding

Definition: The electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions, formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal.

Structure: Ions arrange themselves in a giant 3D lattice to maximize attraction and minimize repulsion.

Properties:

  • High Melting Point: Strong electrostatic forces require massive amounts of energy to break.
  • Conducts (Molten/Aqueous only): Ions are free to move and carry charge. Solid ions are locked in the lattice.
  • Brittle: If layers slide, similar charges align, repel, and shatter the crystal.
06

Covalent Bonding & Simple Molecules

Definition: A chemical bond formed when two non-metal atoms share pairs of electrons to achieve full outer shells.

  • Single Bond: 1 pair shared (e.g., H₂, Cl₂)
  • Double Bond: 2 pairs shared (e.g., O₂, CO₂)
  • Triple Bond: 3 pairs shared (e.g., N₂)

Simple Molecular Properties:

  • Low Melting/Boiling Points: Weak intermolecular forces between molecules (the covalent bonds themselves are very strong).
  • Non-conductive: No free-moving electrons or ions.
07

Giant Covalent Structures

Some covalent substances form massive continuous networks rather than small molecules.

  • Diamond: Each Carbon forms 4 covalent bonds in a rigid 3D tetrahedron. Extremely hard, high melting point, does not conduct (no free electrons).
  • Graphite: Each Carbon forms 3 covalent bonds in flat layers. One electron per atom is delocalized. Soft/slippery (layers slide), high melting point, does conduct electricity.
  • Graphene: A single layer of graphite. Extremely strong, very light, excellent conductor.
08

Metallic Bonding

Definition: The electrostatic force of attraction between positive metal ions arranged in a lattice and a "sea" of delocalized electrons.

Properties:

  • Conducts Solid & Liquid: Delocalized electrons are free to move and carry charge.
  • High Melting Point: Strong attraction between ions and electrons.
  • Malleable/Ductile: Layers of ions can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bond.

Observations: Silvery metal burns with orange flame in green gas; white solid forms.

Worked Examples

Case Study 01 / Basic

Particle Calculation

Question: An atom of Aluminum has an atomic number of 13 and a mass number of 27. Find protons, neutrons, electrons.

Think: Protons = Z. Electrons = Z (neutral). Neutrons = A - Z.

Protons = 13

Electrons = 13

Neutrons = 27 - 13 = 14

Case Study 02 / Intermediate

Ionic Formula Writing

Question: Write the formula for Magnesium Oxide.

1. Mg is Group 2 → Mg²⁺

2. O is Group 6 → O²⁻

3. Balance charges: +2 and -2 cancel in a 1:1 ratio.

Formula = MgO

Case Study 03 / Advanced

Relative Atomic Mass

Question: Chlorine has two isotopes: Cl-35 (75% abundance) and Cl-37 (25% abundance). Calculate $A_r$.

$A_r$ = [(35 × 75) + (37 × 25)] / 100

$A_r$ = [2625 + 925] / 100

$A_r$ = 3550 / 100

Answer = 35.5

Laboratory Protocol

Experiment 01 / Electrical Conductivity

Testing Ionic vs Covalent Substances

Objective: To determine if solid, molten, and aqueous ionic compounds conduct electricity, compared to covalent compounds.

Apparatus: Beaker, 2 carbon electrodes, battery, wires, LED bulb/ammerter, Bunsen burner, crucible.

Method:
1. Set up circuit with electrodes.
2. Test solid NaCl. Record.
3. Melt NaCl in crucible, test. Record.
4. Dissolve NaCl in water, test. Record.
5. Repeat with pure water and sugar solution.

Observations:

  • Solid NaCl: No light.
  • Molten NaCl: Light.
  • NaCl (aq): Light.
  • Pure water: No light.
  • Sugar solution: No light.

Safety: Wear goggles. Molten salts are extremely hot and can spit. Handle with tongs.

Errors: Forgetting to dry electrodes between tests (contamination).

Exam Application: Examiners often ask why solid NaCl doesn't conduct but aqueous does. Answer: Ions are free to move in liquid/aqueous state but fixed in solid lattice.

Exam Protocol

State

Give a fact without explanation.

Example: State the charge of an electron. -1

Describe

Say what is observed or happens.

Example: Describe melting point of ionic. High.

Explain

Give the scientific reason.

Example: Explain why high. Strong electrostatic forces need energy to break.

Compare

Mention similarities/differences.

Example: Compare ionic/covalent mp. Ionic is high, covalent simple is low.

Calculate

Show complete working and units.

Example: Calculate neutrons. A - Z = 27-13 = 14.

Suggest

Give a scientifically reasonable answer supported by info.

Example: Suggest why graphene is strong. Strong covalent bonds in hexagonal sheet.

Error Database

Wrong

Ionic compounds have strong bonds, so they are strong and unbreakable materials.

Correct

Ionic compounds are brittle. When hit, layers slide, similar charges align (+/+), repel, and shatter the crystal.

Wrong

Covalent bonds are weak because simple molecules have low boiling points.

Correct

The intermolecular forces between molecules are weak. The actual covalent bonds inside the molecule are very strong.

Wrong

Atoms lose protons to become negative ions.

Correct

Atoms NEVER lose or gain protons (except in nuclear reactions). They only gain or lose electrons.

Wrong

Solid ionic compounds don't conduct because they have no electrons.

Correct

They have electrons, but they are bound tightly in their shells. Ions are locked in the lattice and cannot move to carry charge.

Rapid Revision

Core Facts
  • Shell 1 = 2e; Shell 2 = 8e; Shell 3 = 8e
  • Proton = +1, Neutron = 0, Electron = -1
  • Isotopes: Same protons, different neutrons
  • Group No = Valence e- ; Period No = Shells
Bonding Rules
  • Metal + Non-metal = Ionic (Transfer)
  • Non-metal + Non-metal = Covalent (Share)
  • Metal + Metal = Metallic (Sea of e-)
  • Graphite conducts due to 1 delocalized e- per C.

Exam Simulation

Basic

1. State the relative mass and charge of a neutron.

Mark Scheme Answer [2 Marks]

Mass = 1, Charge = 0 (neutral).

Intermediate

2. Explain why solid sodium chloride does not conduct electricity, but molten sodium chloride does.

Mark Scheme Answer [2 Marks]

Solid: Ions are locked in a giant lattice and cannot move.
Molten: Ions are free to move and carry the electrical charge.

Advanced

3. Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why diamond is hard but graphite is soft.

Mark Scheme Answer [3 Marks]

Diamond: Each carbon forms 4 strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D tetrahedral structure, making it hard.
Graphite: Each carbon forms only 3 bonds, creating flat layers held by weak intermolecular forces. Layers can slide over each other, making it soft.
