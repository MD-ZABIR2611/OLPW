Settlement and Urbanisation

How settlements are sited, arranged and ranked, how cities are structured, and why rapid urbanisation creates problems and opportunities in both HICs and LICs.

01

Overview

Since 2007 more than half of the world's population has lived in urban areas, and by 2050 the figure is expected to reach about 68%. Settlements range from isolated farmhouses to megacities of over 10 million people such as Dhaka, Mumbai and Lagos. This chapter explains why settlements grow where they do, how services are arranged in a settlement hierarchy, how land use changes across a city, and how rapid urban growth leads to squatter settlements, congestion and pollution. Exam questions often combine map skills with case studies of urban problems and their solutions, so learn both the models and real examples.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain how site, situation and function influence the growth of settlements.
  • Describe dispersed, nucleated and linear settlement patterns and explain their causes.
  • Explain settlement hierarchy using the ideas of threshold, range and sphere of influence.
  • Describe and explain urban land-use zones in HIC and LIC cities.
  • Explain the causes of rapid urbanisation and the problems it creates.
  • Evaluate strategies used to reduce urban problems, including in squatter settlements.
  • Describe the impacts of urban growth on the rural-urban fringe and the environment.
02

Site, Situation and Function

The site of a settlement is the physical characteristics of the land on which it is built. Early settlers chose sites that offered a reliable water supply (a wet-point site), flat land above flood level (a dry-point site), defensive positions such as hilltops or river meanders, shelter from wind, fertile soil for farming and building materials such as stone or timber. Many European towns grew at a bridging point where a river could be crossed, while Dhaka grew on higher ground beside the Buriganga River, which provided water, transport and trade.

The situation of a settlement is its location in relation to surrounding features such as other settlements, routes, rivers and resources. A settlement with a good situation, for example at the meeting point of several valleys or at a river mouth with a sheltered harbour, becomes a route centre and grows larger. The function of a settlement is its main economic activity or purpose, such as a market town, port, mining town, resort, administrative capital or industrial centre. Functions change over time: many former industrial towns now have mainly service functions.

Key fact:Site = the land the settlement is built on. Situation = its location relative to surrounding features.
03

Settlement Patterns

Rural settlement patterns describe how buildings are arranged. A dispersed pattern has isolated farms and houses spread over a wide area; it is found where farms are large, such as on the Canadian Prairies or in upland sheep-farming areas, or where water is widely available. A nucleated pattern has buildings grouped closely together around a central point such as a crossroads, well, church or mosque; it develops for defence, to share water supplies or services, or where land is scarce.

A linear pattern has buildings strung out along a line, such as a road, river, canal, railway, valley floor or raised river levée. In Bangladesh many villages are linear along embankments and raised roads because these stay dry during the monsoon floods. In the Netherlands houses line the dykes, and in mountain areas settlements often follow the flat valley floor. On an Ordnance Survey or topographic map you identify patterns by looking at the spacing and arrangement of the black building symbols.

  • Dispersed: isolated buildings, large farms, few services.
  • Nucleated: clustered buildings, defence, shared water or services.
  • Linear: buildings along a road, river, levée or valley floor.
04

Settlement Hierarchy and Services

A settlement hierarchy ranks settlements by size and importance, from isolated dwellings and hamlets through villages, towns and cities to conurbations and megacities. As you move up the hierarchy, population increases, the number and range of services increases, the distance between settlements of the same rank increases and the number of settlements at each level decreases. A hamlet may have no services, a village may have a primary school, small shop and place of worship, while a city has hospitals, universities, department stores and specialist services.

The pattern is explained by threshold and range. The threshold is the minimum number of people needed to support a service and keep it in business; a corner shop needs a few hundred customers, but a department store or specialist hospital needs hundreds of thousands. The range is the maximum distance people are prepared to travel to use a service. Low-order goods such as bread and newspapers are bought frequently, so people travel only short distances. High-order goods such as furniture, cars and jewellery are bought rarely, so people travel further. The area served by a settlement is its sphere of influence.

05

Urban Land Use and Structure

Cities are divided into land-use zones. In HIC cities the Burgess concentric model shows a central business district (CBD) at the centre, surrounded by a zone of transition or inner city, then low-cost housing, medium-cost suburbs and high-cost outer suburbs, with the rural-urban fringe at the edge. The CBD has the highest land values because it is the most accessible point, so it contains tall office blocks, department stores, banks and entertainment. Land values and building density generally decline outwards, while house and garden size increase.

In many LIC cities the pattern is different. The CBD is often surrounded by high-quality housing for wealthy residents, close to jobs and services, with lower-quality housing further out. Squatter settlements or informal settlements develop on the edge of the city and on unused land that is dangerous or unwanted, such as steep hillsides, river banks, railway lines and marshes. Industry often lines major roads leading out of the city. Real cities rarely fit models perfectly because of relief, rivers, transport routes and planning decisions.

Key fact:Land values are highest in the CBD because it is the most accessible point in the city.
06

Causes of Rapid Urbanisation

Urbanisation is the increase in the proportion of a country's population living in urban areas. It is happening fastest in LICs and MICs in Africa and Asia for two main reasons. First, rural-urban migration is driven by push factors such as poverty, landlessness, mechanisation of farming, natural hazards and conflict, and pull factors such as jobs, higher wages, education and health care. Second, natural increase is high in cities because migrants are mostly young adults of child-bearing age, and improved health care in cities lowers death rates.

Dhaka's metropolitan population grew from around 400,000 in 1950 to over 23 million today, making it one of the fastest-growing megacities in the world. Lagos in Nigeria and Kinshasa in the Democratic Republic of Congo have also grown extremely rapidly. In contrast, HICs such as the UK became highly urbanised during the Industrial Revolution in the 19th century and are now around 84% urban, with slower growth and some counter-urbanisation.

07

Urban Problems in LICs

Rapid urban growth in LICs often outpaces the ability of governments to provide housing, services and infrastructure. The result is informal or squatter settlements built by residents on land they do not own, using scrap materials such as corrugated iron, plastic, bamboo and wood. These areas typically lack piped water, sewerage, electricity and rubbish collection, so disease spreads easily. Overcrowding, fire risk and flooding are serious problems, and residents have no legal security of tenure, so they may be evicted.

Other problems include traffic congestion, air pollution from vehicles, brick kilns and factories, water pollution from untreated sewage and industrial waste, and a shortage of formal jobs, so many people work in the informal sector as street vendors, rickshaw pullers or waste pickers. Dhaka regularly ranks among the most polluted and congested cities in the world. However, squatter settlements are also places of enterprise and community: Dharavi in Mumbai has thousands of small businesses, including recycling, pottery and leather work, with an estimated annual turnover of hundreds of millions of US dollars.

  • Housing: squatter settlements, overcrowding, no legal tenure.
  • Services: lack of clean water, sanitation, electricity and waste collection.
  • Economy: unemployment, informal sector work, low pay.
  • Environment: air and water pollution, flooding, traffic congestion.
08

Urban Problems and Solutions in HICs

HIC cities face different problems. Inner-city areas may suffer decline when older industries close, leaving derelict land, unemployment, poor-quality housing and crime. Traffic congestion, air pollution, high house prices and homelessness are common, and urban sprawl onto the rural-urban fringe destroys farmland and green space. Solutions include urban regeneration, in which old industrial areas are redeveloped: London Docklands was transformed from derelict docks into a financial and residential district including Canary Wharf from the 1980s onwards.

Transport solutions include congestion charging, as in London since 2003 and Singapore's electronic road pricing since 1998, park-and-ride schemes, trams and metro systems. Green belts, first established around London in the 1940s and 1950s, restrict urban sprawl. Brownfield sites, previously developed land, are reused to reduce pressure on greenfield sites. Sustainable cities aim to reduce waste, energy use and car dependence while providing a good quality of life for residents.

09

The Rural-Urban Fringe and Urban Sprawl

The rural-urban fringe is the zone at the edge of a city where urban and rural land uses meet. It is attractive for development because land is cheaper, more spacious and accessible by ring roads and motorways. Land uses include out-of-town shopping centres, business and science parks, airports, housing estates, golf courses, sewage works and landfill sites. Urban sprawl is the uncontrolled outward spread of the built-up area into the surrounding countryside.

Sprawl brings loss of farmland and wildlife habitats, increased car use and commuting, more traffic and pollution, and the merging of separate settlements. It also increases flood risk because impermeable surfaces replace fields. In Dhaka, sprawl has led to the filling in of wetlands and low-lying land to the north and east of the city, reducing natural flood storage. Planning controls, higher-density building and brownfield redevelopment are used to reduce sprawl.

Worked Examples

Case study: Rapid urban growth in Dhaka, Bangladesh

For a named city in an LIC or MIC, explain the causes of its rapid growth and describe the problems this has created. [7 marks]

  1. Growth: Dhaka's metropolitan population has grown from about 400,000 in 1950 to over 23 million today, adding several hundred thousand people each year.
  2. Causes: rural-urban migration driven by landlessness, low farm wages, river erosion and cyclones in rural areas; pull of about 4 million jobs in the garment industry, government jobs as the capital city, and better schools and hospitals; plus high natural increase among young migrants.
  3. Housing problems: several million people live in informal settlements such as Korail, built on government land beside Gulshan Lake, where tin and bamboo houses are overcrowded and at risk of fire and eviction.
  4. Service problems: many households share toilets and water points, and waterlogging after monsoon rain disrupts the city because drains are blocked and wetlands have been filled.
  5. Environmental problems: severe air pollution from brick kilns, vehicles and construction dust; the Buriganga River was heavily polluted by tannery waste from Hazaribagh until tanneries were relocated to Savar from 2017.
  6. Transport: severe traffic congestion; the Dhaka Metro Rail (MRT Line 6), opened from December 2022, and the Dhaka Elevated Expressway are attempts to reduce this.
Dhaka has grown rapidly because of rural-urban migration and natural increase. This has created squatter settlements such as Korail, inadequate water and sanitation, flooding, air and river pollution, and traffic congestion, which the government is beginning to address through metro rail, tannery relocation and housing projects.

Case study: Improving squatter settlements - Rocinha, Rio de Janeiro and self-help schemes

Evaluate strategies used to improve the quality of life in squatter settlements. Use named examples. [7 marks]

  1. Background: Rocinha is one of the largest favelas in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, built on steep hillsides with an estimated population of 70,000 to over 100,000.
  2. Strategy 1 - Upgrading (site and service/self-help): the Favela-Bairro project from the 1990s paved roads, installed water, sewerage and electricity, and built health centres, while residents improved their own homes, often replacing wood with brick. This is cheaper than rebuilding and keeps communities together.
  3. Strategy 2 - Security and tenure: Pacifying Police Units (UPPs) from 2008 aimed to remove drug gangs; giving residents legal land titles encourages them to invest in their homes.
  4. Strategy 3 - Relocation and rebuilding: some governments demolish slums and rehouse residents in apartment blocks, such as the Minha Casa Minha Vida programme in Brazil from 2009, but new housing is often far from jobs and breaks up communities.
  5. Evaluation: upgrading and self-help are generally most effective and affordable, but they need government funding and community involvement; security measures in Rocinha had mixed success as violence returned after 2013.
Self-help and upgrading schemes such as Favela-Bairro are the most sustainable because they provide services cheaply while keeping residents near jobs and communities. Relocation and police action can help but often have negative side effects, so a combination of legal tenure, basic services and community participation works best.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Confusing site and situation. Site is the land itself (dry point, bridging point); situation is the position relative to surrounding areas and routes.
  • Defining urbanisation as the growth of cities. It is an increase in the proportion of people living in urban areas, which can happen even when total population is stable.
  • Describing all squatter settlements as purely negative. Mention their enterprise, community spirit and role in providing cheap housing close to jobs.
  • Applying the Burgess model to LIC cities without adjustment. In many LIC cities the wealthy live near the CBD and squatter settlements are on the periphery.
  • Mixing up threshold and range. Threshold is the number of customers needed; range is the distance people will travel.
  • Writing about a city without facts. Always include figures and named areas, such as Korail in Dhaka, Dharavi in Mumbai or Rocinha in Rio.

Exam tips

  • For map questions on site, look for contour patterns, rivers, bridges and road junctions, and use map evidence with grid references.
  • Structure urban problem answers under housing, services, economy, transport and environment to cover a range quickly.
  • When evaluating solutions, say who benefits, what it costs and whether it is sustainable in the long term.
  • Use the words threshold, range and sphere of influence in hierarchy questions, as examiners look for them.
  • Know one LIC city (Dhaka, Mumbai or Rio) and one HIC urban solution (London Docklands, congestion charge or green belt) with specific facts.
  • In compare questions about land use, use comparative words such as higher, denser, older and more expensive, not two separate descriptions.

Model answerBuildings arranged in a line (1). Reason: along a road, river, levée, embankment or valley floor for access or to avoid flooding (1).

Model answerThreshold is the minimum number of people needed to support a service (1); range is the maximum distance people will travel to use a service (1); high-order services have high thresholds and large ranges (1).

Model answerLarger populations provide the high thresholds needed for high-order services (1); large sphere of influence attracts customers from a wide area (1); good transport links make them accessible (1); high-order goods have large ranges so people travel to them (1). Maximum 3.

Model answerHighest land values (1); tall, high-density buildings (1); offices, banks, department stores and entertainment (1); most accessible point with road and rail focus (1); few residents (1); congestion and pedestrianised streets (1). Maximum 4.

Quick Recap

  1. Site is the land a settlement is built on; situation is its location relative to surrounding features; function is its purpose.
  2. Rural settlement patterns can be dispersed, nucleated or linear, each with physical and human causes.
  3. Settlement hierarchy is explained by threshold, range and sphere of influence.
  4. HIC cities often follow a concentric pattern with a CBD at the centre; LIC cities often have squatter settlements on the edge.
  5. Rapid urbanisation in LICs is caused by rural-urban migration and high natural increase.
  6. Urban problems include squatter settlements, poor services, pollution and congestion, as seen in Dhaka.
  7. Solutions include self-help upgrading, regeneration, congestion charging, public transport and green belts.
  8. The rural-urban fringe is under pressure from urban sprawl, which destroys farmland and habitats.
