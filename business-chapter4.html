Business Growth and Size

How business size is measured, why and how businesses grow internally and externally, the problems growth can cause, why small businesses survive, and why some businesses fail.

01

Overview

Businesses come in every size, from one-person enterprises to global corporations employing hundreds of thousands of people. Many owners want their businesses to grow, because growth can bring lower costs, higher profits and greater security, but growth also brings risks and management problems. The Cambridge syllabus expects you to compare methods of measuring size, distinguish between internal and external growth, explain the different types of merger and takeover, and analyse why small businesses continue to thrive and why some businesses fail. Case-study questions often ask which method of growth a business should use, so you must be able to weigh up the advantages and drawbacks of each option.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Describe the main methods of measuring business size and explain their limitations.
  • Explain why the owners of a business may want it to grow.
  • Distinguish between internal and external growth.
  • Explain horizontal, vertical (backward and forward) and conglomerate integration, with their advantages and disadvantages.
  • Explain the problems linked to business growth and how they can be overcome.
  • Explain why many businesses stay small and why some businesses fail.
02

Measuring the Size of a Business

There is no single perfect measure of business size, so several methods are used. The number of employees is simple to understand and compare, but it can be misleading: a highly automated chemical plant may produce huge output with few workers, while a labour-intensive restaurant chain employs many people but may be smaller in terms of value. Value of output measures how much the business produces in money terms, while sales revenue (turnover) measures the value of goods and services sold.

Capital employed is the total amount of long-term finance invested in the business. It is useful for comparing capital-intensive businesses, but a business with high capital employed may still employ few people. Market share measures the business's sales as a percentage of total market sales, which shows its importance within its industry, although a small business in a small niche market may have a high market share. Profit is not a good measure of size, because profit depends on efficiency and market conditions as well as size: a small business can be very profitable and a large business can make a loss.

Because each measure has weaknesses, it is best to use more than one method when comparing businesses, particularly businesses in different industries.

  • Number of employees – simple, but ignores capital intensity.
  • Sales revenue – useful, but varies with prices and markets.
  • Capital employed – good for capital-intensive firms, ignores labour.
  • Market share – shows position in the market, depends on market size.
  • Value of output – shows production, but different from sales.
Key fact:Profit is not a measure of business size.
03

Why Owners Want to Grow

Owners and managers often aim for growth for several reasons. Larger businesses can benefit from economies of scale, such as bulk-buying discounts and cheaper finance, which reduce average costs and allow lower prices or higher profits. Growth can increase market share, giving the business more influence over prices and more bargaining power with suppliers and retailers. Growing businesses may also be more secure, because they can spread risk across different products and markets and are less likely to be forced out by competitors.

Growth can also satisfy the personal ambitions of owners and managers. Managers of larger businesses usually receive higher salaries and greater status. For shareholders, growth can increase the value of their shares and the size of future dividends.

04

Internal Growth

Internal (organic) growth happens when a business expands its existing operations. This may involve opening new branches or factories, increasing production capacity, developing new products, entering new markets, or selling online. Internal growth is usually funded by retained profits or loans.

The advantages of internal growth are that it is relatively low risk, it can be carefully controlled, and the business's culture and management style are maintained because no new organisation is being absorbed. The main disadvantage is that it is slow: it can take many years to build a large business this way. It may also be limited by the amount of finance available and by the size of the existing market.

05

External Growth: Mergers and Takeovers

External growth occurs when a business grows by joining with or buying another business. A merger is when two businesses agree to join together to form a single new business, usually with the agreement of both sets of owners. A takeover (acquisition) is when one business buys a controlling interest, more than 50% of the shares, in another business. Takeovers can be friendly or hostile, where the target company's directors oppose the bid. External growth is much faster than internal growth, but it is often expensive and can cause problems when two different organisations are combined.

Horizontal integration is when two businesses in the same industry and at the same stage of production join together, such as two supermarket chains or two car manufacturers. Benefits include reduced competition, increased market share, economies of scale and the opportunity to cut costs by removing duplicated activities. Governments may investigate large horizontal mergers because they could lead to monopoly and higher prices for consumers.

Vertical integration is when businesses at different stages of production in the same industry join together. Backward vertical integration is when a business joins with a supplier, such as a car manufacturer buying a steel producer; this guarantees supplies, controls their quality and cost, and may deny supplies to competitors. Forward vertical integration is when a business joins with a business closer to the consumer, such as a clothing manufacturer buying a chain of clothing shops; this guarantees retail outlets, allows the business to capture the retailer's profit margin and gives it direct information about customers. Conglomerate integration is when businesses in completely unrelated industries join together, such as a food company buying a hotel chain. This spreads risk through diversification, but managers may lack experience in the new industry.

06

Problems of Growth and How to Overcome Them

Growth can create serious problems. Communication becomes more difficult as more layers of management are added and messages must pass through more people, which can cause delays and misunderstandings. Coordination and control are harder, because managers cannot supervise all activities directly. Workers may feel less valued in a very large organisation, which can reduce motivation. These problems can lead to diseconomies of scale, where average costs rise as the business grows.

Growth can also create financial problems. Expansion often requires large amounts of finance, and if too much is borrowed, interest payments can threaten the business. Rapid expansion may cause cash-flow problems because money is spent on new premises, stock and staff before extra revenue is received. Mergers and takeovers bring their own difficulties: different corporate cultures may clash, some workers may be made redundant, and the combined business may be too large to manage effectively.

Businesses can reduce these problems by delegating authority to managers of divisions or regional branches, improving communication systems using technology, running training and team-building programmes after a merger, and planning finance carefully using cash-flow forecasts.

  • Poor communication – solution: improve IT systems and reduce layers of hierarchy.
  • Loss of control – solution: decentralise and delegate to divisional managers.
  • Low motivation – solution: team working, recognition and clear communication.
  • Cash shortages – solution: cash-flow forecasting and suitable long-term finance.
  • Culture clashes after mergers – solution: consultation and joint training.
07

Why Some Businesses Remain Small

Despite the benefits of growth, most businesses in every country are small. One reason is the type of industry: services such as hairdressing, plumbing, private tutoring and car repairs are personal services where customers value individual attention, so there is little benefit from being large. Another reason is market size: businesses serving small niche markets or local rural communities cannot grow large because demand is limited.

Owners' objectives also matter. Many owners prefer to stay small so that they keep full control, avoid the stress of managing a large business and maintain a good work-life balance. Small businesses may also lack access to finance needed for expansion. Small firms have advantages: they can offer personal service, respond flexibly and quickly to changes in customer demand, and often have lower overheads. Governments support small firms because they create jobs, encourage competition and innovation, and may grow into large businesses of the future.

08

Why Businesses Fail

Many new and small businesses fail within their first few years. Internal causes include poor management, such as a lack of experience or poor planning; a lack of finance and poor cash-flow management, which leaves the business unable to pay its debts; failure to plan for change; and poor location, which may mean too few customers. Over-expansion can also cause failure if a business grows faster than its finance or management capacity allows.

External causes include increased competition from larger businesses or online retailers, changes in consumer tastes or technology that make the product obsolete, economic recession that reduces consumer spending, and rising costs of supplies or interest rates. New businesses are particularly vulnerable because they have few regular customers, little experience and limited cash reserves.

Worked Examples

Identifying types of integration

FreshBake plc, a large bread manufacturer, is considering three options: (A) buying a flour mill, (B) buying a rival bread manufacturer, (C) buying a chain of bakery cafés. Identify the type of integration in each case and state one benefit of each.

  1. Option A: the flour mill supplies FreshBake with a raw material, so this is backward vertical integration. Benefit: guaranteed supply of flour at cost price and control over its quality.
  2. Option B: the rival is in the same industry at the same stage of production, so this is horizontal integration. Benefit: increased market share and economies of scale, such as bulk buying.
  3. Option C: the cafés sell bread to final consumers, so this is forward vertical integration. Benefit: guaranteed retail outlets and access to the retail profit margin.
  4. Consider risks: option B may be investigated by competition authorities; options A and C require managing unfamiliar activities.
A is backward vertical, B is horizontal and C is forward vertical integration, each with distinct benefits for supply security, market share and access to customers.

Evaluating internal versus external growth

StyleUp Ltd owns 12 fashion shops and wants to double in size within three years. It could open 12 new shops itself or take over a rival chain with 14 shops for $6 million. Recommend which method StyleUp should use.

  1. Internal growth: lower risk, StyleUp controls location and design of each shop and keeps its culture; however, opening 12 shops in three years requires finding sites, recruiting and training staff, which is slow and may not meet the deadline.
  2. Takeover: immediately doubles the number of shops, removes a competitor and brings experienced staff and established customers.
  3. Takeover risks: $6 million is expensive and may require borrowing; the rival's shops may be in poor locations; staff from different cultures may resist change.
  4. Consider the deadline: the objective is to double in three years, which favours the faster method.
  5. Judgement: the takeover is justified if StyleUp can finance it without excessive debt and if the rival's shops are profitable.
The takeover is more likely to achieve the three-year target, but StyleUp should investigate the rival's profitability and plan staff integration carefully; if finance is too risky, it should grow internally at a slower pace.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Using profit as a measure of business size. Profit reflects efficiency and market conditions, not size.
  • Confusing mergers and takeovers. A merger is agreed by both businesses; a takeover involves buying a controlling share.
  • Mixing up backward and forward vertical integration. Backward is towards suppliers; forward is towards customers.
  • Assuming growth always reduces costs; beyond a certain size, diseconomies of scale can raise average costs.
  • Forgetting that internal growth is slow but low risk, while external growth is fast but costly and risky.
  • Explaining business failure only with 'lack of money' without identifying the underlying cause, such as poor cash-flow planning.

Exam tips

  • When identifying integration types, ask: same stage (horizontal), supplier (backward), customer (forward) or unrelated (conglomerate)?
  • In recommendation questions, link your choice to the business's objective and deadline, as well as its finance.
  • Explain why a measure of size can be misleading, using a capital-intensive versus labour-intensive example.
  • Always give the benefit to the business, not just a definition, when explaining integration types.
  • For 'why small firms survive' questions, mention personal service, niche markets, owner objectives and flexibility.
  • Link problems of growth to diseconomies of scale and suggest a realistic solution for each problem.

Model answerAny two from: number of employees, sales revenue, capital employed, market share, value of output.
[2 marks]

2. Define takeover.

Model answerWhen one business buys more than 50% of the shares in another business, giving it control.
[2 marks]

3. What is meant by conglomerate integration?

Model answerWhen two businesses in completely unrelated industries join together, diversifying their activities.
[3 marks]

4. Explain why the number of employees may not be a good measure of business size.

Model answerSome businesses are capital-intensive, using machinery rather than workers, so they may have high output and sales with few employees. Comparing them with labour-intensive firms using employee numbers would give a misleading result.
[4 marks]

5. Explain two advantages of internal growth.

Model answerIt is lower risk than a takeover because the business expands gradually using known methods. The business keeps its existing culture and management style, avoiding clashes between different organisations. (2 marks each.)
[4 marks]

6. Explain two benefits of backward vertical integration for a chocolate manufacturer.

Model answerBuying a cocoa supplier guarantees supplies of cocoa, so production is not disrupted by shortages. The manufacturer can control the quality and cost of cocoa and absorb the supplier's profit margin, reducing costs. (2 marks each.)
[4 marks]

7. Explain two problems that a business might face as it grows.

Model answerCommunication becomes slower and less effective because messages pass through more layers of management, causing mistakes. Cash-flow problems may arise because money is spent on expansion before extra revenue comes in. (2 marks each.)
[4 marks]

8. Explain two reasons why small businesses continue to exist.

Model answerThey can offer personal, flexible service that customers value, such as a local hairdresser. Some markets are too small for large firms to enter profitably, such as niche or local markets. (2 marks each.)
[4 marks]

9. Explain two reasons why a new business might fail.

Model answerPoor cash-flow management means the business cannot pay suppliers and wages even if it is profitable. Lack of experience leads to poor decisions about pricing, location or stock. (2 marks each.)
[3 marks]

10. Explain why a government might investigate a merger between two large supermarkets.

Model answerA horizontal merger could reduce competition and create a dominant business with a very large market share. It might then raise prices, reduce choice or put pressure on suppliers, harming consumers.
[8 marks]

11. SunCola plc, a soft drinks maker, is considering either a takeover of a rival soft drinks company or a takeover of a chain of cinemas. Discuss which option would be better for SunCola. Justify your answer.

Model answerRival soft drinks company (horizontal): increases market share, removes a competitor, gives economies of scale in bottling and distribution, and managers understand the industry; however, it may be investigated by competition authorities and cultures may clash. Cinema chain (conglomerate): diversifies risk, so falling drinks sales would not damage the whole business, and cinemas could stock SunCola drinks; however, managers lack experience in cinemas and the benefit of cost savings is small. Judgement: the horizontal takeover is likely better because SunCola's expertise lies in soft drinks and cost savings are larger, unless the drinks market is declining, in which case diversification reduces risk. 14

Quick Recap

  1. Business size can be measured by employees, sales revenue, capital employed, market share or value of output, but not profit.
  2. Businesses grow to gain economies of scale, market share, security and higher rewards for owners and managers.
  3. Internal growth is slow but low risk; external growth through mergers and takeovers is fast but costly and risky.
  4. Horizontal integration joins firms at the same stage; vertical integration joins firms at different stages; conglomerate joins unrelated firms.
  5. Growth can cause communication, control, motivation and cash-flow problems that may lead to diseconomies of scale.
  6. Many businesses stay small because of personal services, small markets, owner preferences and limited finance.
  7. Businesses fail because of poor management, poor cash flow, competition, changing tastes and economic recession.
