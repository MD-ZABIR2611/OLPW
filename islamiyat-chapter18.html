Chapter 18 · Islamiyat

Islam in the Contemporary World

Islamiyat in the modern world means reasoning from sources to new situations. This chapter covers contemporary applications — Islamic finance, family and medical ethics, extremism and coexistence — with balanced, source-based argument.

Key Concepts

01

Riba (interest on loans) is prohibited by clear Qur'anic texts because it transfers risk unfairly and burdens the poor; Islamic finance replaces it with profit-sharing (mudaraba), cost-plus sale (murabaha) and leasing (ijara).

02

Family law in Islam rests on the marriage contract (nikah) as a solemn covenant: rights and duties of spouses are mutual, divorce (talaq) is permitted but "most disliked of permitted things", and reconciliation is urged at every stage.

03

Medical ethics draw on the sanctity of life: Islam forbids killing and suicide, so euthanasia is not permitted, while organ donation is allowed by many scholars as an act of saving life and many more permit it with consent, citing "whosoever saves a life, it is as if he saved all mankind".

04

On new questions — genetic testing, life support, vaccination — scholars weigh texts against maslaha (public welfare) and the prevention of harm, showing fiqh's continuing vitality through contemporary ijtihad and bodies such as fiqh academies.

05

Muslims are commanded to peaceful coexistence: the Qur'an states "there is no compulsion in religion" and honours the People of the Book; extremist violence is rejected as violating the sanctity of life and the prohibition of killing innocents.

06

Contemporary Muslim life balances identity and citizenship: participation in non-Muslim societies is encouraged where justice and common welfare (serving one's neighbour, honesty in trade, loyalty to covenants) are observed, following the Prophet's own covenants.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
RibaInterest or usury on loans, prohibited in Islam because it unjustly grows wealth from debt and burdens the needy.
MudarabaA profit-sharing partnership in Islamic finance where one party provides capital and the other labour, sharing agreed profits.
MurabahaA cost-plus financing sale permitted in Islamic finance: the bank buys goods and resells at a declared markup, avoiding interest.
NikahThe Islamic marriage contract, a solemn covenant establishing mutual rights and duties between husband and wife.
EuthanasiaThe deliberate ending of a suffering life, not permitted in Islam because life belongs to God and killing is forbidden.
MaslahaPublic welfare or benefit, a juristic principle used to decide modern ethical questions where texts give no direct ruling.
No compulsion in religionThe Qur'anic principle (2:256) that faith cannot be forced, grounding religious freedom and coexistence.
Fiqh academyA contemporary body of scholars that issues collective rulings on new ethical and legal questions facing Muslims.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Structure contemporary-issues answers as "principle — application — balance": state the Qur'anic or Prophetic principle, apply it to the modern case, then acknowledge the counter-consideration before concluding — this mirrors the evaluation marks scheme and avoids one-sided answers.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

State the Islamic ruling on riba and name two Islamic finance contracts designed to avoid it.

Model answerRiba, interest on loans, is prohibited (1). Alternatives include mudaraba (profit-sharing) (1) and murabaha (cost-plus sale) or ijara (leasing) (1).
CHALLENGE

A hospital asks whether a Muslim patient in a permanent coma may have life support withdrawn. Discuss the considerations an Islamic ethicist would weigh, and give a reasoned conclusion.

Model answerSanctity of life: killing and suicide are forbidden, and life is a trust from God, so active withdrawal intending death cannot be permitted (1). Duty of care: the Qur'an praises saving life and forbids abandoning the sick, so nutrition, hygiene and treatment must continue while any hope remains (1). Consideration of certainty and burden: if qualified doctors agree brain-stem death has irretrievably occurred, many fiqh academies treat further machines as futile treatment that may cease, distinguishing it from killing — the family's consent and medical certainty are essential (1). Conclusion: one may not intend or cause death, but when death is medically certain, continued futile machinery may be stopped with proper authority, preserving both the sanctity of life and mercy (1).
