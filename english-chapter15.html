Comprehension: Inference, Tone and Attitude

Top-band comprehension answers go beyond the literal. This chapter trains you to detect what a writer suggests rather than states, name the tone precisely, and prove every inference from the text.

Key Concepts

01

An inference is a conclusion drawn from evidence in the text combined with logic; in answers, state the inference first, then quote the exact words that imply it.

02

Tone is the writer's attitude conveyed through word choice — e.g. ironic, dismissive, affectionate, sceptical — and must be supported by specific quoted language, not by gut feeling.

03

Attitude and bias are revealed by loaded adjectives, selective detail, exaggeration, generalisation and what a writer chooses to omit; spotting omission is a high-level skill.

04

Deductions about characters or situations should use P-E structure: point, then quoted evidence, then the explanation of how the wording creates the implied meaning.

05

Questions asking "what do you learn" or "what is suggested" require answers in your own words; copying the text without reformulation earns little credit.

06

Distinguish fact from opinion in a passage: verifiable claims from claims that signal belief with modal verbs (might, surely) or evaluative vocabulary.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
InferenceA conclusion about meaning that is implied by the text rather than directly stated, supported by evidence and reasoning.
ToneThe writer's attitude towards the subject or audience, conveyed through word choice, punctuation and sentence structure.
ImplicationA meaning suggested by the wording without being explicitly said, which the reader must deduce.
Loaded languageWords chosen to provoke an emotional response and influence the reader rather than to inform neutrally.
BiasA one-sided presentation of a topic that unfairly favours one viewpoint by selective or weighted evidence.
ConnotationThe associations a word carries beyond its dictionary meaning, used by writers to shape tone.
FactA statement that can be verified as true or false against evidence.
OpinionA personal belief or judgement that cannot be proven true or false and often signals itself with evaluative language.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

When a question asks about tone or attitude, begin the answer with a precise adjective ("the tone is contemptuous"), then quote the exact phrase that creates it, then explain the mechanism — this three-step habit converts impression marks into secure points.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

A passage describes a politician's promise as "yet another dazzling fantasy from a man who never delivers". Identify the writer's tone and quote the words that create it.

Model answerThe tone is sceptical and mocking (1). "Dazzling fantasy" mocks the promise as unrealistic and showy (1); "never delivers" states a pattern of failure that creates contempt (1).
CHALLENGE

Explain how a reader can tell that a writer is biased even when no opinion is stated openly, referring to two techniques.

Model answerSelective evidence: the writer includes only supporting examples and omits counter-evidence, e.g. praising a policy's winners while ignoring its losers, which skews the picture without stating a view (1). Loaded language and exaggeration: adjectives such as "disastrous" or "heroic" embed the judgement inside apparently factual reporting, steering the reader towards the writer's conclusion (1). Also accept: generalisation, appeal to emotion, or misrepresenting opponents.
