Reflection

What reflection means in Global Perspectives, how to reflect on changes in your perspective, your research and your collaboration, and how to write specific, evaluative reflection for the Individual Report and Team Project.

01

Overview

Reflection is the skill that many students find hardest, yet it is assessed directly as AO2 and appears in both the Individual Report and the Team Project. Reflection is not a diary of what you did; it is thinking critically about what you learned, how your views changed, what influenced them, and how you would improve in future. Examiners can quickly tell the difference between vague statements such as 'I learned a lot' and genuine, evidence-based reflection. This chapter explains models of reflection and shows how to write reflection that is specific, honest and evaluative.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain what reflection is and how it differs from description.
  • Use a reflective model such as What? So what? Now what? to structure reflection.
  • Reflect on how research and other perspectives have influenced your own viewpoint.
  • Reflect on your personal contribution to teamwork and the effect of collaboration.
  • Identify strengths, weaknesses and improvements in your research and skills.
  • Write specific, evidence-based reflection suitable for coursework.
02

What Is Reflection?

Reflection means looking back on an experience or a piece of learning in order to understand it better and to improve in future. In Global Perspectives, you reflect on three main things: how your personal perspective on an issue has developed through research; how working with others has affected your thinking and the outcome; and how effective your research and other skills were.

Reflection differs from description. Description says what happened: 'We made a poster about recycling.' Reflection explains why it matters and what was learned: 'Designing the poster showed me that many students did not understand which materials could be recycled, which changed my view that people simply do not care about waste. The real problem was lack of information.' The second version shows insight into how thinking changed.

Reflection also involves self-awareness. It asks you to recognise your own assumptions and biases, such as views you held because of your family, culture or the media you consume. Being willing to question your own position, and explaining why you did or did not change it, shows intellectual maturity.

Key fact:Description tells what happened; reflection explains what it meant and how it changed your thinking or practice.
03

Models of Reflection

A simple and effective model is What? So what? Now what? 'What?' describes the experience briefly. 'So what?' explains its significance: what you learned, how you felt, and how it affected your views or work. 'Now what?' looks forward: what you will do differently, what further research you might do, or how you will act on your new understanding.

Another widely used model is Gibbs' reflective cycle, which has six stages: description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion and action plan. It encourages you to consider not only what happened but also your emotional response, what was good and bad about the experience, why things happened as they did, what else you could have done and what you will do next time.

You do not need to name a model in your coursework, but using one as a mental structure prevents your reflection becoming purely descriptive. Keep the description short and spend most of your words on significance and future improvement.

  • What? Briefly describe the experience or learning.
  • So what? Explain its significance and what you learned.
  • Now what? State what you will do differently or explore further.
  • Gibbs' cycle: Description, Feelings, Evaluation, Analysis, Conclusion, Action plan.
04

Reflecting on Your Perspective

A key part of the Individual Report is showing how your own perspective has been affected by your research. Begin by stating your initial view honestly, then explain which evidence or perspectives challenged, confirmed or refined it. Name the specific source or argument that influenced you and explain why it was persuasive.

Your view does not have to change completely. Reflection can show that your view was confirmed but became better informed, or that it became more nuanced. For example, a student might start believing that foreign aid is always beneficial, then discover evidence of aid dependency and corruption, and conclude that aid is valuable when targeted and monitored carefully. This shift from a simple view to a qualified one is excellent reflection.

Also consider how your background influences your perspective. A student living in a country dependent on oil exports may view fossil fuel restrictions differently from a student in a low-lying island nation threatened by sea-level rise. Recognising this shows you understand that perspectives are shaped by circumstances.

Key fact:Show the journey: initial view → evidence encountered → how and why your view changed or was refined.
05

Reflecting on Collaboration

In the Team Project, you reflect on the process of working with others and on your personal contribution. Consider what role you played, what you contributed, how the team made decisions and handled difficulties, and how successful the outcome was. Always support comments with specific examples from your team's work.

Importantly, reflect on how collaboration affected your thinking. Did a teammate's research on another country give you a new perspective? Did discussing possible outcomes make you see practical problems you had missed? Did disagreement force you to justify your views more carefully? Explaining these effects shows the value of collaboration beyond simply sharing the workload.

Balanced reflection includes both strengths and weaknesses of the team and of yourself. Avoid blaming others. If a teammate did not contribute, focus on how the team responded, how you adapted and what you would do differently next time, such as agreeing responsibilities in writing at the start.

06

Reflecting on Research and Skills

You should also reflect on how well you carried out your research. Consider whether your research question was well chosen, whether your sources were reliable and varied, whether you found enough evidence for each perspective, and whether your primary research methods worked. Identify specific limitations, such as relying mostly on sources from one country or having a small survey sample.

Skills reflection means identifying which skills you developed and which need further work. Perhaps you became better at evaluating sources by checking authors and dates, or improved your time management by setting weekly targets. Perhaps you still find it difficult to write concise conclusions. Be specific about what improved and how you know.

The best skills reflection links to future action. For example: 'Because many of my sources were from European organisations, my report underrepresented perspectives from Africa. In future research I will deliberately search for reports from African governments and NGOs to make my perspectives more genuinely global.'

07

Writing Effective Reflection

Effective reflection is written in the first person, is honest, and focuses on learning. Use phrases such as 'I initially believed', 'this challenged my view because', 'I now recognise', 'on reflection', 'if I repeated this I would' and 'this taught me'. These phrases signal to the examiner that you are reflecting rather than describing.

Specificity is the key to high marks. Compare 'Research changed my views' with 'Reading the 2022 World Bank data showing that extreme poverty had increased during the pandemic made me realise that progress in reducing poverty can be reversed by global shocks, so I became more supportive of international safety-net programmes.' The second version names evidence, explains the change and states its effect.

Keep reflection proportionate. It should be substantial enough to show genuine insight, but it should not replace analysis and evaluation elsewhere in your work. In the Individual Report, reflection is often most effective near the end, after your conclusion, although brief reflective comments can also appear throughout.

  • Be specific: name sources, events and examples.
  • Be honest: include genuine difficulties and mistakes.
  • Be analytical: explain why things happened and why views changed.
  • Be forward-looking: suggest realistic improvements.
  • Use first-person reflective language.

Worked Examples

Turning description into reflection

Improve this weak reflection: 'In our project we did a survey and made a video about food waste. It went well and I learned a lot about food waste.'

  1. Identify the problem: it only describes activities and uses vague claims ('went well', 'learned a lot').
  2. Add specific learning: what did the survey reveal? For example, 45% of students threw away part of their lunch daily.
  3. Explain significance and a change in perspective: the student previously thought food waste was mainly a supermarket problem.
  4. Add evaluation: what worked and what did not in the video?
  5. Add a forward-looking action.
'Our survey showed that 45% of students threw away part of their lunch every day, which surprised me because I had assumed food waste was mainly caused by supermarkets. This made me realise individual habits matter too. Our video was watched by many students, but we did not measure whether behaviour changed. Next time I would repeat the survey afterwards to judge our impact.'

Reflecting on a change of perspective

A student researched 'Should wealthy countries cancel the debts of low-income countries?' Write a reflective paragraph showing how their perspective developed.

  1. State the initial view: 'I initially thought debt cancellation was always right because it frees money for health and education.'
  2. Identify influential evidence: the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative, which was followed by increased spending on poverty reduction in several countries.
  3. Identify a challenging perspective: economists who argue that cancellation without reform can encourage further borrowing or benefit corrupt governments.
  4. Explain the refined view: support for cancellation with conditions for transparency.
  5. Link to future action: seek views from citizens in indebted countries.
'I initially believed debt cancellation was always right. Evidence from the HIPC initiative confirmed that relief can increase spending on health and education, but economists' warnings about renewed borrowing and corruption made me realise it is not a complete solution. I now support cancellation linked to transparency measures. In future I would include more perspectives from citizens in indebted countries, as most of my sources came from international organisations.'

Applying What? So what? Now what? to teamwork

Use the What? So what? Now what? model to reflect on a team that missed its deadline for completing a campaign.

  1. What? 'Our team finished our awareness campaign one week late because the poster design took longer than planned.'
  2. So what? 'I realised we had not broken tasks into smaller deadlines, and as coordinator I did not check progress regularly. The delay meant we had less time to evaluate impact.'
  3. Now what? 'In future I would create an action plan with weekly checkpoints and hold short progress meetings.'
The reflection is effective because it briefly describes the problem, analyses its cause and the student's own responsibility, explains its consequence, and proposes specific, realistic improvements.
10

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Writing description of what happened instead of reflection on what was learned and why it matters.
  • Using vague phrases such as 'I learned a lot' or 'it went well' without specific evidence.
  • Claiming your view changed without naming the evidence or perspective that changed it.
  • Blaming teammates rather than reflecting on how the team and you responded to difficulties.
  • Only mentioning positives; balanced reflection includes weaknesses and improvements.
  • Leaving reflection until the end of a project and relying on memory; keep a learning journal throughout.

Exam tips

  • Keep a learning journal and note moments when your opinion shifted and what caused it.
  • Use reflective phrases such as 'I initially believed', 'this challenged my view' and 'next time I would'.
  • Name at least one specific source or teammate contribution that influenced your perspective.
  • Spend most of your words on 'so what' and 'now what' rather than describing what happened.
  • Recognise how your background or confirmation bias may have shaped your initial view.
  • Make improvements specific and realistic, such as piloting questionnaires or setting weekly deadlines.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. Define reflection in the context of Global Perspectives.

Model answerThinking critically about experiences and learning (1) to understand how your views and skills have changed and how to improve in future (1).
[3 marks]

2. What are the three stages of the What? So what? Now what? model?

Model answerWhat?: describe the experience (1). So what?: explain its significance or what was learned (1). Now what?: identify future actions or improvements (1).
[3 marks]

3. State three things you should reflect on in Global Perspectives coursework.

Model answerAny three: how your personal perspective changed (1); the influence of others' perspectives or teamwork (1); your contribution to the team (1); effectiveness of research methods and sources (1); skills developed (1); improvements for the future (1).
[3 marks]

4. Explain the difference between description and reflection, using an example.

Model answerDescription states what happened, e.g. 'We interviewed a councillor' (1). Reflection explains its significance and effect on learning (1), e.g. 'The interview showed me that budget limits restrict recycling schemes, which changed my view that the council did not care' (1).
[3 marks]

5. What is confirmation bias and why is it relevant to reflection?

Model answerThe tendency to seek or favour information that supports existing beliefs (1). Reflection helps you recognise whether you ignored evidence that challenged your view (1), leading to more balanced research (1).
[3 marks]

6. Name the six stages of Gibbs' reflective cycle.

Model answerDescription, Feelings, Evaluation, Analysis, Conclusion, Action plan. Award 1 mark for every two correct stages.
[3 marks]

7. Explain why it is acceptable for your view not to change after research.

Model answerReflection can show a view was confirmed (1) but is now better informed and supported by evidence (1), or became more nuanced with conditions (1), which still demonstrates learning.
[2 marks]

8. Give two examples of specific improvements a student might suggest after reflecting on their research.

Model answerAny two specific improvements, e.g. use sources from a wider range of countries (1); increase survey sample size or pilot questions (1); start research earlier with weekly targets (1); check reliability of sources more systematically (1).
[4 marks]

9. Explain two ways that collaboration can influence a student's perspective on an issue.

Model answerTeammates may research different countries or groups (1), introducing perspectives the student had not considered (1). Discussion and disagreement require the student to justify their view (1), exposing weaknesses or assumptions in their reasoning (1).
[3 marks]

10. Why is keeping a learning journal helpful for reflection?

Model answerIt records thoughts, decisions and changes in views at the time they happen (1), providing specific evidence (1) rather than relying on memory, which may be inaccurate (1).
[6 marks]

11. Evaluate this reflection: 'I worked hard on the project and my team was good. I think my views changed because I read a lot of sources. Next time I will do better.'

Model answerWeaknesses: vague and general, with no specific examples (1); does not identify which sources or evidence changed views (1); does not explain how views changed or from what to what (1); 'do better' is not a specific improvement (1); no evaluation of individual contribution or team challenges (1). Strength: attempts to address perspective and future action (1). Judgement: weak reflection, mostly unsupported claims (1). Maximum 6.
[8 marks]

12. Write a reflective paragraph (for an Individual Report on whether social media does more harm than good to young people) showing how your perspective developed. Explain what makes it effective.

Model answerParagraph should state an initial view (1); name specific evidence or a perspective that challenged or confirmed it, e.g. research linking heavy use to poor sleep, or evidence of online support communities (1-2); explain how and why the view changed or became nuanced (1-2); acknowledge a limitation or bias, e.g. sources mainly from one country (1); suggest further research or action (1). Explanation of effectiveness: specific, honest, analytical and forward-looking (1). Maximum 8.

Quick Recap

  1. Reflection means thinking critically about experiences to understand what was learned and how to improve.
  2. Reflection differs from description because it explains significance and changes in thinking.
  3. Models such as What? So what? Now what? and Gibbs' cycle help structure reflection.
  4. Show how research and others' perspectives influenced your view, naming specific evidence.
  5. Reflect honestly on your contribution to teamwork, including difficulties and how they were handled.
  6. Reflect on your research methods and skills, identifying limitations and specific improvements.
  7. Effective reflection is specific, honest, analytical and forward-looking.
