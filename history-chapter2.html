The Treaty of Versailles

The aims of the Big Three at the Paris Peace Conference, the terms imposed on Germany in 1919, German reactions and the long debate over whether the settlement was fair.

01

Overview

The Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919, was meant to end the First World War permanently, yet within twenty years Europe was at war again. It is the starting point of the Cambridge IGCSE and O-Level core content, and questions on it appear almost every year. You need to know what Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George each wanted, the main terms of the treaty, why Germans hated it, and whether it was a fair settlement given the circumstances of 1919. The best answers show that the peacemakers faced difficult compromises and public pressure rather than simply condemning them.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain the different aims of Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George at the Paris Peace Conference.
  • Describe the territorial, military, financial and war-guilt terms imposed on Germany.
  • Explain why the German people reacted with anger to the Diktat.
  • Outline the other peace treaties with Austria, Hungary, Bulgaria and Turkey.
  • Evaluate how far each of the Big Three was satisfied with the settlement.
  • Reach a supported judgement on whether the treaty was fair and whether it was too harsh or too lenient.
02

The Situation in 1919

The armistice that ended the fighting was signed on 11 November 1918. Europe had suffered appalling losses: around 9 million soldiers were dead, France had lost about 1.4 million men, and large areas of northern France and Belgium had been devastated. Germany had become a republic after Kaiser Wilhelm II abdicated on 9 November 1918, and its new leaders hoped for a peace based on President Wilson's Fourteen Points. Russia, in the middle of a civil war after the Bolshevik Revolution of 1917, was not invited to the peace talks.

The Paris Peace Conference opened in January 1919. Delegates from 32 countries attended, but the key decisions were made by the Big Three: Woodrow Wilson of the USA, Georges Clemenceau of France and David Lloyd George of Britain. Vittorio Orlando of Italy also took part but left for a time in anger when Italy's claims were refused. The defeated powers were not allowed to negotiate; Germany was simply presented with the terms in May 1919 and forced to sign.

Key fact:The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors, exactly five years after the Sarajevo assassination.
03

The Aims of the Big Three

Woodrow Wilson was an idealist who wanted a fair and lasting peace. His Fourteen Points, announced in January 1918, called for an end to secret treaties, freedom of the seas, free trade, disarmament and self-determination for the peoples of Europe, meaning that nations should rule themselves. His most important aim was a League of Nations to settle disputes peacefully. Wilson believed that punishing Germany too harshly would make it want revenge, although he did think Germany should be held accountable.

Georges Clemenceau, nicknamed 'the Tiger', wanted a harsh peace. France had been invaded twice by Germany, in 1870 and 1914, and had suffered the worst damage of any Western power. Clemenceau wanted revenge and, above all, security: he aimed to cripple Germany economically and militarily so that it could never attack France again. He demanded huge reparations, the return of Alsace-Lorraine and at first an independent Rhineland state as a buffer.

David Lloyd George stood in the middle. In the December 1918 general election he promised voters he would 'make Germany pay', and the British public wanted harsh punishment. Privately, however, he wanted a moderate peace because he saw Germany as an important trading partner and feared that a humiliated Germany might turn to communism, as Russia had. He was also keen to destroy the German navy and take Germany's colonies, which would protect the British Empire. In the Fontainebleau Memorandum of March 1919 he warned that an unjust peace would cause another war.

  • Wilson: League of Nations, self-determination, fair peace.
  • Clemenceau: revenge, security, a weak Germany.
  • Lloyd George: justice but not revenge, trade, naval and imperial security.
04

The Terms of the Treaty

The easiest way to remember the terms is under four headings: blame, reparations, armed forces and territory. Article 231, the War Guilt Clause, forced Germany to accept responsibility for causing all the loss and damage of the war. This clause provided the legal justification for reparations, the compensation Germany had to pay for war damage. The Reparations Commission fixed the total in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks, or £6,600 million, to be paid in instalments over many decades.

Germany's armed forces were severely restricted. The army was limited to 100,000 men and conscription was banned, so all soldiers had to be volunteers. Germany was not allowed tanks, military aircraft or submarines, and its navy was limited to six battleships. The Rhineland, the area of Germany bordering France, was demilitarised, meaning no German troops or fortifications were allowed there, and Allied troops would occupy the west bank of the Rhine for 15 years.

Germany lost around 13 per cent of its European territory and about six million people. Alsace-Lorraine returned to France; Eupen and Malmedy went to Belgium; North Schleswig went to Denmark after a plebiscite; West Prussia and Posen went to the new Poland, creating the Polish Corridor that separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany. Danzig became a Free City under the League, the Saar coalfield was run by the League for 15 years with France taking its coal, and Memel was placed under Allied control before Lithuania seized it in 1923. Germany's overseas colonies became mandates run by Britain, France and others on behalf of the League. Anschluss, union with Austria, was forbidden, and Germany was excluded from the League of Nations.

Key fact:Remember BRAT: Blame (Article 231), Reparations (£6,600 million), Army (100,000 men), Territory (about 13 per cent lost).
05

German Reactions

Germans across the political spectrum were outraged. They called the treaty a Diktat, a dictated peace, because Germany had not been allowed to take part in the negotiations and was threatened with renewed war if it did not sign. Many Germans did not believe they had been defeated militarily, since no Allied troops had invaded Germany in 1918, so they accepted the 'stab in the back' myth that socialists, Jews and politicians had betrayed the army. The politicians who signed the treaty became known as the 'November Criminals'.

The War Guilt Clause was hated most because Germans believed they had fought a defensive war. The military restrictions left Germany feeling defenceless when its neighbours remained armed, and the territorial losses placed millions of Germans under foreign rule, which seemed to contradict Wilson's principle of self-determination. Reparations were seen as a deliberate attempt to ruin the German economy.

The treaty damaged the new Weimar Republic from the start. Right-wing opponents attempted the Kapp Putsch in 1920, and when Germany failed to pay reparations, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr industrial region in January 1923. The government ordered passive resistance and printed money to pay striking workers, causing hyperinflation that wiped out people's savings. Resentment of the treaty was later exploited by Adolf Hitler, who promised to destroy it.

06

The Other Peace Treaties

Germany's allies were dealt with in separate treaties, all named after places near Paris. The Treaty of Saint-Germain (1919) broke up the Austro-Hungarian Empire; Austria lost territory to Italy, Czechoslovakia, Poland and Yugoslavia, its army was limited to 30,000 men and union with Germany was banned. The Treaty of Trianon (1920) reduced Hungary to about a third of its pre-war size, leaving millions of Hungarians under foreign rule.

The Treaty of Neuilly (1919) made Bulgaria give land to Greece, Romania and Yugoslavia and pay reparations. The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) broke up the Ottoman Empire, giving its Arab lands to Britain and France as mandates. Turkish nationalists led by Mustafa Kemal rejected Sèvres and fought successfully against Greece, so the Allies replaced it with the more generous Treaty of Lausanne in 1923. This showed that the peace settlement could be overturned by force, a lesson later noted by Hitler and Mussolini.

  • Saint-Germain 1919: Austria.
  • Neuilly 1919: Bulgaria.
  • Trianon 1920: Hungary.
  • Sèvres 1920: Turkey, replaced by Lausanne 1923.
07

Were the Big Three Satisfied?

Clemenceau got many of his aims: Alsace-Lorraine, a demilitarised Rhineland, heavy reparations and a much smaller German army. However, he failed to make the Rhineland an independent state and the Anglo-American guarantee to defend France collapsed when the USA refused to ratify the treaty. Many French people thought the treaty too lenient, and Clemenceau lost the presidential election in 1920.

Lloyd George was reasonably satisfied. Britain gained German colonies as mandates, the German navy was reduced and the German fleet scuttled itself at Scapa Flow in June 1919. However, he felt the treaty was too harsh and would cause future trouble, and within a few years Britain was sympathetic to German complaints. Wilson was the least satisfied. Self-determination was applied inconsistently, the treaty was harsher than he wanted, and although the League was created, the US Senate rejected the treaty in November 1919 and March 1920, so the USA never joined his League.

08

Was the Treaty Fair?

Arguments that the treaty was unfair focus on the Diktat, the war guilt clause, and the loss of German people to other states. The economist John Maynard Keynes, who attended the conference, argued in 1919 that reparations would wreck the German economy and harm all of Europe. The treaty humiliated Germany without destroying its power, which left it resentful and still capable of revenge.

Arguments that it was fair, or even lenient, point out that Germany had imposed an even harsher treaty on Russia at Brest-Litovsk in March 1918, taking about a third of Russia's farmland and population. Germany remained the largest country in central Europe with its industry largely intact, and reparations were later reduced by the Dawes Plan of 1924 and the Young Plan of 1929. France had suffered enormous destruction, and the peacemakers were under pressure from voters who wanted Germany punished.

A balanced judgement recognises that the Big Three had to compromise between conflicting aims in difficult circumstances. The treaty was probably harsh enough to anger Germany but not harsh enough to keep it weak, which is why many historians see it as a flawed compromise that contributed to, but did not by itself cause, the Second World War.

Key fact:Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918): Germany took huge areas of land, population and resources from Russia.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
ArmisticeAn agreement to stop fighting; the First World War armistice was signed on 11 November 1918.
Fourteen PointsWoodrow Wilson's peace programme of January 1918, including self-determination, disarmament and a League of Nations.
Self-determinationThe right of a people or nation to decide how they are governed and to form their own state.
Article 231The War Guilt Clause of the Treaty of Versailles, making Germany accept responsibility for the war's loss and damage.
ReparationsCompensation paid by a defeated country for war damage; Germany's bill was fixed in 1921 at £6,600 million.
Demilitarised zoneAn area where military forces and fortifications are banned, such as the Rhineland after 1919.
DiktatA dictated peace; the German term for a treaty imposed without negotiation.
MandateA former colony of a defeated power governed by a victor nation on behalf of the League of Nations.
AnschlussUnion between Germany and Austria, forbidden by the Treaty of Versailles.
PlebisciteA direct vote by the people of an area on a single issue, such as which country they wish to belong to.
Polish CorridorThe strip of land given to Poland to provide access to the sea, which separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany.
November CriminalsThe insulting name German nationalists gave to the politicians who signed the armistice and accepted the treaty.
Stab-in-the-back mythThe false belief that the German army had not been defeated but had been betrayed by politicians at home.

Worked Examples

Model answer: explaining aims (6 marks)

Why did Clemenceau want a harsh treaty?

  1. Reason one, security: France shared a border with Germany and had been invaded in 1870 and 1914, so Clemenceau wanted Germany too weak to attack again, for example through a small army and demilitarised Rhineland.
  2. Reason two, damage and losses: about 1.4 million French soldiers had died and the industrial north-east had been devastated, so France needed reparations to rebuild.
  3. Reason three, public opinion and revenge: the French public demanded punishment and the return of Alsace-Lorraine, lost in 1871.
  4. Explain each reason fully rather than simply naming it.
Clemenceau wanted a harsh treaty to guarantee French security against a third German invasion, to pay for the huge damage France had suffered and to satisfy a French public that wanted revenge. Three developed reasons reach the top level.

Model answer: judging fairness (10 marks)

'The Treaty of Versailles was fair to Germany.' How far do you agree? Explain your answer.

  1. Agree: Germany had imposed the harsh Treaty of Brest-Litovsk on Russia in 1918, which suggests it would have been harsher if it had won.
  2. Agree: France had suffered massive destruction, so reparations were a reasonable way to pay for rebuilding, and they were later reduced by the Dawes and Young Plans.
  3. Disagree: Germany was not allowed to negotiate, making it a Diktat, and Article 231 blamed Germany alone when other powers shared responsibility.
  4. Disagree: self-determination was denied to Germans, for example in the Polish Corridor, the Sudetenland and through the ban on Anschluss.
  5. Judgement: explain which side is stronger and why.
The treaty was partly fair, because Germany had behaved just as harshly at Brest-Litovsk and France deserved compensation. However, the lack of negotiation, the war guilt clause and the denial of self-determination to Germans made it seem unjust, and this perception of unfairness mattered because it fuelled resentment that Hitler later exploited. Overall, it was understandable in 1919 but not fair.

Source analysis: a German cartoon

A German cartoon of 1919 shows Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George standing over a guillotine with a German figure lying beneath the blade. What is the cartoonist's view of the treaty?

  1. Describe relevant detail: the Big Three are shown as executioners and Germany as a helpless victim.
  2. Use context: Germans called the treaty a Diktat because they could not negotiate and were forced to accept Article 231 and heavy reparations.
  3. Explain the message: the cartoonist believes the treaty is a deliberate attempt to destroy Germany, not a fair peace.
  4. Consider provenance: a German cartoon published in 1919 reflects the anger felt across Germany when the terms were revealed.
The cartoonist sees the treaty as a cruel execution of Germany by the Allies. The view is typical of German outrage in 1919 at the Diktat, the war guilt clause and reparations.
11

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up the aims of the Big Three. Link Wilson to idealism and the League, Clemenceau to revenge and security, and Lloyd George to a middle position focused on trade and empire.
  • Saying Germany lost the Rhineland. It remained German but was demilitarised and occupied by Allied troops for 15 years.
  • Saying the Saar was given to France. It was run by the League for 15 years while France took its coal, and it voted to return to Germany in 1935.
  • Giving wrong figures: the army was 100,000, the navy six battleships, and reparations £6,600 million fixed in 1921, not in 1919.
  • Claiming the USA joined the League. The US Senate rejected the treaty, so the USA never joined.
  • Condemning the treaty without considering the pressures on the peacemakers, such as public opinion and French losses.

Exam tips

  • Use the BRAT or LAMB (Land, Army, Money, Blame) memory aid to recall terms quickly, but always explain the effect of each term on Germany.
  • For 'how far was each leader satisfied' questions, give what they gained and what they failed to gain, then make a judgement.
  • Compare Versailles with Brest-Litovsk when arguing that the treaty was not as harsh as Germans claimed.
  • Use German reactions to explain later events such as the Ruhr occupation of 1923, hyperinflation and Hitler's rise.
  • In source questions, identify whether a cartoon is British, French or German, because each nation saw the treaty very differently.
  • Avoid hindsight in judgements about 1919: explain what the peacemakers knew and wanted at the time.

Judgement: they achieved a workable compromise given the pressures, but its flaws helped destabilise Europe (2).

Quick Recap

  1. The Paris Peace Conference opened in January 1919 and the Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919.
  2. Wilson wanted a fair peace and a League, Clemenceau wanted revenge and security, and Lloyd George sought a middle way.
  3. Germany accepted war guilt, paid £6,600 million in reparations, had its army cut to 100,000 and lost about 13 per cent of its land.
  4. Germans called the treaty a Diktat and resented war guilt, reparations and territorial losses.
  5. Separate treaties dealt with Austria, Hungary, Bulgaria and Turkey, and Sèvres was replaced by Lausanne in 1923.
  6. None of the Big Three was fully satisfied, and the USA never ratified the treaty.
  7. Historians still debate whether the treaty was fair, but its resentment contributed to the instability that led to the Second World War.
