Consumer Protection

Why consumers need protection, how the law, government agencies, consumer associations, self-regulation and the media protect them, and the rights and responsibilities of consumers.

01

Overview

Consumers are often at a disadvantage when dealing with businesses: they may lack technical knowledge, be misled by advertising or be sold unsafe or faulty goods. Consumer protection refers to the laws, organisations and practices that safeguard buyers against such exploitation. In Cambridge Commerce, candidates are asked to explain why consumers need protection, to describe the main methods of protection, including legislation, government bodies, consumer associations, trade associations and the media, and to advise a consumer who has a complaint. This chapter gives the principles behind consumer law without requiring detailed knowledge of any one country's statutes, while using well-known examples to illustrate.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain why consumers need protection in modern markets.
  • Describe the main areas of consumer protection legislation, including sale of goods, trade descriptions, weights and measures, food safety and consumer credit.
  • Explain the role of government agencies, local authorities and standards bodies in protecting consumers.
  • Describe the work of consumer associations, trade associations, ombudsmen and the media.
  • Explain the rights and responsibilities of consumers and the steps to take when making a complaint.
  • Evaluate the effectiveness of different methods of consumer protection.
02

Why Consumers Need Protection

In the past, the principle of caveat emptor, 'let the buyer beware', meant the buyer was responsible for checking goods before purchase. This was reasonable when goods were simple and sold face to face. Today products are complex, such as smartphones, cars, medicines and financial products, and consumers cannot judge their quality or safety by inspection. Businesses are often large and powerful, with more knowledge and resources than individual buyers.

Consumers may be exploited in many ways: short weights and measures; false or misleading descriptions and advertising; dangerous goods such as faulty electrical appliances or toys with small parts; adulterated or contaminated food; hidden charges and unfair credit terms; high-pressure selling; and fake or counterfeit products. Online shopping adds new risks such as fraud, non-delivery and misuse of personal data. Monopolies can overcharge because consumers have no alternative supplier.

Consumer protection aims to ensure that goods are safe, of satisfactory quality and fairly described, that prices and credit terms are clear, and that consumers have a means of redress, such as a refund, repair, replacement or compensation, when things go wrong. Protection also benefits honest businesses by preventing unfair competition from dishonest traders.

Key fact:Caveat emptor (let the buyer beware) has largely been replaced by laws placing responsibility on sellers and producers.
03

Consumer Protection Legislation

Most countries have laws covering similar areas. Sale of goods laws state that goods must be of satisfactory (merchantable) quality, fit for the purpose for which they are sold, including any particular purpose the buyer made known, and as described by the seller. If they are not, the buyer can reject them and obtain a refund, or claim repair, replacement or compensation. The contract is between buyer and retailer, so the retailer, not the manufacturer, is responsible for putting things right. Supply of services laws require services to be carried out with reasonable care and skill, within a reasonable time and at a reasonable price.

Trade descriptions laws make it an offence to give a false description of goods or services, such as claiming a used car has travelled fewer kilometres than it has, or displaying false claims about 'reduced' prices. Weights and measures laws require goods to be sold in accurate quantities and scales to be checked and stamped by inspectors. Food safety laws set standards of hygiene, labelling and ingredients, and require that food is fit for human consumption. Consumer safety laws set standards for goods such as toys, electrical appliances and car tyres, and allow dangerous products to be banned or recalled.

Consumer credit laws require lenders to be licensed, to state the annual percentage rate (APR) clearly so consumers can compare the true cost of borrowing, and to give a cooling-off period for certain credit agreements signed away from business premises. Competition laws prevent monopolies and cartels from fixing prices or abusing market power. Data protection laws control how businesses collect and use consumers' personal information.

  • Sale of goods: satisfactory quality, fit for purpose, as described.
  • Trade descriptions: no false or misleading claims about goods, services or prices.
  • Weights and measures: accurate quantities; scales inspected.
  • Food safety and labelling: hygiene, ingredients, use-by dates.
  • Consumer safety: standards and recalls for dangerous goods.
  • Consumer credit: licensing, APR disclosure, cooling-off periods.
04

Government Agencies and Standards Bodies

Laws are only effective if enforced. Governments set up agencies, such as competition commissions and consumer protection councils, to investigate unfair practices, take action against traders and publish advice for consumers. Local authorities employ trading standards officers, sometimes called weights and measures inspectors, who test scales and petrol pumps, check that goods are correctly labelled and priced, investigate complaints and prosecute offenders. Environmental health officers inspect restaurants, food shops and factories for hygiene and can close premises that are unsafe.

National standards bodies, such as the British Standards Institution (Kitemark), the Pakistan Standards and Quality Control Authority or the Bureau of Indian Standards (ISI mark), set minimum quality and safety standards and allow manufacturers whose products pass tests to display a quality mark. Consumers can then recognise tested products. Regulators are also set up for particular industries, especially former state monopolies such as electricity, telecommunications and water, to control prices and service quality. Financial regulators and the central bank protect bank customers and investors.

05

Consumer Associations and Self-Regulation by Business

Consumer associations are independent, non-profit organisations that test products and services and publish the results, often in magazines or websites such as Which? in the UK. They compare products for quality, safety and value, give advice to members, campaign for new laws and represent consumers' views to government and industry. Their independence gives their reports credibility, but consumers usually need to pay a subscription, and many people are unaware of them.

Many industries protect consumers through self-regulation. Trade associations, such as associations of travel agents, motor dealers or builders, draw up codes of practice that members agree to follow, covering standards of service, clear pricing and complaints procedures. Members may display the association's logo, which reassures customers, and some associations run arbitration schemes to settle disputes cheaply without going to court. Travel industry associations may guarantee refunds if a member firm collapses.

Advertising standards bodies check that advertisements are 'legal, decent, honest and truthful' and can order misleading advertisements to be withdrawn. Ombudsmen are independent officials who investigate complaints against banks, insurers, public services and other bodies, and can order compensation. Self-regulation is cheaper and more flexible than legislation, but critics argue it is weaker because there is no legal penalty and firms that are not members are not covered.

06

The Role of the Media and Businesses Themselves

Newspapers, television, radio and websites help protect consumers by exposing dishonest traders, publishing consumer advice columns and running investigative programmes. Social media and online review sites allow consumers to share experiences instantly, so poor service can damage a firm's reputation quickly. This gives businesses a strong incentive to treat customers fairly.

Many businesses protect consumers voluntarily because satisfied customers return and recommend the firm. They offer guarantees and warranties, no-questions-asked return policies, customer service departments and loyalty schemes. A reputation for quality is a valuable asset, and firms selling faulty goods risk bad publicity, lawsuits and product recalls that cost far more than good quality control.

07

Consumer Rights, Responsibilities and Making a Complaint

Consumer rights are often summarised as the right to safety, the right to be informed, the right to choose, the right to be heard, the right to redress, and the right to consumer education. Consumers also have responsibilities: to read labels and instructions, to check goods and receipts, to keep proof of purchase, to use goods correctly, to complain promptly and reasonably, and to avoid buying counterfeit or stolen goods.

When a consumer has a problem, the first step is to return to the shop with the goods and the receipt, explain the fault calmly and ask for a refund, repair or replacement. If this fails, the consumer should put the complaint in writing to the manager or head office, keeping copies. Next, the consumer can seek help from a trading standards office, a consumer advice centre or the relevant trade association or ombudsman. As a last resort, the consumer may take the case to a small claims court, which handles low-value claims cheaply without the need for a lawyer.

Key fact:Complaint ladder: shop → written complaint to head office → trading standards / trade association / ombudsman → small claims court.
08

Evaluating Methods of Consumer Protection

Legislation gives consumers legal rights and penalties for offenders, but it can be complex, slow and expensive to use, and many consumers do not know their rights. Enforcement depends on having enough inspectors, which is a problem in many developing countries. Consumer associations give independent, expert information but reach mainly educated, better-off consumers. Self-regulation is flexible and cheap but lacks legal force. The media can be powerful but may be sensational or one-sided.

In practice, effective protection needs a combination: strong laws and enforcement, independent information, responsible businesses and informed consumers. Education is especially important, because a consumer who reads labels, compares prices, keeps receipts and knows how to complain is far harder to exploit.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Consumer protectionLaws, organisations and practices that safeguard consumers against unfair, unsafe or dishonest trading.
Caveat emptorLet the buyer beware: the old principle that buyers must check goods before purchase.
Satisfactory qualityThe legal requirement that goods are free from faults, safe and durable, as a reasonable person would expect.
Fit for purposeThe requirement that goods can do what they are designed or sold to do.
Trade descriptions lawLaw that makes it an offence to give false or misleading descriptions of goods, services or prices.
Trading standards officerA local authority official who enforces laws on weights, measures, pricing, labelling and descriptions.
Consumer associationAn independent non-profit body that tests products, informs consumers and campaigns for their interests.
Trade associationA body representing firms in one industry that may set a code of practice for members.
Code of practiceA set of voluntary rules on standards of behaviour that members of a trade association agree to follow.
OmbudsmanAn independent official who investigates complaints against organisations and can recommend compensation.
RedressRemedy for a consumer's loss, such as a refund, repair, replacement or compensation.

Small claims courtA simple, low-cost court procedure for settling disputes involving small sums without lawyers.
Product recallAction by a producer to take back products found to be unsafe or faulty.

Worked Examples

Advising a consumer with a faulty product

Sara bought a new blender for $60. After two weeks it stopped working, although she used it according to the instructions. The shop says she must contact the manufacturer. Advise Sara. [6 marks]

  1. Identify her legal rights: under sale of goods laws, the blender must be of satisfactory quality and fit for purpose. A blender failing after two weeks of normal use is neither.
  2. Identify who is responsible: the contract is between Sara and the shop, so the shop, not the manufacturer, must put things right.
  3. State her remedy: she is entitled to a refund, repair or replacement.
  4. Explain the steps: return to the shop with the receipt, speak to the manager, then write to head office; if refused, contact the trading standards office or a consumer advice centre; finally, use the small claims court.
Sara should insist that the shop deals with her claim because her contract is with the shop. The blender is not of satisfactory quality, so she can demand a refund, repair or replacement, escalating to trading standards or the small claims court if the shop refuses.

Comparing legislation and self-regulation

The government is deciding whether to pass a new law controlling advertising to children or to let the advertising industry regulate itself through a code of practice. Which approach would better protect consumers? [8 marks]

  1. Legislation advantages: legally binding on all firms; penalties such as fines deter offenders; enforced by government officials.
  2. Legislation disadvantages: slow to pass and change; enforcement costs taxpayers; may be inflexible as media such as social platforms change rapidly.
  3. Self-regulation advantages: industry experts write the rules; cheaper for government; faster to update; firms may comply to protect reputation.
  4. Self-regulation disadvantages: no legal penalty; firms outside the association are not covered; industry may put its own interests first.
  5. Judgement: children are vulnerable consumers, so strong protection is needed.
Legislation is likely to be more effective for protecting children because it applies to all advertisers and carries penalties, although it could be combined with an industry code that gives detailed guidance and can be updated quickly. A justified conclusion either way earns credit.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Telling a consumer to complain to the manufacturer. The legal contract is with the retailer, which must deal with the complaint.
  • Describing consumer associations as government bodies. They are independent, non-profit organisations.
  • Writing 'the government protects consumers' without explaining how, such as through laws, inspectors and regulators.
  • Forgetting consumer responsibilities. Consumers must keep receipts, read labels and use goods correctly.
  • Confusing trade associations with trade unions. Trade associations represent firms; trade unions represent workers.
  • Assuming consumers are entitled to a refund if they simply change their mind. Legal rights apply to faulty or misdescribed goods; change-of-mind refunds are a shop's goodwill policy.

Exam tips

  • Structure answers on methods of protection under headings: legislation, government agencies, consumer associations, self-regulation, media.
  • In advice questions, identify the right, who is responsible, the remedy and the steps to take.
  • Use the three legal tests for goods: satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described.
  • When evaluating, consider how easily ordinary consumers can use each method and whether it has legal force.
  • Include examples such as quality marks, APR on loan advertisements or trading standards checks on petrol pumps.
  • Remember the complaint ladder and present it in logical order in answers.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. What is meant by 'caveat emptor'?

Model answerLet the buyer beware (1): the buyer is responsible for checking goods before purchase and has little comeback if they are faulty (1).
[3 marks]

2. State three reasons why consumers need protection.

Model answerAny three: goods are complex and cannot be judged by inspection (1); misleading advertising (1); dangerous or faulty goods (1); short weights (1); unfair credit terms (1); monopoly power of large firms (1); online fraud (1).
[3 marks]

3. State the three requirements that goods must meet under sale of goods legislation.

Model answerOf satisfactory quality (1); fit for purpose (1); as described (1).
[4 marks]

4. Explain the role of a trading standards officer.

Model answerChecks weighing and measuring equipment such as scales and petrol pumps are accurate (1–2); checks labelling, pricing and descriptions (1–2); investigates consumer complaints and prosecutes offending traders (1–2).
[4 marks]

5. Explain how a consumer association protects consumers.

Model answerTests products and publishes comparative reports on quality, safety and value (2). Campaigns for changes to laws and represents consumers' views to government (2). Gives advice to members (1).
[3 marks]

6. What is a code of practice and who draws it up?

Model answerA set of voluntary rules on standards of behaviour and service (1) drawn up by a trade association (1) for its member firms to follow (1).
[4 marks]

7. Explain why APR must be stated in credit advertisements.

Model answerAPR shows the true annual cost of borrowing, including interest and charges (2), so consumers can compare different credit offers fairly and avoid expensive loans (2).
[4 marks]

8. List four consumer rights.

Model answerAny four: right to safety; right to be informed; right to choose; right to be heard; right to redress; right to consumer education (1 each).
[5 marks]

9. Describe the steps a consumer should take when a product is faulty.

Model answerReturn to the shop with the goods and receipt (1); ask calmly for refund, repair or replacement (1); if refused, write to the manager or head office (1); contact trading standards, consumer advice centre, trade association or ombudsman (1); use the small claims court as a last resort (1).
[6 marks]

10. Explain two ways businesses protect consumers voluntarily and why they do so.

Model answerOffering guarantees or warranties (1–2) and return policies or customer service departments (1–2). They do so to build a good reputation, encourage repeat custom and avoid bad publicity (2).
[10 marks]

11. Evaluate the effectiveness of legislation as a method of consumer protection.

Model answerStrengths: gives legal rights to all consumers (1); penalties deter dishonest traders (1); applies to every firm (1); enforced by officials such as trading standards (1). Weaknesses: many consumers do not know their rights (1); legal action can be slow and costly (1); enforcement needs resources, weak in some countries (1); laws may lag behind new technology such as online selling (1). Conclusion: effective as a foundation but needs support from education, consumer associations and self-regulation (1–2).

Quick Recap

  1. Consumers need protection because goods are complex and businesses have more knowledge and power.
  2. Consumer law requires goods to be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described.
  3. Other laws cover trade descriptions, weights and measures, food safety, product safety, credit and competition.
  4. Trading standards officers, environmental health officers, regulators and standards bodies enforce protection.
  5. Consumer associations test products and campaign; trade associations issue codes of practice.
  6. The media, online reviews and firms' own guarantees add further protection.
  7. Consumers have rights and responsibilities and should follow a clear complaints procedure.
  8. Effective protection combines law, enforcement, independent information, responsible business and consumer education.
