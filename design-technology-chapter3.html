Generating and Developing Ideas

Creative techniques for producing a wide range of design ideas, communicating them through sketching and modelling, and refining the best idea into a detailed final solution.

01

Overview

Once the specification is written, the designer must turn requirements into real, workable ideas. This stage is where creativity meets discipline: ideas need to be original and varied, but they must also be evaluated honestly against the specification and developed until every detail is resolved. Examiners reward candidates who can sketch clearly, annotate meaningfully, explain how modelling informs decisions and show a logical path from rough concept to final design. This chapter covers idea-generation strategies, graphical communication, evaluating ideas, modelling and detailed development.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Use creative strategies such as brainstorming, SCAMPER, mood boards and biomimicry to generate varied ideas.
  • Communicate ideas through freehand sketching, crating, rendering and useful annotation.
  • Evaluate initial ideas objectively against a specification using a decision matrix.
  • Explain the purpose of different types of model and the materials used to make them.
  • Describe how an idea is developed in terms of form, materials, construction, ergonomics and finish.
  • Produce the information needed for manufacture, including working drawings and parts lists.
02

Strategies for Generating Ideas

Many students find the first idea easy and the second hard. Creative strategies help break this block by forcing the mind to explore different directions. Brainstorming, also called mind mapping, places the problem in the centre of the page and branches out into related words, functions, materials, shapes and user needs. Each branch can suggest a different concept. The key rule is to record every thought without judging it, because unlikely thoughts often combine into strong ideas later.

SCAMPER is a checklist applied to an existing product: Substitute a part or material, Combine features, Adapt an idea from elsewhere, Modify or magnify a feature, Put to another use, Eliminate a part, and Reverse or rearrange the layout. Applying SCAMPER to a standard desk lamp might produce a lamp that folds flat (rearrange), a lamp that doubles as a phone charger (combine) or a lamp with the stand removed that clips to a shelf (eliminate).

Other strategies include mood boards, which collect images, colours and textures to establish a visual style; biomimicry, which takes inspiration from nature, such as Velcro inspired by burrs or streamlined forms based on fish; and design movements, where the designer borrows the style of Art Deco, Bauhaus, Memphis or other movements. Using themes from geometry or architecture can also give a collection of ideas a strong identity.

  • Brainstorming or mind mapping: explore words and associations around the problem.
  • SCAMPER: systematically change an existing product.
  • Mood boards: set the visual style, colours and textures for the target market.
  • Biomimicry: copy solutions found in nature.
  • Design movements: take inspiration from styles such as Bauhaus or Art Deco.
  • Morphological chart: combine different options for each function to create new ideas.
03

Communicating Ideas Through Sketching

Initial ideas are normally shown as freehand sketches. Speed matters more than perfection, but sketches must be clear enough for someone else to understand. Crating is a technique in which the designer first draws a light box (the crate) in perspective or isometric, then carves the object out of it. This keeps proportions accurate and makes complex forms easier to draw. Construction lines are drawn lightly and the final outline is darkened.

Rendering adds tone, colour and texture to show form and material. Shading one side darker than another shows that a shape is three-dimensional. Using a thick line for outer edges and thin lines for inner edges, known as line weighting, makes a sketch stand out. Materials can be suggested with simple techniques: wood grain lines, highlights on polished metal, or reflections on glossy plastic.

Annotation turns a drawing into design thinking. Good annotations explain rather than label. "Handle" is a label; "Handle 110 mm long, 30 mm diameter, fits 95th percentile hand, moulded TPE grip for comfort" is an annotation that shows how the idea meets the specification. Annotations should cover function, materials, manufacture, sizes, ergonomics, safety and aesthetics, and should include honest comments on weaknesses as well as strengths.

Key fact:Label = what it is. Annotation = why it is like that and how it meets the specification.
04

Evaluating Initial Ideas

After producing a range of ideas, the designer must decide which to develop. Choosing a favourite without reasons gains little credit. Instead, each idea should be evaluated against the specification. This can be done in writing beneath each sketch, or with a decision matrix, also called an evaluation matrix, where ideas are listed in rows and specification criteria in columns.

Each idea is scored for each criterion, commonly from 1 to 5. Criteria can also be weighted to reflect their importance: if safety is critical, its scores might be multiplied by 3, while aesthetics might be multiplied by 1. The totals give an objective ranking. The highest-scoring idea is usually developed, although designers often combine the best features of two or three ideas.

Client and user feedback is another valuable form of evaluation. Showing sketches to the intended user and recording their comments provides evidence for decisions and often reveals preferences that the designer had not considered. Recording this feedback in the folio strengthens the justification for the chosen idea.

05

Modelling

A model is a simplified representation used to test and communicate a design before committing to expensive materials. Models reduce risk: it is far cheaper to discover that a handle is uncomfortable using a foam model than after a batch of aluminium handles has been machined. Models also help clients understand a design, because many people find it hard to read technical drawings.

Different models serve different purposes. Sketch models are quick, rough models in card, paper or foam used to explore form and proportion. Block models are solid, often made from styrofoam or MDF, used to check size and appearance. Working models or prototypes test mechanisms and function, often using construction kits, card linkages or 3D printed parts. Scale models are made smaller than full size, commonly 1:10 or 1:20 for furniture and architecture, while full-size mock-ups test ergonomics directly with users.

Virtual modelling using CAD software allows designers to test assemblies, check for clashes between parts, simulate stresses and produce photorealistic renderings. Virtual models can be changed quickly and shared digitally, but they cannot replace the experience of holding a physical object, so both are commonly used.

  • Card and paper: cheap, quick, easy to cut and score; good for nets and mechanisms.
  • Styrofoam (blue foam): easy to shape with saws and abrasive paper; good for hand-held forms.
  • Modelling clay or plasticine: sculpt organic, ergonomic shapes.
  • Corrugated card and foam board: larger rigid models and furniture mock-ups.
  • 3D printing (PLA): accurate functional parts from CAD files.
  • Construction kits: test gears, pulleys, levers and linkages.
Key fact:Scale 1:5 means 1 mm on the model represents 5 mm on the real product.
06

Developing the Chosen Idea

Development is the process of refining the chosen idea until it is ready to be made. It is not simply redrawing the same idea more neatly. Real development shows change and improvement driven by testing, research and feedback. A clear development section might show three or four versions of a handle shape tested as foam models, with notes explaining why each change was made and which version was chosen.

Development usually addresses several aspects in turn. Form and proportion are refined through sketches and models. Materials are selected by comparing properties, cost and availability, often by testing samples. Construction methods and joints are chosen and trialled. Ergonomics is checked with users. Finishes are tested on scrap material to compare appearance and durability. Manufacturing methods are considered to ensure the design can actually be made with available equipment.

Throughout development, the designer keeps checking the specification. Sometimes development shows that a specification point is unrealistic, for example a target cost that cannot be met with the required material. In that case the designer should explain the problem and justify a revised specification point. Showing this reflective thinking is a sign of high-level work.

07

The Final Design and Information for Manufacture

The outcome of development is a final design proposal. This is normally presented as a rendered pictorial drawing, often in isometric or two-point perspective, showing what the finished product will look like. It is accompanied by a statement explaining how the final design meets each key specification point.

For manufacture, the designer produces a working drawing, usually in third angle orthographic projection, with dimensions in millimetres, a scale, a title block and a parts list. Sectional views and enlarged details may be added to show internal features and joints. An exploded view may show how parts fit together. A cutting list gives the finished sizes and materials of every part, allowing materials to be ordered and costed.

A costing calculates the total cost of materials and components. For example, if a product needs 0.12 m² of 6 mm birch plywood costing $18 per m², the plywood costs 0.12 × 18 = $2.16. Adding hinges, screws and finish gives the total material cost, which can be checked against the specification.

09

Worked Examples

Weighted decision matrix

Three ideas for a phone stand are scored out of 5 against three criteria. Weights: stability ×3, cost ×2, aesthetics ×1. Idea A scores 4, 3, 5. Idea B scores 5, 4, 2. Idea C scores 3, 5, 4. Which idea should be developed?

  1. Idea A: (4 × 3) + (3 × 2) + (5 × 1) = 12 + 6 + 5 = 23.
  2. Idea B: (5 × 3) + (4 × 2) + (2 × 1) = 15 + 8 + 2 = 25.
  3. Idea C: (3 × 3) + (5 × 2) + (4 × 1) = 9 + 10 + 4 = 23.
  4. Compare totals: B has the highest score of 25.
  5. Note that B scores poorly on aesthetics, so development could borrow the appearance of Idea A.
Idea B should be developed because it scores highest (25) when the most important criterion, stability, is weighted. Its appearance can be improved during development using features from Idea A.

Scale calculation for a model

A bookcase will be 1800 mm tall, 900 mm wide and 300 mm deep. A student makes a 1:10 scale model. Calculate the model dimensions and the area of card needed for the two sides.

  1. At 1:10, divide each real dimension by 10.
  2. Height = 1800 ÷ 10 = 180 mm; width = 900 ÷ 10 = 90 mm; depth = 300 ÷ 10 = 30 mm.
  3. Each side panel measures height × depth = 180 mm × 30 mm = 5400 mm².
  4. Two sides = 2 × 5400 = 10 800 mm², which is 108 cm².
The model is 180 mm × 90 mm × 30 mm, and the two sides need 10 800 mm² (108 cm²) of card, excluding waste.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Producing ideas that are only small variations of one concept. Show genuinely different forms, mechanisms and materials.
  • Labelling sketches instead of annotating them. Explain how features meet the specification.
  • Choosing an idea because you like it without evidence. Use the specification, a decision matrix or user feedback.
  • Presenting development as a neater redraw of the chosen idea. Development must show change and reasons for change.
  • Forgetting that models have limitations. Say what the model tested and what it could not show.
  • Getting scale calculations the wrong way round. At 1:10 the model is ten times smaller, so divide the real size by 10.

Exam tips

  • When asked to sketch an idea in the exam, add at least four annotations covering materials, sizes, function and manufacture.
  • Use crating lines lightly, then darken final outlines; examiners value clarity over artistic flair.
  • Name specific modelling materials and explain why each suits the purpose, such as styrofoam for hand-held forms.
  • In "develop" questions, show at least one clear improvement and explain the reason for it.
  • Show working in decision matrix and scale calculations so method marks can be awarded.
  • Link final design statements directly to specification points.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. State what each letter of SCAMPER stands for.

Model answerSubstitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify (or Magnify), Put to another use, Eliminate, Reverse (or Rearrange). 2 marks for all seven, 1 mark for four to six.
[3 marks]

2. Explain the difference between labelling and annotating a sketch.

Model answerA label simply names a part (1). An annotation explains a feature and why it is designed that way (1), linking it to the specification, materials, function or user (1).
[2 marks]

3. What is biomimicry? Give one example.

Model answerDesigning by copying structures or processes found in nature (1). Example: Velcro inspired by burrs; streamlined train nose based on a kingfisher's beak; honeycomb structures (1).
[3 marks]

4. Describe the technique of crating.

Model answerA light box or crate is drawn first in the correct projection (1) to the overall dimensions of the object (1); the object is then drawn inside by removing parts of the box, keeping proportions accurate (1).
[2 marks]

5. State two reasons for making a model during development.

Model answerTo test size, proportions or ergonomics (1); to test a mechanism or function (1); to show the client and gain feedback (1); cheaper than making mistakes in final materials (1). Any two.
[3 marks]

6. Name a suitable modelling material for testing the grip of a hand-held torch and justify your choice.

Model answerStyrofoam/blue foam or modelling clay (1). It is easy and quick to shape with abrasive paper or by hand (1) and can be adjusted to test different grip forms with users (1).
[2 marks]

7. A table 750 mm high is modelled at 1:5. Calculate the model height.

Model answer750 ÷ 5 (1) = 150 mm (1).
[4 marks]

8. Explain how a weighted decision matrix helps a designer choose between ideas.

Model answerIdeas are scored against specification criteria (1); weights reflect the importance of each criterion (1); totals give an objective ranking (1), reducing personal bias in choosing an idea (1).
[4 marks]

9. State four pieces of information that should be included on a working drawing.

Model answerDimensions in mm (1); scale (1); projection symbol (1); title block with name/date/title (1); parts list or materials (1); sectional or detail views (1). Any four.
[5 marks]

10. Compare the use of virtual CAD models and physical models during development.

Model answerCAD: quick to modify (1), accurate, can simulate stresses and produce realistic renders (1), easy to share digitally (1). Physical: can be handled to test ergonomics and feel (1), easier for clients to understand in 3D (1), but slower to change (1). Maximum 5 with both types covered.
[6 marks]

11. Describe what a student should show in the development section of a coursework folio for a desk tidy.

Model answerRefinement of form through sketches and models (1); tests of models with reasons for changes (1); selection and testing of materials (1); trial of joints or construction methods (1); ergonomic checks with users (1); finish trials (1); consideration of manufacture with available tools (1); reference back to the specification (1). Maximum 6.

13

Quick Recap

  1. Creative strategies such as brainstorming, SCAMPER, mood boards and biomimicry produce varied ideas.
  2. Crating, line weighting and rendering make sketches clear and three-dimensional.
  3. Annotations explain design decisions and link features to the specification.
  4. Decision matrices, especially weighted ones, give an objective basis for choosing ideas.
  5. Models such as sketch models, block models, prototypes and mock-ups test designs cheaply.
  6. Development shows real change driven by testing, research and feedback.
  7. The final design is presented pictorially and supported by working drawings, cutting lists and costings.
