Mechanisms in Depth

Mechanisms multiply force, change speed or transfer motion. Levers, gears and pulleys all obey the same three calculations: mechanical advantage, velocity ratio and efficiency.

Key Concepts

01

Levers multiply force: a class 1 lever has the fulcrum between load and effort (crowbar, scissors), class 2 has the load between fulcrum and effort (wheelbarrow, nutcracker), and class 3 has the effort between fulcrum and load (tweezers, human forearm).

02

Mechanical advantage (MA) = load ÷ effort; velocity ratio (VR) = distance moved by effort ÷ distance moved by load; in an ideal machine MA equals VR.

03

Efficiency = MA ÷ VR × 100, equivalently useful work output ÷ work input × 100; real machines stay below 100% because friction and the weight of moving parts waste energy.

04

Gears transmit rotary motion: the gear ratio equals driven teeth ÷ driver teeth, an idler gear reverses direction without changing the ratio, and compound gears give large reductions.

05

Bevel gears transfer drive between shafts at 90 degrees, a rack and pinion converts rotation into linear motion, and a worm gear gives a high reduction ratio while locking against back-driving.

06

Pulleys change the direction of a force (a fixed pulley, MA 1) or multiply it (a movable pulley and block and tackle); lever equilibrium is analysed with moments, force × perpendicular distance from the pivot.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
FulcrumThe fixed pivot point about which a lever rotates; the point of support for the turning forces.
Mechanical advantageThe ratio of the output force (load) to the input force (effort), showing how much a mechanism multiplies an applied force.
Velocity ratioThe ratio of the distance moved by the effort to the distance moved by the load in a machine.
EfficiencyThe ratio of useful work output to total work input, expressed as a percentage; friction keeps it below 100 per cent in real machines.
MomentThe turning effect of a force about a pivot, calculated as the force multiplied by its perpendicular distance from the pivot.
Idler gearA gear placed between the driver and driven gears that reverses the direction of rotation without changing the velocity ratio.
Bevel gearA gear with teeth cut at an angle, used to transmit drive between two shafts whose axes intersect, usually at 90 degrees.
Worm gearA screw-like gear meshing with a spur gear, giving a large speed reduction and preventing the output shaft from driving backwards.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Show every step in mechanism calculations: write the formula, substitute the numbers, then answer with units. When asked why MA is less than VR, name friction and the weight of moving parts — those two points appear in almost every mark scheme.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

A lever lifts a load of 240 N using an effort of 60 N; the effort moves 1.5 m while the load rises 0.3 m. Calculate the mechanical advantage, the velocity ratio and the efficiency.

Model answerMechanical advantage = load ÷ effort = 240 ÷ 60 = 4 (1). Velocity ratio = effort distance ÷ load distance = 1.5 ÷ 0.3 = 5 (1). Efficiency = MA ÷ VR × 100 = 4 ÷ 5 × 100 (1) = 80% (1). The missing 20% is wasted against friction and in moving the parts of the machine (1).
CHALLENGE

A salesman claims his pulley system multiplies force tenfold with no energy loss. Explain why a machine's mechanical advantage can equal, but never exceed, its velocity ratio, and what happens in practice.

Model answerIn an ideal machine with no friction, MA equals VR because work input equals work output (1). A real machine wastes some input work against friction and in moving its own parts (1), so its MA is always smaller than its VR (1). Therefore efficiency = MA ÷ VR × 100 is always below 100% (1). Judgement: the salesman's no-energy-loss claim is impossible, so a tenfold MA would require a velocity ratio greater than 10 and still deliver less than 100% efficiency (1).
