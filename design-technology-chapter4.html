Woods

01

Overview

Wood is one of the oldest and most versatile design materials, used for furniture, toys, buildings, boats and packaging. In IGCSE and O-Level Design and Technology you must know the difference between hardwoods, softwoods and manufactured boards, recognise common examples, explain their properties and uses, and understand how timber is converted, seasoned and supplied. Exam questions often ask you to select a suitable wood for a given product and justify your choice, so this chapter focuses on linking properties to applications.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Classify timbers as hardwoods, softwoods or manufactured boards and give common examples of each.
  • Describe the working properties and typical uses of common timbers.
  • Explain how timber is converted and seasoned, and why seasoning is necessary.
  • Identify common defects in timber and their causes.
  • Describe the structure, advantages and uses of plywood, MDF, chipboard and blockboard.
  • Select and justify appropriate timbers for specific products.
02

Hardwoods and Softwoods

Timber is classified botanically, not by how hard it feels. Hardwoods come from broad-leaved, deciduous trees that usually lose their leaves in winter, or from broad-leaved evergreens in tropical regions. Their seeds are enclosed in a fruit or nut. Softwoods come from coniferous trees, which are usually evergreen with needle-like leaves and seeds carried in cones. Balsa is technically a hardwood even though it is extremely soft, while yew is a softwood that is quite hard, which shows why the botanical definition matters.

Hardwoods generally grow slowly, often taking 80 to 100 years or more to mature. This makes them denser, more durable and more attractive in grain, but also more expensive and less sustainable if not sourced from managed forests. Softwoods grow quickly, often in 25 to 40 years, so they are cheaper, widely available and commonly used in construction and joinery.

Under a microscope, hardwoods contain vessels, which are tube-like cells that carry sap and appear as pores in the end grain. Softwoods have no vessels; instead, tracheids carry sap and provide support. These structural differences affect how the wood machines, glues and finishes.

Key fact:Hardwood = broad-leaved (deciduous) tree with seeds in fruit. Softwood = coniferous tree with needles and cones.
03

Common Hardwoods

Oak is a heavy, strong, tough and durable hardwood with a distinctive light-brown colour and attractive grain. It contains tannic acid, which corrodes steel fittings, so brass or stainless steel screws should be used. Oak is used for high-quality furniture, flooring, beams and boat building. Beech is pale pink-brown, close-grained, hard and tough, and it does not taint food, so it is used for kitchen utensils, chopping boards, toys, workbenches and chair frames. It is not durable outdoors.

Mahogany is reddish-brown, relatively easy to work and has a fine finish; it is used for quality furniture and veneers, though sustainability concerns mean certified sources are essential. Teak is golden-brown and contains natural oils, making it highly resistant to water, rot and insects, which makes it ideal for outdoor furniture, decking and boat building, but the oils can make gluing difficult. Ash is pale, tough and flexible, absorbing shock well, so it is used for tool handles, sports equipment and bent furniture parts. Balsa is very light and soft and is used for model making.

  • Oak: strong, durable, tough; furniture, flooring, beams. Corrodes steel fittings.
  • Beech: hard, close-grained, non-tainting; toys, utensils, workbenches.
  • Mahogany: easy to work, fine finish; quality furniture, veneers.
  • Teak: oily, water and rot resistant; outdoor furniture, boats.
  • Ash: tough, flexible, shock resistant; tool handles, sports equipment.
  • Balsa: extremely light and soft; models.
04

Common Softwoods

Scots pine, often called red deal or redwood, is pale cream to yellow with darker brown knots. It is fairly strong, easy to work and cheap, and it is widely used for construction, roof joists, interior joinery and budget furniture. Knots can make it harder to plane and finish. Spruce, sometimes called whitewood, is creamy-white, lightweight, easy to work and has good strength-to-weight ratio; it is used for general construction, packaging, pallets and musical instrument soundboards.

Western red cedar is reddish-brown, lightweight and naturally resistant to rot and insects due to its oils, making it popular for outdoor cladding, sheds, fencing and beehives without the need for heavy preservative treatment. Parana pine is a light-coloured, mostly knot-free softwood that was used for staircases and interior fittings, though it can warp. Larch is harder and more durable than most softwoods and is used for fencing, boat planking and cladding.

  • Scots pine: cheap, easy to work, knotty; construction, joinery, budget furniture.
  • Spruce: light, easy to work; construction, packaging, pallets.
  • Western red cedar: light, naturally rot resistant; cladding, sheds, fencing.
  • Larch: durable for a softwood; fencing, boat planking.
05

Conversion and Seasoning

Conversion is the process of sawing a felled log into usable planks and boards. The two main methods are plain (through-and-through) sawing and quarter sawing. Plain sawing cuts parallel slices through the log. It is quick, cheap and produces little waste, but boards cut away from the centre are prone to cupping as they dry. Quarter sawing cuts the log into quarters and then cuts boards roughly at right angles to the growth rings. It is slower and more wasteful but produces stable boards that resist warping and show attractive figure, such as the silver ray pattern in oak.

Freshly felled timber, called green timber, can contain more than its own weight in water. Seasoning is the controlled drying of timber to a suitable moisture content. Seasoned timber is stronger, more stable, lighter, less likely to rot and easier to glue and finish. Timber for outdoor use is typically seasoned to around 18 to 20% moisture content, while timber for heated indoor use needs about 8 to 12%.

Air seasoning stacks timber in open-sided sheds with sticks (stickers) between boards so air can circulate. It is cheap but slow, taking roughly a year per 25 mm of thickness, and cannot reduce moisture below that of the surrounding air. Kiln seasoning places timber in a chamber where steam, heat and airflow are carefully controlled. It takes days or weeks rather than years, can achieve low moisture contents for indoor use and kills insects and fungi, but it requires expensive equipment and energy.

Key fact:Moisture content (%) = (wet mass − dry mass) ÷ dry mass × 100.
06

Timber Defects

Defects reduce the strength, appearance or usability of timber. Some are natural. Knots are the bases of branches; live knots are firmly attached, while dead knots can fall out, leaving holes and weakening the board. Shakes are splits along or across the grain that form in the growing tree or during felling. Other defects occur during seasoning when timber dries unevenly.

Warping describes any distortion from a flat, straight board. Cupping is curvature across the width; bowing is curvature along the length on the face; springing is curvature along the edge; and twisting is a spiral distortion. Splits or checks often occur at the ends of boards because end grain loses moisture faster than the rest of the board, which is why the ends are sometimes sealed with paint or wax during seasoning. Fungal attack such as dry rot and wet rot, and insect attack such as woodworm, damage timber that is damp or untreated.

  • Knots: branch bases; weaken timber and can be hard to plane.
  • Shakes: splits caused by stresses in the tree or felling.
  • Cupping, bowing, springing, twisting: distortions from uneven drying.
  • End splits and checks: rapid drying of end grain.
  • Rot and woodworm: attack damp or unprotected timber.
07

Manufactured Boards

Manufactured boards are made by bonding wood layers, strips, particles or fibres with adhesive, usually under heat and pressure. They are available in large, stable sheets, typically 2440 mm × 1220 mm, which natural timber cannot provide. They use lower-grade timber and waste wood, making them cheaper and often more sustainable, and they do not warp or split as easily as natural timber. However, their edges are often unattractive and may need edging strip or lipping, and some adhesives contain formaldehyde.

Plywood is made from an odd number of thin veneers glued with the grain of each layer at 90° to its neighbour. The cross-grained construction gives strength in all directions and resists warping. Exterior and marine grades use waterproof adhesive. Plywood is used for furniture, boat building, flooring and flexible curved forms (flexiply). Medium density fibreboard (MDF) is made from fine wood fibres and resin compressed into a smooth, uniform board. It machines well, has no grain, takes paint well and is ideal for flat-pack furniture and laser-cut models, but it is heavy, absorbs water readily and produces fine hazardous dust.

Chipboard is made from wood chips bonded with resin. It is cheap but weak, crumbles at the edges and holds screws poorly unless special fittings are used. It is usually faced with melamine or veneer for kitchen units and flat-pack furniture. Blockboard has a core of softwood strips glued side by side and faced with veneers; it is strong along its length and used for shelves and doors. Hardboard is a thin, dense fibreboard with one smooth face, used for drawer bases and cabinet backs.

Key fact:Plywood always has an odd number of layers so the outer grains run in the same direction and the board stays balanced.
08

Stock Forms and Selecting Timber

Timber is supplied in standard forms to reduce waste and cost. Rough sawn timber comes straight from the sawmill with an uneven surface. Planed all round (PAR) timber has all four faces planed smooth, which reduces its size by about 3 to 5 mm from the nominal sawn size. Mouldings are shaped sections such as dowel rod, skirting board and beading. Manufactured boards come in standard sheets of 2440 × 1220 mm in thicknesses such as 3, 6, 9, 12 and 18 mm.

When selecting timber, designers balance properties, appearance, cost, availability, sustainability and the working methods available. The question to ask is always: which property does this product need most? A child's toy needs a non-toxic, hard, close-grained wood that will not splinter, so beech is suitable. An outdoor bench needs natural durability, so teak or cedar, or treated pine, would be appropriate. A painted flat-pack bookcase needs large, stable, cheap sheets, so MDF or melamine-faced chipboard fits. Sustainability can be addressed by choosing timbers certified by the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC).

Worked Examples

Calculating moisture content

A sample of timber has a mass of 460 g when cut. After oven drying it has a mass of 400 g. Calculate its moisture content and state whether it is suitable for indoor furniture.

  1. Mass of water = wet mass − dry mass = 460 − 400 = 60 g.
  2. Moisture content = (mass of water ÷ dry mass) × 100.
  3. Moisture content = (60 ÷ 400) × 100 = 15%.
  4. Indoor furniture in heated rooms needs about 8 to 12% moisture content.
The moisture content is 15%, which is too high for indoor furniture; the timber would shrink and possibly warp or split in a heated room. It should be kiln seasoned further.

Selecting timber for a child's building blocks

Name a suitable timber for a set of wooden building blocks for toddlers and give three justified reasons.

  1. Identify key requirements: safe, hard-wearing, smooth, non-toxic and able to take a child-safe finish.
  2. Consider options: pine is soft and dents easily and may splinter; MDF produces dust and swells if chewed; beech is hard and close-grained.
  3. Reason 1: beech is hard and tough, so it resists dents and chipping when blocks are dropped or thrown.
  4. Reason 2: beech is close-grained with no strong smell or taste, so it does not taint and sands to a smooth, splinter-free surface.
  5. Reason 3: beech takes non-toxic water-based paints and oils well, allowing bright colours.
Beech is the most suitable timber because it is hard, tough, close-grained and non-tainting, and it can be finished safely with toy-safe paint.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Classifying woods by how hard they feel. Balsa is a hardwood and yew is a softwood; the classification is botanical.
  • Stating that manufactured boards are made from hardwood or softwood. They are a separate category made from veneers, particles or fibres.
  • Saying plywood has an even number of layers. It always has an odd number so it stays balanced.
  • Forgetting that oak corrodes steel fittings due to tannic acid. Use brass or stainless steel screws.
  • Giving vague reasons such as "it is strong". Name the specific property and link it to the product's use.
  • Confusing air and kiln seasoning. Air seasoning is slow and cheap; kiln seasoning is fast, controlled and reaches lower moisture contents.

Exam tips

  • Learn at least four hardwoods, four softwoods and four manufactured boards with one property and one use each.
  • When justifying a timber choice, give the property, then explain why that property matters for the product.
  • For manufactured boards, always mention stability and large sheet sizes as key advantages over natural timber.
  • Draw a simple labelled sketch of plywood layers if asked to describe its structure; diagrams earn marks.
  • In seasoning questions, state benefits: stronger, more stable, lighter, resists rot, easier to glue and finish.
  • Mention FSC certification in any question about sustainable timber.

1. State two differences between hardwoods and softwoods.

Model answerHardwoods come from broad-leaved/deciduous trees, softwoods from coniferous trees (1). Hardwood seeds are in fruit/nuts, softwood seeds in cones (1). Hardwoods usually grow slower and are more expensive (1). Any two.
[2 marks]

2. Name one hardwood suitable for outdoor furniture and give a reason.

Model answerTeak (or iroko/oak) (1). Contains natural oils making it resistant to water, rot and insects (1).
[3 marks]

3. Explain why plywood is more stable than a solid timber board of the same size.

Model answerPlywood's veneers are glued with the grain of each layer at 90° to the next (1). Movement or shrinkage in one direction is resisted by the adjacent layers (1), so it does not warp or split easily (1).
[3 marks]

4. Give three reasons why timber is seasoned.

Model answerIncreases strength (1); reduces shrinkage and warping in use/more stable (1); reduces risk of rot and fungal attack (1); lighter to transport (1); easier to glue and finish (1). Any three.
[4 marks]

5. Compare air seasoning with kiln seasoning.

Model answerAir seasoning is cheap and needs little equipment (1) but is slow, about one year per 25 mm (1) and cannot reach low moisture content for indoor use (1). Kiln seasoning is fast (1), controlled, reaches low moisture content and kills insects/fungi (1) but is expensive in equipment and energy (1). Maximum 4.
[3 marks]

6. A timber sample weighs 330 g when wet and 300 g when dry. Calculate the moisture content.

Model answerWater = 30 g (1). MC = 30 ÷ 300 × 100 (1) = 10% (1).
[4 marks]

7. Name and describe two types of warping.

Model answerCupping: curvature across the width of a board (1+1). Bowing: curvature along the length of the face (1+1). Also twisting (spiral distortion) or springing (curve along the edge). 2 marks each.
[3 marks]

8. State two advantages and one disadvantage of MDF.

Model answerAdvantages: smooth surface with no grain, takes paint well (1); stable, large sheets (1); cheap, machines and laser cuts well (1). Disadvantage: heavy / swells when wet / fine dust is hazardous / poor screw holding in edges (1).

9. Explain why brass screws should be used with oak.

Model answerOak contains tannic acid (1) which reacts with and corrodes steel, causing black staining and weakening fixings (1). Brass does not react in this way (1).

10. Explain why manufactured boards are often considered a sustainable choice.

Model answerThey use low-grade timber, thinnings and waste such as chips and sawdust (1), making efficient use of each tree (1). Large stable sheets reduce waste in cutting (1). Can be made from fast-growing, FSC-certified softwoods (1). Maximum 3.
[5 marks]

11. A designer is making a painted flat-pack bookcase for students. Recommend a material and justify your choice.

Model answerMDF or melamine-faced chipboard (1). Available in large standard sheets suitable for shelves and sides (1). Stable, so will not warp (1). Cheap, keeping the product affordable for students (1). MDF has a smooth surface that paints well (1). Can be used with knock-down fittings for flat-pack assembly (1). Maximum 5.

12. Describe the processes of plain sawing and quarter sawing, and evaluate each.

Model answerPlain sawing: log cut into parallel slices (1); quick, cheap and little waste (1); outer boards prone to cupping (1). Quarter sawing: log quartered then cut roughly perpendicular to growth rings (1); stable boards resist warping and show attractive figure (1); slower, more expensive and more waste (1). Maximum 6.

Quick Recap

  1. Hardwoods come from broad-leaved trees and softwoods from conifers; the classification is botanical.
  2. Common hardwoods include oak, beech, mahogany, teak and ash; common softwoods include pine, spruce and cedar.
  3. Conversion saws logs into boards; quarter sawing gives more stable boards than plain sawing.
  4. Seasoning reduces moisture content, making timber stronger, more stable and resistant to rot.
  5. Defects such as knots, shakes, warping and splits reduce quality and are often caused by uneven drying.
  6. Manufactured boards such as plywood, MDF and chipboard provide large, stable, cheaper sheets.
  7. Timber selection must link specific properties to the needs of the product, including sustainability.
