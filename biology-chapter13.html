Inheritance, Genetics & Variation

CORE
SECTION 01 — CHAPTER OVERVIEW

DNA & Heredity

Genetics explains how traits are passed from parents to offspring. This chapter covers the structure of DNA, the concepts of dominant and recessive alleles, how to construct Punnett squares, and the theory of evolution by natural selection.

  • Construct and interpret Punnett squares for monohybrid crosses.
  • Explain how sex is determined in humans (XX, XY).
  • Describe the process of natural selection.
SECTION 03 — CORE THEORY

The Code of Life

DNA is a molecule that carries genetic information. It is organized into structures called chromosomes (found in the nucleus). A gene is a short length of DNA that codes for a specific protein, determining a characteristic (e.g., eye color).

Key Genetic Terms

Allele: A different version of the same gene (e.g., brown vs blue eyes).

Dominant: An allele that is expressed even if only one copy is present (Capital letter, e.g., B).

Recessive: An allele that is only expressed if two copies are present (Lower case, e.g., b).

Genotype: The genetic makeup (e.g., Bb).

Phenotype: The physical appearance (e.g., Brown eyes).

Homozygous: Two identical alleles (BB or bb).

Heterozygous: Two different alleles (Bb).

Natural Selection

1. Organisms produce more offspring than can survive. 2. There is variation (genetic differences) within a population. 3. Those with characteristics best suited to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce ("survival of the fittest"). 4. They pass on their advantageous alleles to the next generation. Over time, the whole population changes.

SECTION 04 — KEY DEFINITIONS

Examination Definitions

GENE: A length of DNA that codes for a specific protein/characteristic.

ALLELE: An alternative form of a gene.

NATURAL SELECTION: The process where organisms better adapted to their environment tend to survive and produce more offspring.

SECTION 06 — DATA & GRAPH SKILLS

Variation in Humans

AIM

To analyze continuous and discontinuous variation in a population.

METHOD

Measure hand spans of 30 students. Plot a histogram. Then classify students by blood type or ability to roll tongue. Plot a bar chart.

CONCLUSION

Hand span is continuous variation (a range, plotted as a line/histogram, influenced by genes and environment). Blood type is discontinuousuous variation (distinct categories, plotted as a bar chart, influenced only by genes).

SECTION 07 — EXAM TECHNIQUE

Punnett Square Rules

Always use capital letters for dominant alleles. Make sure to write the dominant allele first in heterozygous genotypes (e.g., Bb, not bB). When stating ratio, always simplify (e.g., 3:1).

SECTION 08 — COMMON MISTAKES

Avoid These Errors

Mistake: Saying chromosomes are made of just genes.

Correction: Chromosomes are long lengths of DNA. Genes are just short sections of that DNA.

Mistake: Forgetting that mutations are random.

Correction: Natural selection is not random, but the mutations that cause variation are random.

SECTION 09 — QUICK REVISION

Fast Facts

  • DNA makes up chromosomes, which contain genes.
  • Dominant alleles mask recessive ones.
  • Homozygous = same alleles. Heterozygous = different.
  • Sex is determined by XX (female) or XY (male).
  • Mutations cause variation; natural selection acts on variation.
SECTION 10 — EXAM QUESTIONS

Test Your Knowledge

Question 1 (Challenge)

Two parents who can roll their tongues have a child who cannot. Explain how this is possible. (Tongue rolling is dominant, T).

3 marks: Tongue rolling is a dominant characteristic. Both parents must be heterozygous (Tt). When they reproduce, there is a 25% chance they pass on the recessive allele (t) to the child. The child is homozygous recessive (tt), so cannot roll their tongue.
