Biomechanics in Sport

Sport is applied physics: pushes and pulls, balance, grip, air resistance and the flight of a ball all follow mechanical rules that performers exploit deliberately.

Key Concepts

01

A force is a push or pull that can start, stop, change the speed or direction of, or deform a body; in sport forces come from muscles (internal) and from gravity, friction, air resistance and reaction forces (external).

02

Newton's third law — every action has an equal and opposite reaction — explains propulsion: a sprinter drives backwards against the starting blocks and the ground, and the ground pushes the athlete forwards with an equal and opposite reaction force.

03

The centre of mass is the point where the body's mass is balanced; its height, together with the size of the base of support and the body's mass, determines stability, which tacklers, gymnasts and wrestlers manipulate deliberately.

04

Friction between shoe and surface provides the grip needed for acceleration and changes of direction; too little causes slipping, while excessive friction wastes energy in endurance events, hence different stud patterns for dry and wet pitches.

05

Air resistance (drag) and water resistance oppose motion at higher speeds; streamlining the body and equipment, drafting behind opponents and aerodynamic suits and helmets all reduce drag.

06

Projectiles follow a curved path set by the speed, angle and height of release plus gravity and air resistance; a release angle near 45 degrees gives maximum range, adjusted slightly below 45 degrees for shots released above landing level, as in the shot put.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
ForceA push or pull that changes the motion, speed, direction or shape of a body, measured in newtons.
Centre of massThe point at which a body's mass is considered concentrated and about which it balances.
Base of supportThe area enclosed by the points of contact between the body and the ground, which with mass and centre-of-mass height determines stability.
StabilityThe resistance of a body to being overturned, increased by a wider base of support, a lower centre of mass and greater mass.
FrictionA force between two surfaces that resists sliding, useful for grip in sport but wasteful when it opposes motion.
DragAir or water resistance opposing the motion of a body, which increases with speed and is reduced by streamlining.
StreamliningShaping the body or equipment into a smooth, narrow form to reduce drag from air or water.
ProjectileA body projected into the air whose range depends on its release speed, release angle and release height.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Answer how-to-improve-stability with the three controls — widen the base, lower the centre of mass, increase mass — one mark each, then apply them by naming a sporting example such as a rugby scrum or wrestling stance.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

State two ways an athlete can increase their stability and one way friction helps a sprinter.

Model answerAny two: widen the base of support (1); lower the centre of mass (1); increase body mass (1). Friction gives grip between the spikes and the track, so the sprinter can push back against the ground without slipping and accelerate forwards (1).
CHALLENGE

Using Newton's third law, explain how a swimmer propels themselves through the water, and how streamlining reduces drag.

Model answerNewton's third law states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction (1). The swimmer pushes backwards on the water with their hands and feet, and the water pushes forwards on the swimmer with an equal and opposite reaction force, driving them through the pool (1). Streamlining makes the body long, narrow and horizontal, reducing the turbulent wake and pressure drag around it (1). With less drag, less of the swimmer's force is wasted overcoming resistance, so more of each stroke converts into forward speed (1).
