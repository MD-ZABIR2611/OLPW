Weathering & Soils

Weathering breaks down rock in place, soil builds up from the debris, and climate, rock type, vegetation and human activity together determine how fast both happen.

Key Concepts

01

Weathering is the breakdown and decay of rocks in situ (in place) by physical, chemical and biological processes; it differs from erosion, which involves the movement of material by gravity, rivers, wind or ice.

02

Physical (mechanical) weathering includes freeze-thaw, in which water freezing in cracks expands by about 9%, widening joints until rock shatters — most effective where temperatures cross 0°C repeatedly; insolation weathering, in which heating by day and cooling by night expand and contract rock until outer layers peel off (exfoliation); and salt crystallisation in arid climates.

03

Chemical weathering includes carbonation, in which rainwater dissolves carbon dioxide to form weak carbonic acid that reacts with limestone (calcium carbonate) to form soluble calcium bicarbonate, producing karst scenery such as limestone pavements and caves; hydrolysis changes minerals such as feldspar in granite into clay.

04

Biological weathering widens cracks as plant roots grow (root wedging), mixes soil as burrowing animals dig, and speeds chemical attack as rotting vegetation releases organic acids.

05

Weathering is fastest in hot, wet tropical climates and where rocks are well-jointed on exposed slopes with large day-night temperature ranges; on gentle, well-vegetated slopes weathered material accumulates rather than being washed away.

06

Soil develops from weathered parent rock under the influence of climate, organisms, relief and time, forming horizons: O (organic litter), A (dark, humus-rich topsoil, minerals leached out), B (subsoil, minerals deposited by illuviation), C (weathered parent material) and R (bedrock); lateritic soils of the humid tropics are deep and red but so heavily leached they become infertile once cleared, while intensive farming strips humus and can cause erosion, salinisation and desertification.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
WeatheringThe in-situ breakdown and decay of rocks by physical, chemical and biological processes without material being transported away.
Freeze-thaw weatheringThe shattering of rock when water freezing in cracks expands by about 9%, widening the joints.
CarbonationChemical weathering of limestone by weak carbonic acid in rainwater, which dissolves the rock.
ExfoliationThe peeling away of outer rock layers as repeated heating and cooling cause expansion and contraction.
HumusDecayed organic matter in soil that darkens the topsoil, holds moisture and supplies plant nutrients.
Soil profileA vertical section through soil showing its horizons from organic litter (O) through topsoil (A) and subsoil (B) to parent material (C) and bedrock (R).
LeachingThe downward washing of dissolved minerals and nutrients out of the topsoil by percolating rainwater.
LateriteA deep red, iron-rich tropical soil that is heavily leached and becomes hard and infertile when cleared and dried.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Always separate weathering from erosion in the first line — weathering is in place, erosion involves movement — because many mark schemes award the distinction itself; then name each process, the climate that drives it, and one landform or soil effect.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Describe three types of weathering and name the climate or conditions in which each works fastest.

Model answerFreeze-thaw: water in cracks freezes and expands, shattering rock; fastest where temperatures repeatedly cross 0°C, e.g. mountain and mid-latitude climates (1). Carbonation: weak carbonic acid in rainwater dissolves limestone; fastest in wet climates on limestone, forming karst landforms (1). Insolation or exfoliation: heating by day and cooling by night make rock expand and contract, peeling off layers; fastest in hot deserts with large temperature ranges (1). Accept also biological weathering by growing roots or burrowing animals (1).
CHALLENGE

Explain how climate and human activity affect the formation and erosion of tropical soils.

Model answerHigh temperatures and heavy rain speed chemical weathering and humus decay, producing deep soils, but heavy rainfall also leaches nutrients downward out of the topsoil, leaving laterite that is infertile once trees are removed (1). Forest vegetation recycles nutrients quickly into new growth, storing them in biomass rather than soil (1). Clearing rainforest for farming or logging removes this protection: bare soil is baked hard, washed or blown away, and loses its humus (1). Overgrazing, repeated cropping without fertiliser, and irrigation in dry areas cause salinisation and desertification (1). Overall, tropical soils are productive only under continuous vegetation cover, so deforestation converts them rapidly into degraded land (1).
