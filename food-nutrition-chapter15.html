Protein Function in Cookery

Proteins are the cook's structural toolkit: heat, whisking and resting transform them in predictable ways, and controlling those changes decides whether food is tender, tough, airy or set.

Key Concepts

01

Heat denatures protein: the bonds holding its folded shape break, the molecule uncoils and the change is permanent; this is why egg white turns from clear to opaque white as it cooks.

02

Coagulation follows denaturation: unfolded protein molecules bond into a three-dimensional network that sets and traps water; eggs set between 62 and 65 °C, and overcooking squeezes out moisture, giving rubbery eggs or dry meat.

03

Tough meat softens with long, moist cooking: gentle heat converts the connective-tissue protein collagen into soft gelatin, whereas fast, dry, high heat shrinks and toughens the muscle proteins.

04

Gluten forms when water is worked into wheat flour: the proteins gliadin and glutenin link into an elastic network that stretches around gas bubbles, giving bread its structure; resting relaxes gluten so pastry does not shrink.

05

Foam formation traps air bubbles in a film of denatured protein: whisked egg white can expand up to eight times its volume because albumen unfolds at the air-water interface, and a trace of fat or yolk prevents foaming.

06

Gels set mixtures as protein networks hold liquid in a semi-solid structure; in custard, gelatinising starch and coagulating egg proteins work together, so the setting temperature rises and curdling is less likely.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
DenaturationThe permanent unfolding of a protein's structure by heat, acid, salt or agitation, changing its properties, e.g. egg white turning opaque and solid.
CoagulationThe setting of denatured protein molecules into a firm three-dimensional network that traps water, as when eggs or meat proteins cook.
GlutenThe elastic protein network formed from gliadin and glutenin in wheat flour when mixed with water, which traps gas and gives structure to doughs.
FoamA dispersion of air bubbles trapped and stabilised by a film of denatured protein, e.g. whisked egg white.
GelA semi-solid formed when a protein or starch network traps liquid within its structure, as in set custard, jellies and yoghurt.
CollagenThe tough connective-tissue protein in meat which converts to soft gelatin during long, moist cooking, tenderising stews and casseroles.
AlbumenThe main protein of egg white, which denatures when heated and forms the stabilising film when egg white is whisked into a foam.
CurdlingThe separation of a cooked mixture such as custard into solid protein curds and liquid when its proteins over-coagulate.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Link every cooking method to a named protein change: whisking to foaming, heating to denaturation and coagulation, resting to relaxing gluten and stewing to gelatinising collagen; examiners award marks for naming the process and the result in the food.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Name the protein change in each process: (a) boiling an egg, (b) whisking egg white into a foam, (c) stewing meat for two hours, (d) resting rolled-out pastry.

Model answer(a) Denaturation followed by coagulation as the egg white and yolk set (1). (b) Foam formation: air is trapped in a film of denatured albumen (1). (c) Collagen in connective tissue converts to soft gelatin, tenderising the meat (1). (d) The gluten network relaxes, so the pastry is less likely to shrink (1).
CHALLENGE

Explain why a custard thickened with flour is less likely to curdle than one made with eggs alone.

Model answerStarch granules gelatinise in the milk as it heats, swelling and bursting to thicken the mixture before the egg proteins fully coagulate (1). The gelatinised starch raises the temperature at which the custard sets, so the egg proteins coagulate more gradually and evenly instead of seizing into lumps (1). Starch interrupts and softens the egg protein network, holding water in the gel and preventing whey separation (1). With eggs alone the proteins reach coagulation temperature quickly, and hotspots make the network over-tighten and squeeze out liquid, which is curdling (1).
