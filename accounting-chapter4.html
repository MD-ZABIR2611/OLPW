The Cash Book and Petty Cash

Shows how to write up and balance two-column and three-column cash books, record contra entries and cash discounts, and operate a petty cash book using the imprest system.

01

Overview

Cash is the lifeblood of any business, so receipts and payments must be recorded carefully and promptly. The cash book combines the cash account and the bank account in one book, making it both a book of prime entry and part of the double-entry ledger. Small everyday payments are recorded separately in the petty cash book, usually operated on the imprest system. Examination questions regularly ask you to prepare a three-column cash book from a list of transactions, post the discount totals, or complete an analysed petty cash book and restore the imprest. These are highly structured tasks where careful layout earns full marks.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain why the cash book is both a book of prime entry and part of the ledger.
  • Write up and balance a two-column cash book with cash and bank columns.
  • Record contra entries between cash and bank correctly.
  • Prepare a three-column cash book including discount allowed and discount received.
  • Post discount column totals to the nominal ledger.
  • Operate an analysed petty cash book using the imprest system.
  • Explain the advantages of the imprest system for controlling small payments.
02

The Role of the Cash Book

The cash book records all money received and paid, whether in notes and coins or through the bank account. It replaces separate cash and bank accounts in the ledger. Receipts are entered on the debit side because they increase the asset of cash or bank; payments are entered on the credit side because they reduce it. Each entry is then posted to the opposite side of the other account involved, completing the double entry.

Because the cash book contains the actual cash and bank accounts, it is part of the double-entry system, not just a list. At the same time, transactions are recorded in it for the first time, from receipts, cheque counterfoils and paying-in slips, so it is also a book of prime entry. This dual role is a favourite short examination question.

Keeping cash and bank side by side makes it easy for the owner to see how much money is available at any time, which helps with planning payments and avoiding unexpected overdrafts.

Key fact:Cash book debit side = receipts; credit side = payments.
03

The Two-Column Cash Book

A two-column cash book has a cash column and a bank column on each side. Each row shows the date, the details (the name of the other account) and the amount in the appropriate column. For example, cash sales of $400 are entered on the debit side in the cash column with the details 'Sales'. A cheque paid to a supplier is entered on the credit side in the bank column with the supplier's name.

The cash and bank columns are balanced separately at the end of the period in the same way as any ledger account. The cash column can never have a credit balance, because a business cannot pay out more notes and coins than it holds. The bank column can have a credit balance, which represents a bank overdraft. When bringing down balances, the cash balance always appears on the debit side, while the bank balance appears on the debit side if there is money in the bank or on the credit side if the account is overdrawn.

In an examination, rule off both columns on the same line, show 'Balance c/d' in each column and 'Balance b/d' on the first day of the next period.

04

Contra Entries

A contra entry is a transaction between the cash account and the bank account, both of which are in the cash book. The two most common are paying office cash into the bank and withdrawing cash from the bank for office use. Because both accounts are in the same book, both sides of the double entry appear in the cash book itself and no posting to any other ledger account is needed.

When cash is paid into the bank, the bank column is debited (bank increases) and the cash column is credited (cash decreases). The details show 'Cash' in the bank entry and 'Bank' in the cash entry, and each is marked with the letter C in the folio column to show it is a contra. When cash is withdrawn from the bank for business use, the cash column is debited and the bank column credited.

Be careful to distinguish between cash withdrawn for business use (a contra) and cash withdrawn by the owner for personal use, which is drawings: credit bank and debit drawings.

  • Cash paid into bank: Dr Bank column, Cr Cash column (both marked C).
  • Cash withdrawn from bank for office use: Dr Cash column, Cr Bank column (both marked C).
  • No entries in any other ledger account are needed for a contra.
05

The Three-Column Cash Book

A three-column cash book adds a discount column to each side. The debit side has a discount allowed column, recording cash discounts given to customers who pay promptly. The credit side has a discount received column, recording cash discounts received from suppliers when the business pays promptly. The discount columns are memorandum columns: they are not part of the double entry within the cash book and are not balanced.

When a customer pays, the amount actually received goes in the bank or cash column and the discount goes in the discount allowed column on the same line. The customer's account is then credited with both amounts, clearing the full debt. When the business pays a supplier, the amount paid goes in the bank or cash column on the credit side, the discount in the discount received column, and the supplier's account is debited with both amounts.

At the end of the period each discount column is totalled. The total of the discount allowed column is debited to the discount allowed account in the nominal ledger, because it is an expense. The total of the discount received column is credited to the discount received account, because it is income. This completes the double entry, since the personal accounts have already received the individual discount entries.

Key fact:Discount allowed total → Dr Discount allowed account. Discount received total → Cr Discount received account.
06

Petty Cash and the Imprest System

Many small payments, such as postage, cleaning materials, bus fares and tea for visitors, are too minor to be paid by cheque or bank transfer. Recording each one in the main cash book would clutter it, so these payments are handled by a petty cashier using a petty cash book. The main cashier is freed from routine work and the petty cashier gains experience and responsibility.

Under the imprest system, the petty cashier starts each period with a fixed amount of cash called the imprest, for example $200. Each payment must be supported by a signed petty cash voucher and receipt. At the end of the period, the main cashier gives the petty cashier a sum equal to the amount spent, which restores the float to the imprest amount. This reimbursement is recorded as a credit in the main cash book (usually the bank column) and a debit in the petty cash book.

The imprest system provides good control. At any time the cash in hand plus the total of vouchers should equal the imprest amount, so any shortage is quickly detected. The amount reimbursed is checked against the vouchers, and the petty cashier is limited to spending no more than the imprest in one period.

  • Fixed float (imprest) given at start of each period.
  • Every payment supported by a numbered, authorised petty cash voucher.
  • At period end the amount spent is reimbursed, restoring the imprest.
  • Cash in hand + vouchers = imprest at all times.
07

The Analysed Petty Cash Book

The petty cash book has a receipts column on the left, followed by date, details and voucher number, a total payments column, and several analysis columns such as postage, stationery, cleaning, travel and ledger accounts. Each payment is entered in the total column and again in the relevant analysis column. At the end of the period, the analysis columns are totalled and cross-checked: the sum of the analysis column totals must equal the total payments column.

The analysis column totals are posted to the debit side of the relevant expense accounts in the nominal ledger, for example 'Postage $30'. This is efficient because only one posting per expense type is needed rather than one per voucher. A ledger accounts column is used for payments to credit suppliers from petty cash; each item in this column is debited individually to the supplier's account in the purchases ledger.

The petty cash book is then balanced: the balance c/d is the cash remaining before reimbursement. It is brought down, and the reimbursement from the main cashier is entered in the receipts column to restore the imprest.

08

Advantages of the Cash Book System

The cash book system gives the owner up-to-date information about liquid resources, which is vital for day-to-day management. The bank column can later be compared with the bank statement in a bank reconciliation, which you will study in Chapter 7. Discount columns reduce the number of postings, because only the totals go to the discount accounts.

The petty cash book reduces the workload of the main cashier, gives control over small payments through the imprest, and analyses minor expenses so that totals can be posted efficiently. Together, the two books form a strong system of control over the most easily misused asset in any business: money.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Cash bookA book of prime entry that also forms part of the ledger, containing the cash account and bank account.
Two-column cash bookA cash book with a cash column and a bank column on each side.
Three-column cash bookA cash book with cash, bank and discount columns on each side.
Contra entryAn entry where both sides of the double entry appear in the cash book, such as cash paid into the bank.
Bank overdraftA credit balance on the bank account, meaning the business owes the bank money; a current liability.
Discount allowedCash discount given to credit customers for prompt payment; an expense of the business.
Discount receivedCash discount received from credit suppliers for prompt payment; income of the business.
Memorandum columnA column used for information only and not part of the double entry, such as the discount columns in the cash book.
Petty cash bookA book used to record small cash payments, usually analysed into expense columns.
Imprest systemA system in which the petty cashier starts each period with a fixed float that is restored by reimbursing the amount spent.
ImprestThe fixed amount of cash held by the petty cashier at the start of each period.
Petty cash voucherA document supporting each petty cash payment, showing the amount, purpose and authorisation.
Analysis columnA column in the petty cash book used to group payments of the same type, whose total is posted to the ledger.

Worked Examples

Preparing a three-column cash book

On 1 May, Leila's cash book showed cash $350 and bank $4,200 (debit). Transactions: 3 May, received a cheque from A. Bose for $1,470 in full settlement of $1,500; 6 May, cash sales $620; 9 May, paid C. Diaz by cheque $2,925 in full settlement of $3,000; 14 May, paid $500 cash into the bank; 20 May, paid rent by cheque $1,100; 27 May, paid wages in cash $380. Prepare the three-column cash book and balance it at 31 May.

  1. Debit side: 1 May Balances b/d cash $350, bank $4,200; 3 May A. Bose bank $1,470, discount allowed $30; 6 May Sales cash $620; 14 May Cash (C) bank $500.
  2. Credit side: 9 May C. Diaz bank $2,925, discount received $75; 14 May Bank (C) cash $500; 20 May Rent bank $1,100; 27 May Wages cash $380.
  3. Cash column: debits $350 + $620 = $970; credits $500 + $380 = $880; balance c/d $90 on the credit side.
  4. Bank column: debits $4,200 + $1,470 + $500 = $6,170; credits $2,925 + $1,100 = $4,025; balance c/d $2,145 on the credit side.
  5. Totals: cash $970 both sides; bank $6,170 both sides; discount allowed $30; discount received $75. Bring down on 1 June: cash $90 Dr, bank $2,145 Dr.
  6. Postings: Cr A. Bose $1,470 and $30; Dr C. Diaz $2,925 and $75; Dr Discount allowed $30; Cr Discount received $75.
Balances b/d on 1 June: cash $90 (debit) and bank $2,145 (debit). Discount allowed $30 is debited and discount received $75 credited in the nominal ledger.

Analysed petty cash book with imprest

The petty cashier of Rowan Ltd has an imprest of $200. In June she paid: 2 June postage $18 (voucher 41); 5 June cleaning $35 (42); 9 June stationery $22 (43); 14 June taxi fare $40 (44); 20 June postage $12 (45); 26 June J. Tan, a credit supplier, $30 (46). Show the analysis, balance the book and restore the imprest on 30 June.

  1. Step 1: Enter 1 June Balance b/d $200 in the receipts column.
  2. Step 2: Enter each payment in the total column and in the analysis columns: Postage $18 + $12 = $30; Cleaning $35; Stationery $22; Travel $40; Ledger (J. Tan) $30.
  3. Step 3: Total payments = $18 + $35 + $22 + $40 + $12 + $30 = $157. Cross-check: $30 + $35 + $22 + $40 + $30 = $157.
  4. Step 4: Balance c/d = $200 − $157 = $43. Bring down on 1 July (or 30 June) Balance b/d $43.
  5. Step 5: Restore the imprest: receipts column 'Bank $157'; credit the bank column of the main cash book $157. New balance = $43 + $157 = $200.
  6. Step 6: Post totals: Dr Postage $30, Dr Cleaning $35, Dr Stationery $22, Dr Travel $40, Dr J. Tan $30.
Total spent $157; closing cash $43; reimbursement of $157 restores the imprest to $200.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Showing a credit balance on the cash column. Cash in hand can never be negative; recheck your entries if this happens.
  • Balancing the discount columns. Discount columns are memorandum totals only; total them but do not balance them.
  • Posting discount allowed to the credit of the discount allowed account. Discount allowed is an expense and is debited.
  • Posting contra entries to a separate ledger account. Both sides of a contra are within the cash book.
  • Crediting the customer only with the cash received. The customer's account must be credited with both the cash received and the discount allowed.
  • Reimbursing the petty cashier with the full imprest rather than the amount spent. Only the amount spent is reimbursed.

Exam tips

  • Draw the cash book layout carefully first: Date, Details, Folio, Discount, Cash, Bank on each side.
  • Put the amount received or paid and the discount on the same line so the examiner can follow your entries.
  • Mark contra entries with C in the folio column to show you understand they need no further posting.
  • When a question says 'in full settlement', calculate the discount as the difference between the debt and the amount paid.
  • In petty cash questions, always cross-cast the analysis columns to check they agree with the total column.
  • State whether a bank balance brought down is a debit (money in bank) or credit (overdraft) when interpreting it.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. Explain why the cash book is both a book of prime entry and part of the ledger.

Model answerTransactions are first recorded in the cash book from source documents, so it is a book of prime entry (1). It contains the cash and bank accounts themselves, so it forms part of the double-entry ledger (1).
[2 marks]

2. What is meant by a contra entry? Give an example.

Model answerAn entry where both the debit and credit are in the cash book (1). Example: cash paid into the bank — debit bank column, credit cash column (1).
[2 marks]

3. State the double entry for the total of the discount allowed column in the cash book of $145.

Model answerDebit Discount allowed account $145 (1). The credit entries have already been made in the individual customers' accounts (1).
[3 marks]

4. A customer, K. Adeyemi, owed $2,400. He paid by cheque after deducting 2.5% cash discount. Calculate the cheque amount and state the entries in the cash book and his account.

Model answerDiscount = $60; cheque = $2,340 (1). Cash book debit side: bank $2,340, discount allowed $60 (1). K. Adeyemi account credited with Bank $2,340 and Discount allowed $60 (1).
[2 marks]

5. Explain why the bank column of a cash book may have a credit balance but the cash column cannot.

Model answerA credit balance on the bank column means the business has an overdraft, having paid out more than it had in the bank with the bank's agreement (1). The business cannot pay out more notes and coins than it physically holds, so cash cannot have a credit balance (1).
[4 marks]

6. Describe how the imprest system operates.

Model answerThe petty cashier is given a fixed amount of cash (the imprest) at the start of a period (1). Payments are made against authorised vouchers (1). At the end of the period the main cashier reimburses the amount spent (1), restoring the balance to the imprest amount (1).
[2 marks]

7. State two advantages of using a petty cash book.

Model answerRelieves the main cashier of recording many small payments (1). Analysis columns allow totals to be posted, reducing ledger entries (1). Also accept: control through imprest; gives training to junior staff.
[2 marks]

8. The petty cashier's imprest is $150. At the end of the month vouchers total $118. How much cash should be in the petty cash box, and how much will be reimbursed?

Model answerCash in hand should be $150 − $118 = $32 (1). Reimbursement is $118 (1).
[4 marks]

9. Prepare the bank column entries only and balance at 31 March: 1 March overdraft $850; 5 March received cheque from B. Oni $2,200; 12 March paid electricity $340; 20 March paid J. Wu $1,650; 28 March cash paid into bank $400.

Model answerCredit side: Balance b/d $850, Electricity $340, J. Wu $1,650 = $2,840 (1). Debit side: B. Oni $2,200, Cash (C) $400 = $2,600 (1). Balance c/d $240 on the debit side (1). Balance b/d $240 on the credit side on 1 April, an overdraft (1).
[2 marks]

10. The total of the analysis columns in a petty cash book does not agree with the total payments column. Suggest two possible reasons.

Model answerA payment has been entered in the total column but omitted from an analysis column (1). An amount has been entered incorrectly (transposed or wrong figure) in one of the columns (1). Also accept: casting error in a column total.
[8 marks]

11. Prepare a three-column cash book for July from: 1 July cash $180, bank $2,600; 4 July paid T. Rahman $1,940 by cheque in full settlement of $2,000; 9 July received cash $485 from G. Bell in full settlement of $500; 15 July paid $500 cash into bank; 22 July cash purchases $95; 30 July received cheque $760 from H. Iqbal. Balance the cash book.

Model answerDebit side: Balances b/d cash $180, bank $2,600 (1); G. Bell cash $485, discount allowed $15 (1); Cash (C) bank $500 (1); H. Iqbal bank $760 (1). Credit side: T. Rahman bank $1,940, discount received $60 (1); Bank (C) cash $500; Purchases cash $95 (1). Cash: debits $665, credits $595, balance c/d $70 (1). Bank: debits $3,860, credits $1,940, balance c/d $1,920; both balances brought down on the debit side on 1 August (1).
[6 marks]

12. Evaluate the usefulness of the imprest system as a method of controlling petty cash.

Model answerThe fixed float limits the amount at risk at any time (1). Cash in hand plus vouchers must equal the imprest, so shortages are quickly detected (1). Reimbursement is made only on production of vouchers, so each payment is checked (1). Petty cashier is accountable for the float (1). However, it cannot prevent a voucher being falsified or unauthorised spending within the limit (1), and the float may be too small in busy periods (1). Conclusion: it is an effective and simple control for small payments (1). Max 6.

14

Quick Recap

  1. The cash book records all receipts (debit) and payments (credit) of cash and bank, and is both a book of prime entry and part of the ledger.
  2. The cash column can never have a credit balance; a credit balance on the bank column is an overdraft.
  3. Contra entries between cash and bank appear on both sides of the cash book and need no other posting.
  4. The three-column cash book records cash discounts in memorandum discount columns.
  5. Discount allowed total is debited to the discount allowed account; discount received total is credited to the discount received account.
  6. The imprest system gives the petty cashier a fixed float, restored at the end of each period by the amount spent.
  7. Analysis column totals from the petty cash book are posted to the debit of expense accounts.
