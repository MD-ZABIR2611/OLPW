The Timed Examination: Planning and Execution

The externally set assignment ends in an eight-hour supervised test, but the result is largely decided in the preparatory period of research, experiments and a clear final design.

08Key terms

01

Key Concepts

01

The externally set assignment offers a choice of starting points; candidates should select the brief that offers the most personal, researchable line of enquiry rather than the most obvious image.

02

The preparatory period is for collecting sources, researching relevant artists and testing media and compositions; the timed test then focuses on producing the final outcome from that preparation.

03

Timetabling the eight hours — for example two hours to establish the composition, four hours to develop the main work and two hours to resolve and refine — prevents under-finished outcomes.

04

Materials and equipment must be planned in advance: surfaces prepared, palettes and mediums ready, and backup media chosen in case a first choice fails within the time available.

05

Working at an appropriate scale matters: a scale too large for eight hours risks an unfinished surface, while one too small may not showcase ambition; full-size thumbnail sketches settle this in the preparatory period.

06

Moderators view the preparatory studies and the timed outcome together, so annotations made during the test that link decisions back to research help demonstrate a thinking process under time pressure.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Externally set assignmentThe exam component of 6090 in which candidates respond to a brief set by the board, ending in a supervised timed test of eight hours.
Preparatory periodThe weeks of research, experimentation and planning between receiving the paper and the timed test.
Timed testThe eight hours of supervised, unaided work in which the final response to the externally set assignment is produced.
Starting pointOne of the briefs or themes offered on the exam paper from which the candidate's investigation develops.
Thumbnail sketchA small, rapid compositional sketch used to test layout, tone and scale before committing to a final piece.
Time planA schedule dividing the test hours into stages of establishing, developing and resolving the outcome.
Supporting studiesThe preparatory research, experiments and annotations submitted together with the final exam outcome.
ResolutionThe stage of finishing an artwork decisively, unifying edges, tonal balance and focal points so it reads as complete.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Practise the whole process before the exam: set yourself eight hours on a mock brief with the same materials, so you know how long your working stages really take and can adjust your plan.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Outline the two parts of the externally set assignment and how the eight hours of the timed test are used.

Model answerA preparatory period follows receipt of the paper, used for research, experiments and planning (1). The timed test is eight hours of supervised, unaided work producing the final outcome (1). The time is typically divided between establishing the composition, developing the main work and resolving and refining it (1). The supporting studies are submitted together with the timed outcome for assessment (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate the importance of planning and time management compared with technical skill in succeeding in the eight-hour test.

Model answerTechnical skill matters, but without a time plan a candidate can overwork one area and leave the outcome unfinished, and unfinished work loses marks regardless of quality (1). Planning in the preparatory period — thumbnail compositions, tested materials, chosen scale — means the eight hours are spent executing decisions already made (1). Good time management allows deliberate resolution, unifying edges and tonal balance, which is where marks for a realised outcome are won (1). However, planning cannot compensate for weak fundamental skills, so the strongest candidates combine both: solid technique disciplined by a realistic schedule (1).
