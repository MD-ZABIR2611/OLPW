THEORY

FOUNDATIONS // OBSERVATION & CLASSIFICATION
01 / THEORYQUALITATIVE vs QUANTITATIVECORE

Experimental chemistry is built on two kinds of evidence. Qualitative observations describe what happens. Quantitative measurements state exactly how much, how hot, how long.

Every practical investigation in this chapter uses both: you observe a colour change (qualitative) and you record a temperature of 78 °C (quantitative). Examiners reward precise language — never write "it went a bit cloudy" when you can write "a white precipitate formed".

01.1 / QUALITATIVEDESCRIPTIONS
  • Colour change: "blue solution turned colourless"
  • Gas evolved: "effervescence / bubbles formed"
  • Precipitate: "a white insoluble solid formed"
  • State change: "the solid melted / sublimed"
  • Odour (with caution): "a pungent gas was released"
01.2 / QUANTITATIVEMEASUREMENTS
  • Mass: 12.50 g (electronic balance, 2 d.p.)
  • Volume: 25.0 cm³ (pipette — accurate)
  • Temperature: 78 °C (thermometer)
  • Time: 34.2 s (stopwatch)
  • Every measurement = number + unit
01.3 / CLASSIFICATIONMIXTURES — ELEMENTS — COMPOUNDSDATASHEET
CLASSDEFINITIONBONDS BETWEEN PARTICLESMELTING / BOILING POINTSEPARATED BY

EXAM INTEL

Because mixtures are not chemically bonded, they can be separated by physical methods that exploit differences in particle size, solubility, boiling point or adsorption. If the exam asks "why does this method work?" — always name the property being exploited.

01.4 / SAFETYLABORATORY SAFETY PROTOCOLMANDATORY
  1. Eye protection: wear safety goggles whenever chemicals or heating are involved — not optional.
  2. Long hair tied back; loose sleeves secured before using any flame.
  3. Flammable liquids: never heat with a naked flame — use a water bath or electric heater.
  4. Heating test tubes: point the mouth away from people; heat the liquid, not the bottom rim; move the tube in and out of the flame.
  5. Hot glassware looks identical to cold glassware — use tongs or heat-resistant gloves.
  6. Never taste chemicals. Smell gases only by wafting towards the nose, and only if instructed.
  7. Read hazard labels before use: flammable, toxic, corrosive, irritant, oxidising.
  8. Report all spills, breakages and injuries immediately.

02.2 / MEASURING MASSELECTRONIC BALANCE
  • Place balance on a flat, level, vibration-free surface.
  • Tare (zero) the balance with the empty container first.
  • Record to the precision of the balance — e.g. 12.50 g, not 12.5 g.
  • Close draught shields for very small masses.
EXAM INTEL

If a balance reads 0.04 g with nothing on it, that is a zero error — a systematic error. Every reading is too high by 0.04 g. Fix it by zeroing or subtracting.

02.3 / MEASURING VOLUMELIQUIDS & GASES
  • Place cylinder on a flat surface; bring your eye level with the liquid surface.
  • Read the bottom of the meniscus (for water and most liquids).
  • Wrong eye level ⇒ parallax error: reading too high or too low.
  • Accurate fixed volume ⇒ pipette; accurate variable volume ⇒ burette.
  • Gas volume ⇒ gas syringe or an inverted measuring cylinder over water.
02.4 / TEMPERATURETHERMOMETER
  • Wait until the reading stops changing, then record.
  • Bulb must be fully surrounded by the substance — not touching the container.
  • In distillation, the bulb sits level with the side-arm so it measures the vapour temperature.
  • Read at eye level; estimate between scale divisions.
02.5 / TIMESTOPWATCH / TIMER
  • Start the clock at a clearly defined moment (e.g. the instant reactants mix).
  • Record time intervals at fixed points (every 15 s, every 30 s) in rate experiments.
  • Human reaction time (~0.2 s) is a random error — reduce it by repeating and averaging.
02.6 / KEY TERMSACCURACY // PRECISION // ERRORDEFINITIONS
TERM
ACCURACY.
DEFINITION

How close a measurement is to the true / accepted value.

TERM
PRECISION.
DEFINITION

How close repeat measurements are to each other (small spread). A precise set can still be inaccurate.

TERM

DEFINITION

Unpredictable error that varies in either direction each reading. Reduced by repeating and taking an average.

TERM

DEFINITION

The same error in the same direction every time (e.g. zero error). Repeating does NOT fix it — recalibrate or correct the reading.

An instrument not reading zero at rest — e.g. a balance showing 0.04 g when empty. Shifts all readings equally.

DEFINITION

Apparent shift of a scale reading when viewed at an angle instead of at eye level — common with cylinders and thermometers.

03.3 / EVAPORATIONSOLVENT REMOVAL, TO DRYNESS

The solution is heated in an evaporating basin until all the solvent boils away, leaving the solid behind. Fast, but crude.

  • Suitable when the solute is stable on heating and you only need the solid, quickly.
  • Heat gently — vigorous boiling causes bumping and loss of solid (spitting).
  • Not suitable for hydrated salts — see crystallisation below.
EXAM INTEL

Evaporation leaves a dry powder but gives no crystals and may decompose heat-sensitive solids. When the question says "pure, dry crystals" — the answer is crystallisation, not evaporation to dryness.

03.4 / CRYSTALLISATIONPURE CRYSTALS FROM SOLUTIONKEY TECHNIQUE

The solution is heated to concentrate it, then left to cool slowly. As the solvent evaporates and the temperature falls, the solute becomes less soluble and forms ordered crystals.

  1. Heat the solution gently to evaporate some solvent.
  2. Test for the crystallisation point: dip a glass rod in, remove it — if small crystals form on the rod as it cools, stop heating.
  3. Leave the concentrated solution to cool slowly — slow cooling gives large, pure crystals.
  4. Filter off the crystals and dry them between sheets of filter paper.
REACTION 01 // HYDRATED SALT WARNING
CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) —blue crystals → CuSO₄(s) + 5H₂O(g) —white powder

03.5 / SIMPLE DISTILLATIONPURE SOLVENT FROM A SOLUTIONFIG.02

A solution (e.g. salt water) is boiled. The solvent vaporises, is cooled and condensed back to liquid, and is collected — the distillate. The solute (salt) has no boiling point change at 100 °C and stays behind in the flask.

  • Thermometer position is critical: the bulb must be level with the side-arm so it reads the temperature of the vapour — i.e. the boiling point of the distillate.
  • Condenser water flows in at the bottom, out at the top (counter-current) so the jacket stays completely full of cold water and cooling is efficient.
  • The liquid collected is the distillate — here, pure water.
  • Never distil to dryness — the flask can crack and, in a sealed system, pressure can build dangerously.
EXAM INTEL

Distillation cycle to memorise: H₂O(l) → heat → H₂O(g) → cool/condense → H₂O(l). Evaporation followed by condensation. The salt does not evaporate because it has an extremely high boiling point.

03.6 / FRACTIONAL DISTILLATIONMISCIBLE LIQUIDS // DIFFERENT BOILING POINTSFIG.03

Used to separate two (or more) miscible liquids whose boiling points differ — e.g. ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C). A fractionating column packed with glass beads sits above the flask.

  • The mixture is heated; both liquids begin to evaporate, but the lower boiling point liquid (ethanol) vaporises more.
  • Vapour rises up the column. The column is hotter at the bottom, cooler at the top.
  • Glass beads provide a large surface area for repeated condensation and re-evaporation.
  • Each cycle enriches the vapour in the more volatile liquid. The higher boiling point liquid (water) condenses and drips back into the flask.
  • Only ethanol vapour reaches the top of the column at 78 °C, passes into the condenser, and is collected as the first fraction (distillate).
  • Later, the temperature at the thermometer rises to 100 °C — then water is collected as the second fraction.

ADSORPTION // Rf VALUES // IDENTIFICATION
04.1 / PRINCIPLESTATIONARY & MOBILE PHASESCORE
TERM
STATIONARY PHASE.

The phase that does not move — the filter paper (chromatography paper).

TERM
MOBILE PHASE.

The phase that moves — the solvent travelling up the paper.

TERM
SOLVENT.

The liquid that dissolves the substances and carries them up the paper (water or ethanol).

TERM
SOLUTE.

The dissolved substances being separated — the dyes.

TERM
CHROMATOGRAM.

The finished, developed paper showing the separated spots.

TERM
SOLVENT FRONT.

The furthest point reached by the solvent. Mark it immediately on removing the paper — it evaporates.

Separation happens because each dye has a different balance of solubility in the solvent and adsorption (sticking) to the paper. Highly soluble, weakly adsorbed dyes travel furthest; strongly adsorbed dyes stay near the baseline.

04.2 / METHODRUNNING A CHROMATOGRAM
  1. Draw the start line with a pencil — pencil is insoluble, so it will not dissolve or separate.
  2. Place a small spot of each sample on the baseline. Let it dry; re-spot to concentrate it.
  3. Stand the paper in a beaker with the solvent below the baseline — spots must not touch the solvent.
  4. Cover the beaker (a lid) to prevent the solvent evaporating.
  5. Wait for the solvent to rise near the top. Remove and immediately mark the solvent front.
  6. Dry the paper. Measure distances and calculate Rf values; compare spots with known dyes.

PURITY OF SUBSTANCES

TESTING // MELTING POINT // BOILING POINT
05.1 / DEFINITIONWHAT "PURE" MEANS

A pure substance contains only one element or one compound — a single substance, nothing else mixed in. A pure substance has a sharp, fixed melting point and boiling point.

  • Pure water melts at exactly 0 °C and boils at exactly 100 °C (at standard pressure).
  • An impure substance melts/boils over a range of temperatures, not sharply.
05.2 / TESTINGHOW TO TEST PURITY
  1. Melting/boiling point test: heat the sample and measure the temperature at which it changes state. A sharp temperature matching data = pure. Melting over a range = impure.
  2. Effect of impurity: impurities lower the melting point and raise the boiling point of a substance.
  3. Chromatography test (for dyes/coloured substances): one spot only = pure; multiple spots = a mixture.
EXAM INTEL

Classic exam line: "Impurities lower the melting point and raise the boiling point." Learn it word-for-word. It also explains why salt water boils above 100 °C and why salt is spread on icy roads.

07.3 / SKILLDRAWING & READING GRAPHSCHECKLIST

DRAWING — CHECKLIST

  • Axes labelled with quantity and unit: "Temperature / °C" against "Time / s".
  • Independent variable on the x-axis; dependent on the y-axis.
  • Sensible scale — use at least half the grid in both directions; easy divisions (1, 2, 5, 10).
  • Plot points precisely with sharp pencil crosses.
  • Draw a single smooth line or line of best fit — do not dot-to-dot; it does not have to pass through the origin.

READING — RULES

  • Interpolation = reading between plotted points (reliable).
  • Extrapolation = extending beyond the data (state that it is an estimate).
  • A plateau = state change at constant temperature (freezing/boiling) or a pure fraction distilling.
  • Steeper gradient = faster change (e.g. faster rate of reaction).
  • Always quote graph readings with units.

EXPERIMENT 01

SEPARATION OF SAND + SALT — FILTRATION & CRYSTALLISATION

DURATION ~45 MIN // HAZARD: LOW
AIM

To separate a mixture of sand and sodium chloride, obtaining a sample of pure dry sand and a sample of pure dry salt crystals.

APPARATUS
  • Beakers, glass rod, stirrer
  • Filter funnel + filter paper
  • Evaporating basin, tripod, gauze, heat-resistant mat
  • Bunsen burner, tongs
  • Distilled water
METHOD
  1. Place the sand/salt mixture in a beaker. Add approximately 50 cm³ of distilled water and stir with a glass rod until the salt dissolves completely.
  2. Fold a piece of filter paper into a cone, place it in the funnel and wet it with a little distilled water so it sticks.
  3. Place the funnel over a clean beaker and pour the mixture down the glass rod into the funnel.
  4. Rinse the original beaker with a small volume of distilled water and pour the rinsings through the funnel — this transfers ALL the salt solution.
  5. Wash the sand (residue) in the funnel with a little distilled water; leave it to dry on fresh filter paper.
  6. Pour the filtrate (salt solution) into an evaporating basin. Heat gently, stirring, until a glass rod dipped in and removed shows small crystals forming — the crystallisation point.
  7. Stop heating. Leave the concentrated solution to cool and crystallise slowly.
  8. Filter off the salt crystals and dry them between sheets of filter paper.
OBSERVATIONS

RESIDUE: pale solid trapped on filter paper (sand)

FILTRATE: clear, colourless liquid (salt solution)

ON COOLING: small cubic crystals form in the basin

RESULT

Sample 1: pure dry sand (residue). Sample 2: pure dry sodium chloride crystals. The mixture was separated by particle size/solubility (filtration) then solubility vs temperature (crystallisation).

SAFETY
  • Wear safety goggles throughout.
  • Stand the Bunsen burner on a heat-resistant mat; use a yellow (safety) flame when not heating.
  • Use tongs for the hot evaporating basin.
ERRORS
  • Salt solution left clinging to the sand — impure sand sample.
  • Heating past the crystallisation point — small, impure crystals or loss of water of crystallisation.
  • Torn filter paper — sand contaminates the filtrate.
IMPROVEMENTS
  • Rinse the residue with more distilled water and test the washings for salt (evaporate a drop).
  • Test with the glass rod regularly; stop heating at the first sign of crystals.
  • Inspect the filter paper for tears before filtering.
EXAM QUESTION // WHY RINSE THE RESIDUE WITH DISTILLED WATER?

To wash off the salt solution clinging to the sand grains. Without rinsing, dried salt would remain mixed with the sand and the sand sample would not be pure.

EXPERIMENT 02

PURE WATER FROM SALT SOLUTION — SIMPLE DISTILLATION

DURATION ~40 MIN // HAZARD: HEAT
AIM

To obtain a sample of pure water (distillate) from aqueous sodium chloride, leaving the salt in the flask.

APPARATUS
  • Round-bottom flask, still head + thermometer
  • Liebig condenser + rubber tubing for coolant
  • Receiving beaker; stand, clamps, boss
  • Heat source; anti-bumping granules
METHOD
  1. Pour the salt solution into the flask to about one-third full. Add a few anti-bumping granules.
  2. Assemble the apparatus. Position the thermometer so the bulb is level with the side-arm to the condenser.
  3. Connect the cooling water so it enters at the bottom of the condenser jacket and leaves at the top.
  4. Heat the flask steadily. The water boils at 100 °C and the thermometer reading becomes steady.
  5. Steam passes into the condenser, condenses to liquid, and runs into the receiving beaker as the distillate.
  6. Stop heating before the flask is dry. Check the distillate boils at 100 °C to confirm purity.
OBSERVATIONS

THERMOMETER: steady at ~100 °C

CONDENSER: liquid drops running steadily into beaker

FLASK (END): white salt crystals remain as water diminishes

RESULT

Distillate = pure water (constant boiling point of 100 °C). Sodium chloride remains in the flask — it is non-volatile at 100 °C. Separation exploited the difference in boiling point.

SAFETY
  • Never distil to dryness — risk of flask cracking.
  • Clamp all glassware securely; keep tubing away from the flame.
  • The condenser and flask remain hot — allow to cool before dismantling.
ERRORS
  • Thermometer bulb too high/low — inaccurate boiling point reading.
  • Coolant connected in reverse — jacket not full, vapour not fully condensed, distillate lost.
  • Loose joints — vapour escapes, yield reduced.
IMPROVEMENTS
  • Check bulb alignment against the side-arm before heating.
  • Connect coolant counter-current (in at bottom).
  • Heat gently — a steady boil, not a violent one.
EXAM QUESTION // WHY MUST THE THERMOMETER BULB BE LEVEL WITH THE SIDE-ARM?

So that the bulb measures the temperature of the vapour entering the condenser — i.e. the boiling point of the substance being collected. Positioned in the liquid, it would read the solution temperature, not confirm which substance is distilling.

EXPERIMENT 03

DYES IN BLACK INK — PAPER CHROMATOGRAPHY

DURATION ~30 MIN // HAZARD: SOLVENT — FLAMMABLE
AIM

To separate the dyes in black ink and determine how many dyes it contains, calculating their Rf values.

APPARATUS
  • Chromatography paper; pencil; ruler
  • Beaker + lid (or boiling tube); solvent (water or ethanol)
  • Capillary tube / teat pipette; known reference dyes
METHOD
  1. Rule a pencil baseline 1 cm from the bottom edge of the paper.
  2. Place one small spot of black ink on the baseline using a capillary tube. Dry it, then re-spot on the same place to concentrate the sample.
  3. Stand the paper in the beaker containing solvent to a depth of less than 1 cm — the baseline and spots must stay above the solvent.
  4. Cover the beaker with a lid to stop the solvent evaporating.
  5. When the solvent has risen to near the top of the paper, remove it and immediately mark the solvent front with a pencil.
  6. Allow the paper to dry. Measure the distance from the baseline to the solvent front and to the centre of each spot. Calculate Rf values and compare with reference dyes run on the same paper.
OBSERVATIONS

PAPER: solvent rises steadily; baseline stays fixed

SPOT: black ink separates into 3 coloured spots at different heights

SOLVENT FRONT: marked at highest point of dampness

RESULT

The black ink is a mixture of three dyes (three spots). Separation exploited differences in solubility / adsorption. Rf = distance of spot centre ÷ distance of solvent front, for each dye.

SAFETY
  • If ethanol is the solvent: no naked flames anywhere nearby.
  • Work in a ventilated room; wear goggles with organic solvents.
ERRORS
  • Baseline drawn in ink — it dissolves and contaminates the chromatogram.
  • Spots too large — dyes smear into each other.
  • Solvent level above the baseline — dyes wash into the solvent.
  • Solvent front left unmarked — paper dries and the front vanishes.
IMPROVEMENTS
  • Always use a pencil baseline.
  • Use small, concentrated spots — spot, dry, re-spot.
  • Cover the beaker and mark the front the instant the paper is lifted.
  • Repeat with the same solvent for consistent Rf values.
EXAM QUESTION // WHY MUST THE START LINE BE DRAWN IN PENCIL?

Pencil "ink" is graphite, which is insoluble in the solvent. Pen ink would dissolve and travel up the paper, separating alongside the sample and ruining the chromatogram.

Q01MARKS: 02DIFFICULTY: BASIC

State the difference between accuracy and precision.

MARK SCHEME

EXPLANATION

A set of readings can be precise (very similar to each other) yet inaccurate — if a systematic error such as a zero error shifts every reading the same way.

Q02MARKS: 02DIFFICULTY: BASIC

A student reads the volume of liquid in a measuring cylinder. Describe two things they must do to avoid parallax error.

MARK SCHEME
  • Place the cylinder on a flat surface and bring the eye level with the liquid surface [1]
  • Read the bottom of the meniscus [1]
EXPLANATION

Viewing from above or below makes the mark appear shifted against the scale — this apparent shift is parallax error, a random error between readings.

Q03MARKS: 04DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

A student has a hot, concentrated solution of copper(II) sulfate. Describe how to obtain a sample of pure, dry crystals of copper(II) sulfate from this solution.

MARK SCHEME
  • Heat to further concentrate the solution until the crystallisation point — crystals form on a cooled glass rod dipped in [1]
  • Stop heating and leave the solution to cool slowly so crystals form [1]
  • Filter off the crystals [1]
  • Dry between sheets of filter paper [1]
EXPLANATION

Slow cooling allows ordered crystal growth — large, pure crystals. Heating to dryness would drive off the water of crystallisation and destroy the blue crystals.

Q04MARKS: 03DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

On a chromatogram, a dye moves 5.0 cm from the baseline. The solvent front moved 8.0 cm. Calculate the Rf value of the dye.

FORMULA

Rf = distance moved by substance ÷ distance moved by solvent front

WORKING

Rf = 5.0 ÷ 8.0

MARK SCHEME
  • Correct formula or substitution seen [1]
  • Rf = 0.625 [2]
EXPLANATION

Rf has no units — it is a ratio — and is always between 0 and 1 because the solvent front travels furthest.

Q05MARKS: 03DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

A food colouring produces three spots on its chromatogram. Explain what this shows about the food colouring and why the dyes end up at different heights.

MARK SCHEME
  • The food colouring is a mixture, not a pure substance [1]
  • It contains three different dyes [1]
  • Each dye has a different solubility in the solvent / adsorption to the paper, so each travels a different distance [1]
EXPLANATION

More soluble + less strongly adsorbed ⇒ travels further (higher Rf). Strongly adsorbed dyes cling to the stationary phase and stay near the baseline.

Q06MARKS: 06DIFFICULTY: ADVANCED

Ethanol boils at 78 °C and water at 100 °C. The two liquids are completely miscible. Describe how fractional distillation separates the mixture, and explain the role of the fractionating column.

MARK SCHEME
  • Heat the mixture in a flask [1]
  • Ethanol has the lower boiling point, so it vaporises more / first [1]
  • Vapour rises up the fractionating column; column is hotter at the bottom, cooler at the top [1]
  • Glass beads provide a large surface area for repeated condensation and re-evaporation [1]
  • Water condenses on the beads and runs back into the flask; only ethanol vapour reaches the top [1]
  • Ethanol vapour enters the condenser, condenses, and is collected as the distillate / first fraction at 78 °C [1]
EXPLANATION

Every condensation–evaporation cycle on the beads enriches the vapour in the more volatile liquid (ethanol). When the thermometer later rises to 100 °C, water is collected as the second fraction.

Q07MARKS: 03DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

Before any object is placed on it, an electronic balance reads 0.04 g. Name this type of error, state its effect on all the mass readings, and describe how to correct it.

MARK SCHEME
  • Zero error — a systematic error [1]
  • Every reading is 0.04 g too high (all shifted the same way) [1]
  • Tare / zero the balance before use — or subtract 0.04 g from every reading [1]
EXPLANATION

Repeating the measurements would NOT remove this error — systematic errors must be corrected at the instrument or in the calculation.

Q08MARKS: 02DIFFICULTY: INTERMEDIATE

Explain why crystallisation, rather than evaporation to dryness, is used to obtain crystals of a hydrated salt such as CuSO₄·5H₂O.

MARK SCHEME
  • Heating to dryness drives off the water of crystallisation [1]
  • Crystals lose their shape / decompose — blue CuSO₄·5H₂O turns to white anhydrous CuSO₄ powder [1]
EXPLANATION

Stop heating at the crystallisation point and cool slowly — this preserves the hydrated crystal structure and gives large, pure crystals.

Q09MARKS: 02DIFFICULTY: BASIC

State two safety precautions that should be taken when heating a flammable liquid in the laboratory.

MARK SCHEME
  • Never use a naked flame — heat with an electric heater or water bath [1]
  • Wear safety goggles; keep the liquid container stoppered when not in use / work in a ventilated area [1]
EXPLANATION

Flammable vapours can ignite at a distant flame — the vapour, not the liquid, burns. This is why water baths are standard for organic solvents.

Q10MARKS: 06DIFFICULTY: ADVANCED

You are given a beaker containing a mixture of sand, sodium chloride and water. Describe how to obtain a sample of pure dry sand AND a sample of pure dry salt from this mixture.

MARK SCHEME
  • Add more water if needed and stir so the salt dissolves completely [1]
  • Filter the mixture: sand = residue; filtrate = salt solution [1]
  • Wash the residue with distilled water and dry it — pure sand [1]
  • Heat the filtrate in an evaporating basin to the crystallisation point (crystals form on a dipped, cooled glass rod) [1]
  • Stop heating and leave to cool slowly so salt crystals form [1]
  • Filter off the crystals and dry between filter papers — pure salt [1]
EXPLANATION

Two methods in sequence, each exploiting a different property: filtration separates by particle size / insolubility; crystallisation separates by solubility vs temperature. Washing the residue is the mark most candidates forget.
