Chapter 18 · Literature in English

Writing Literature Essays Under Time Pressure

The literature exam is won in the plan. This chapter gives the timed workflow: five-minute plans, paragraph architecture, quotation discipline and closing strategies that keep quality high when the clock is not.

Key Concepts

01

Use a five-minute plan per essay: decode the question's key word (how, why, in what ways), pick three or four argument points, and note one quotation per point before writing.

02

Answer the question asked, not the poem or novel you revised: circle the command and focus words, and check each paragraph against them — drift is the most common mark-loss.

03

Paragraphs follow PEEL: point, evidence (short quotation embedded in your sentence), explanation of technique and effect, and a link back to the question.

04

Embed short quotations (under ten words) inside your own syntax with a forward slash for line breaks; long, dropped-in quotations waste time and weaken control.

05

Manage the paper: know the mark-per-minute ratio (roughly a mark a minute), give each essay its full allocation, and never sacrifice the final essay by overrunning the first.

06

If time runs short, compress, don't abandon: state your remaining points briefly with the key quotation and a sentence of analysis each — partial structure still earns partial credit.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
PEEL paragraphA paragraph structured as Point, Evidence, Explanation and Link, ensuring every claim is supported and tied to the question.
Embedded quotationA short quotation woven grammatically into the student's own sentence, with line breaks marked by a slash.
Command wordThe verb in an essay question (analyse, compare, evaluate) that dictates the required thinking level.
Thesis-driven answerAn essay organised around a clear central argument developed consistently across every paragraph.
Time allocationThe planned division of exam minutes across questions in proportion to their marks.
Alternative interpretationA second, defensible reading of a text offered in evaluation to show critical awareness.
JudgementA reasoned final position on the question, weighing the evidence presented rather than repeating points.
Partial creditMarks earned for incomplete but relevant answers, rewarding structure and evidence even when a response is not finished.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

Practise the "three-point sprint": for past questions, spend four minutes producing only plan, thesis and one model PEEL paragraph — training planning speed matters more than writing full essays, because every exam disaster begins with a missing plan.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

State the four stages of a PEEL paragraph in order and explain the purpose of the final stage.

Model answerPoint: state the paragraph's single idea answering the question (1). Evidence: a short, embedded quotation supporting it (1). Explanation: analyse technique and effect (1). Link: connect the point back to the question's wording, keeping the essay on-task (1).
CHALLENGE

In a 75-minute paper with one 25-mark and one 20-mark essay, explain how you would allocate time and why, and what you would do if you overran the first essay.

Model answerAllocate roughly a mark a minute with planning inside each block: about 27 minutes (5 plan, 22 write) for the 25-mark essay and 22 minutes (4 plan, 18 write) for the 20-mark essay, keeping roughly 25 minutes for the second question plus a final check (1). Overrunning starves the second essay, which then loses more marks than the extra minutes gained, so stop mid-flow at the limit (1). If overrun happens: compress the remaining answer — give the thesis, each point in one sentence with its key quotation and one line of analysis — structured partial answers still earn substantial partial credit (1).
