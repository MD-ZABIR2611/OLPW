Nutrition for Sport, Pregnancy and Special Diets

Applied nutrition meets real lives: matching intake to training load, supporting a healthy pregnancy, and adapting meals safely for swallowing difficulties and allergic conditions.

Key Concepts

01

Energy balance matches intake to expenditure; athletes need extra carbohydrate to refill muscle glycogen, while chronic low energy availability causes RED-S, harming performance, bone health and hormones.

02

Protein needs rise with training to repair and build muscle — roughly 1.2 to 2.0 g per kilogram of body mass per day for serious athletes compared with about 0.75 g for sedentary adults — but surplus protein is stored as fat or used for energy, not automatically converted to muscle.

03

Pregnancy raises requirements for folate (to prevent neural tube defects), iron (for expanded blood volume), calcium, vitamin D and protein; guidance advises avoiding alcohol, limiting caffeine, and excluding high-mercury fish and unpasteurised cheeses that risk listeria.

04

Modified-texture diets — soft, minced, puréed or liquidised — are prescribed for dysphagia or after surgery; they must still meet nutritional needs, and fluids are thickened to graded consistencies so people with swallowing difficulty can hydrate safely.

05

Coeliac disease is an autoimmune reaction to gluten requiring a strict gluten-free diet for life; wheat allergy triggers immune reactions to wheat proteins, while lactose intolerance is an enzyme deficiency, not an allergy, managed by limiting lactose or using lactose-free products.

06

Allergies, intolerances and coeliac disease are managed by careful label checking, preventing cross-contact in the kitchen, and substituting equivalent nutrients, e.g. calcium from fortified plant milks when dairy is excluded.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Energy balanceThe relationship between energy consumed in food and energy expended; intake above expenditure leads to weight gain, below to weight loss.
GlycogenThe stored form of carbohydrate in liver and muscle, the main fuel for high-intensity exercise, refilled by carbohydrate-rich meals.
RED-SRelative Energy Deficiency in Sport: health and performance harm from chronically consuming too little energy for training demands.
Modified-texture dietA diet of softened, minced, puréed or liquidised food prescribed for people with chewing or swallowing difficulties.
DysphagiaDifficulty swallowing that may require texture-modified foods and thickened fluids to make eating and drinking safe.
Coeliac diseaseAn autoimmune disorder in which gluten damages the lining of the small intestine, managed with a strict lifelong gluten-free diet.
Food intoleranceA non-immune difficulty digesting a food, such as lactose intolerance, causing discomfort but not an allergic reaction.
AnaphylaxisA severe, potentially fatal allergic reaction requiring immediate treatment with adrenaline, triggered in susceptible people by foods such as peanuts.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

In special-diet questions, state the nutritional risk first (e.g. a dairy-free diet risks low calcium and vitamin D) and then the substitution (fortified plant milks, tinned fish with bones); that nutrient-risk-substitute chain earns the analysis marks.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

State two nutrients required in higher amounts during pregnancy and two foods or drinks that should be avoided or limited.

Model answerAny two nutrients: folate, iron, calcium, vitamin D, protein (1) (1). Avoid or limit: alcohol (1); high-mercury fish such as shark and swordfish, unpasteurised soft cheeses, pâté, or excess caffeine (1).
CHALLENGE

Explain the difference between coeliac disease, wheat allergy and lactose intolerance, and how each condition changes the diet.

Model answerCoeliac disease is an autoimmune reaction to gluten that damages the small intestine, so it requires a strict lifelong gluten-free diet excluding wheat, barley and rye (1). Wheat allergy is an immune reaction to wheat proteins that can cause hives or, in severe cases, anaphylaxis, so wheat must be avoided and cross-contact prevented (1). Lactose intolerance is not an allergy but a deficiency of the lactase enzyme, so lactose is poorly digested and causes discomfort; it is managed by limiting lactose or using lactose-free products (1). All three require label checking, but only coeliac disease requires permanent, complete exclusion with substitution of equivalent nutrients such as fibre and B vitamins (1). Small amounts are often tolerated in intolerance, whereas allergy and coeliac disease leave no safe amount (1).
