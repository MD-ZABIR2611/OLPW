Chambers of Commerce & Trade Associations

Voluntary business organisations that promote trade: a chamber of commerce serves all the businesses of a locality, while a trade association serves one industry, and both offer members information, representation and support.

Key Concepts

01

A chamber of commerce is a voluntary, non-profit association of the businesses and business people of a town, city or region, formed to promote and protect the interests of commerce in that area.

02

Local and national chambers are linked internationally through the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC), founded in 1919 with headquarters in Paris, which represents world business and publishes standard trade rules such as Incoterms.

03

Services to members include supplying market and credit information about customers, advising on export markets and documentation, organising trade fairs and trade missions, and arranging arbitration of commercial disputes.

04

Chambers speak on behalf of members to local and national government, lobby over proposed laws and taxes, promote training and ethical trading, and issue certificates of origin needed for customs clearance of exports.

05

A trade association is an organisation of firms in the same industry, such as a bankers' association or motor traders' association; unlike chambers, which are territorial and cross-industry, trade associations are industry-specific.

06

Trade associations publish trade journals and statistics, exchange market information, set and police standards of quality and service, agree common terms of business and promote the image of their industry to the public.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Chamber of CommerceA voluntary, non-profit association of the businesses of a town, city or region formed to promote trade and protect members' common interests.
Trade associationAn organisation of firms in the same industry that promotes the industry's interests, sets common standards and shares market information.
International Chamber of CommerceThe world business organisation, founded in 1919 and based in Paris, representing enterprises in international trade policy and publishing Incoterms.
Certificate of originA document issued by a chamber of commerce certifying the country in which exported goods were manufactured.
ArbitrationThe settlement of a commercial dispute by an independent third party whose decision the parties agree in advance to accept, avoiding court action.
Trade missionAn organised visit by a group of business people to another country to explore export markets and make trading contacts.
Trade fairAn exhibition at which firms in an industry display goods to buyers, agents and the public to win orders and promote the industry.
IncotermsStandardised international trade terms published by the ICC defining when risk and costs pass from seller to buyer.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

When asked about the work of a chamber of commerce, write four separate services — information, representation to government, certificates of origin, and arbitration or trade fairs — and add a linking line such as "this helps the business by..." to convert description into explanation marks.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Distinguish between a chamber of commerce and a trade association.

Model answerA chamber of commerce is a voluntary association of businesses in a particular town, city or region, open to all trades (1). It aims to promote the general commercial interests of that locality (1). A trade association is an organisation of firms in one particular industry or trade (1). It aims to protect and promote that industry's interests, for example by setting standards and publishing trade information (1).
CHALLENGE

A small manufacturer wants to begin exporting its products. Explain four ways in which its local chamber of commerce could help the firm.

Model answerIt could provide market research and credit information about overseas customers, reducing the risk of bad debts (1). It could issue certificates of origin, which customs authorities require before goods may be admitted (1). It could advise on export documents, payment methods and procedures the firm has not handled before (1). It could arrange contacts through trade fairs and trade missions, or arbitrate if a dispute arises with a foreign buyer (1). Overall the chamber reduces the cost and risk of entering export markets, which is why small firms benefit most (1).
