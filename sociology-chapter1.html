Overview

Sociology is the systematic study of human societies, the groups within them and the patterns of behaviour that shape everyday life. Instead of explaining behaviour purely in terms of individual choice or biology, sociologists look at how families, schools, religions, the media and the economy influence who we are and what we do. This opening chapter introduces the key concepts and the major perspectives, functionalism, Marxism, feminism and interactionism, that run through every other topic in the Cambridge IGCSE 0495 and O-Level 2251 course. Examiners reward candidates who can use these perspectives to explain and evaluate evidence, so a secure grasp of them now will lift your marks in every later paper.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Define sociology and explain how it differs from common-sense explanations of behaviour.
  • Explain the idea of the sociological imagination as described by C. Wright Mills.
  • Describe the key concepts of culture, norms, values, roles, status and socialisation.
  • Outline and compare the functionalist, Marxist, feminist and interactionist perspectives.
  • Distinguish between consensus and conflict theories, and between structural and action approaches.
  • Use evidence such as feral children to assess the nature versus nurture debate.
02

What Is Sociology?

Sociology is the scientific study of society, social groups and social behaviour. The word was coined by the French thinker Auguste Comte in the 1830s, who believed that society could be studied using the same logical, evidence-based methods as the natural sciences. Sociologists are interested in patterns rather than isolated events: for example, not why one particular pupil leaves school early, but why pupils from certain social classes or ethnic groups are more likely to do so. By identifying such patterns, sociology helps to explain inequality, social change and the forces that hold societies together.

Sociology differs from common sense because its claims are based on systematic research rather than personal experience, tradition or stereotype. Common sense often tells us that behaviour is natural, for instance that women are naturally better at childcare. Sociologists test such claims against evidence and frequently find that behaviour varies between cultures and over time, which suggests it is learned rather than biologically fixed. Sociology is also different from psychology, which focuses on the individual mind, and from history, which describes past events, although sociologists borrow ideas and evidence from both.

Key fact:Key definition: sociology is the systematic, evidence-based study of how society shapes human behaviour and how people in turn shape society.
03

The Sociological Imagination

The American sociologist C. Wright Mills (1959) argued that thinking sociologically requires a sociological imagination: the ability to link private troubles to public issues. A single person losing their job is a private trouble, but when millions become unemployed during a recession the cause lies in the structure of the economy rather than in individual laziness. Mills believed that people often feel trapped by forces they do not understand, and that sociology gives them the tools to see how their personal lives are connected to wider history and social structure.

Using the sociological imagination means stepping back from familiar routines and asking why they exist. Why do most people in Britain marry for love while arranged marriages are common in parts of South Asia? Why do girls now outperform boys at GCSE and IGCSE level when the reverse was true in the 1970s? Questions like these show that behaviour we take for granted is shaped by culture, economics and power. In the exam, showing this kind of thinking, moving from the individual to the structural level, is a clear sign of sociological understanding.

04

Key Concepts: Culture, Norms, Values, Roles and Status

Culture refers to the whole way of life of a society, including its language, beliefs, customs, knowledge, dress, food and technology. Culture is learned and shared, and it is passed from one generation to the next through socialisation. Within culture, values are general beliefs about what is important and worth striving for, such as honesty, education or respect for elders. Norms are the specific, unwritten rules of behaviour that put values into practice, for example queuing, shaking hands or removing shoes before entering a home.

Every person occupies a number of statuses, or social positions, such as daughter, student or doctor. Ascribed status is fixed at birth, for example gender, ethnicity or royal title, while achieved status is earned through effort or merit, such as becoming a lawyer. Each status carries a role, a set of expected behaviours; a teacher is expected to prepare lessons and treat pupils fairly. When a person holds two roles with clashing expectations, such as a working mother asked to stay late at the office on the night of her child's school play, sociologists call this role conflict.

  • Values: broad ideals, for example the value placed on human life.
  • Norms: specific rules that follow from values, for example not driving after drinking alcohol.
  • Mores: strongly held norms whose breach causes outrage, for example rules against incest.
  • Folkways: everyday customs whose breach causes mild disapproval, for example table manners.
05

Nature Versus Nurture

One of the oldest debates in social science is whether human behaviour is mainly caused by nature, meaning genetics and biology, or by nurture, meaning upbringing, socialisation and the social environment. Sociologists generally emphasise nurture. They point out that behaviour such as table manners, language, religious belief and ideas about masculinity and femininity vary enormously between societies, which would not happen if they were simply biologically programmed. Anthropologist Margaret Mead's studies of three tribes in New Guinea in the 1930s, for instance, found very different gender roles in each, suggesting that gender behaviour is culturally shaped.

The strongest evidence for nurture comes from feral children, who have grown up with little or no human contact. Genie, discovered in California in 1970 at the age of thirteen after years of isolation and abuse, never fully learned language despite intensive support. Oxana Malaya, found in Ukraine in 1991 after living with dogs, barked, ran on all fours and ate like a dog. These cases suggest that the human skills we assume are natural must be learned through socialisation. However, sociologists acknowledge that such cases are rare and often involve abuse or possible disability, so they cannot be generalised with confidence.

Key fact:Evaluation point: feral children cases are small in number and may involve trauma or learning difficulties, so they support but do not prove the nurture argument.
06

Functionalism: A Consensus Structural Theory

Functionalism sees society as a system of interrelated parts, such as the family, education, religion and the economy, each of which performs a function that helps society survive. The French sociologist Emile Durkheim, writing in the late nineteenth century, compared society to a living organism in which every organ has a job to do; this is often called the organic analogy. Durkheim argued that society depends on value consensus, a shared set of norms and values that creates social solidarity and holds people together. Without this shared culture, he warned, society would fall into anomie, a state of normlessness.

The American functionalist Talcott Parsons developed these ideas in the mid twentieth century. He argued that institutions such as the family and school socialise individuals into the core values of society, and that social stratification is fair because it rewards people according to talent and effort. Functionalism is praised for showing how institutions are linked and for explaining social stability. Critics argue that it exaggerates harmony, ignores conflict and inequality, and struggles to explain social change. It has also been accused of justifying the status quo by assuming that whatever exists must be functional.

07

Marxism and Feminism: Conflict Theories

Karl Marx (1818-1883) argued that society is based on conflict between two social classes. The bourgeoisie, or ruling class, own the means of production such as factories, land and capital, while the proletariat, or working class, must sell their labour in order to survive. The bourgeoisie exploit workers by paying them less than the value of what they produce, keeping the difference as profit. Institutions such as education, religion and the media form a superstructure that spreads ruling-class ideology, persuading workers to accept inequality and preventing them from recognising their true interests, a condition Marx called false consciousness.

Feminism is also a conflict theory, but it focuses on inequality between men and women rather than class. Feminists argue that society is patriarchal, meaning male-dominated, and that women are disadvantaged in the family, the workplace, politics and the media. There are several strands. Liberal feminists such as Ann Oakley seek gradual reform through laws and changing attitudes. Radical feminists such as Kate Millett see patriarchy as the root of all oppression. Marxist feminists link women's oppression to capitalism, arguing that unpaid domestic labour benefits employers by reproducing the workforce at no cost.

  • Strength of Marxism: highlights economic inequality and the power of the ruling class.
  • Weakness of Marxism: predicted revolution has not occurred in most capitalist societies, and it tends to ignore gender and ethnicity.
  • Strength of feminism: has exposed gender inequality and influenced laws on equal pay and domestic violence.
  • Weakness of feminism: may overlook progress women have made and differences between women of different classes and ethnicities.
08

Interactionism and the Structure-Action Debate

Functionalism, Marxism and most feminism are structural theories: they see individual behaviour as largely shaped by the wider structure of society. Interactionism takes the opposite view. It is an action or micro approach that focuses on how individuals interpret and give meaning to social situations in small-scale, face-to-face interaction. The ideas of George Herbert Mead and Max Weber inspired this approach. Interactionists such as Howard Becker argue that concepts like deviance or educational failure are not fixed facts but are created when people with power attach labels to others.

The debate between structure and action is central to sociology. Structuralists are criticised for presenting people as puppets who passively absorb norms, while interactionists are criticised for ignoring the wider inequalities of class, gender and ethnicity that limit people's choices. Many modern sociologists, such as Anthony Giddens with his theory of structuration, argue that both matter: social structures shape our actions, but our actions also reproduce and change those structures over time. Showing awareness of this debate in extended answers is an effective way to reach the evaluation marks.

Key fact:Consensus theory = functionalism. Conflict theories = Marxism and feminism. Action theory = interactionism.

Worked Examples

Model answer: explaining the difference between norms and values (4 marks)

Using examples, explain the difference between norms and values.

  1. Define values accurately: general beliefs about what is important or desirable in society, for example the belief that education matters.
  2. Define norms accurately: specific rules of behaviour that put values into practice, for example attending school every day and arriving on time.
  3. Link the two clearly to show the relationship: norms follow from values, so the value of respect for elders produces the norm of giving up a seat for an older person.
  4. Add a point on variation to show sociological depth: both norms and values vary between cultures and over time, which shows they are learned through socialisation.
Values are broad ideals, such as respect for human life, while norms are the specific expected behaviours that follow from them, such as not using violence. Norms are therefore more concrete and situation-specific, and both vary between cultures, showing they are socially learned. A full-mark answer defines both terms, gives a relevant example of each and explains how they are connected.

Worked evaluation: how far do feral children prove that behaviour is learned?

Assess the view that cases of feral children prove that human behaviour is the result of nurture rather than nature. (8 marks)

  1. Introduce the debate: nature refers to biological inheritance, nurture to socialisation and the social environment.
  2. Present supporting evidence: Genie never developed full language after isolation until age thirteen; Oxana Malaya adopted the behaviour of dogs. Both suggest core human behaviours must be learned.
  3. Add wider support: Margaret Mead's New Guinea studies show gender roles vary across cultures, supporting nurture.
  4. Evaluate the evidence: feral cases are very rare, often involve abuse and trauma, and some children may have had learning disabilities from birth, so we cannot be sure the lack of socialisation alone caused their behaviour.
  5. Consider the opposing view: sociobiologists argue some behaviour, such as certain gender differences, has a biological basis linked to hormones.
  6. Reach a supported judgement.
Feral children provide strong evidence that language, social skills and cultural behaviour depend on socialisation, but they do not prove that nature plays no role, because the cases are few, unrepresentative and complicated by abuse. The most balanced conclusion is that biology gives humans the capacity for behaviour, while nurture shapes almost all of its specific content.

Case study: applying perspectives to the family

Show how a functionalist, a Marxist and a feminist would each interpret the role of the family.

  1. Functionalist view (Parsons): the family performs essential functions of primary socialisation and the stabilisation of adult personalities, benefiting both individuals and society.
  2. Marxist view: the family serves capitalism by socialising children to accept hierarchy, passing on private property and acting as a unit of consumption.
  3. Feminist view (Oakley): the family is a key site of patriarchy where women perform most unpaid domestic labour and emotional work.
The same institution can be interpreted in very different ways. Functionalists stress harmony and benefit to all, while Marxists and feminists stress conflict and the unequal benefit to capitalists or men. Applying at least two perspectives in this way is a reliable route to higher-level marks.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Confusing norms and values. Remember values are the broad ideal and norms are the specific rule of behaviour that follows from it.
  • Describing a theory without naming its key thinker. Always attach names such as Durkheim, Parsons, Marx, Oakley or Becker to gain knowledge marks.
  • Treating feminism as one single view. Distinguish liberal, radical and Marxist feminism wherever possible.
  • Claiming feral children prove behaviour is entirely learned. Always note the small number of cases and complicating factors such as abuse.
  • Writing common-sense opinions instead of sociological arguments. Support every claim with a concept, a study or statistical evidence.
  • Forgetting that interactionism is a micro approach. Do not describe it as a structural theory like functionalism or Marxism.

Exam tips

  • Learn a two-line summary of each perspective plus one strength and one weakness; these can be reused in almost every extended answer.
  • In 'explain' questions, make a point, develop it with an example and link it back to the question to gain full development marks.
  • Use the phrase 'consensus versus conflict' or 'structure versus action' in evaluations to show theoretical awareness.
  • When a question says 'using examples', give one clear example per point; generic statements rarely earn the application mark.
  • Keep definitions short and precise; two-mark definition questions reward accuracy, not length.
  • In essay questions, always end with a reasoned judgement that directly answers 'how far' or 'to what extent'.

Practice Questions

[2 marks]

1. What is meant by the term 'culture'?

Model answerThe learned and shared way of life of a society (1), including language, norms, values, beliefs and customs passed between generations (1).
[2 marks]

2. Define ascribed status and give one example.

Model answerA social position fixed at birth that cannot usually be changed (1); for example gender, ethnicity or inheriting a royal title (1).
[2 marks]

3. Identify two agencies of socialisation.

Model answerAny two from: family, education, peer group, religion, mass media, workplace (1 each).
[2 marks]

4. What did C. Wright Mills mean by the sociological imagination?

Model answerThe ability to connect individual or private troubles (1) to wider public issues and social structures, for example seeing mass unemployment as caused by the economy rather than individual failings (1).
[4 marks]

5. Describe two differences between sociology and common sense.

Model answerSociology is based on systematic research and evidence, while common sense relies on personal experience or tradition (2). Sociology questions ideas that seem natural and shows they vary between cultures, whereas common sense often treats behaviour as fixed or biological (2).
[4 marks]

6. Explain what is meant by role conflict, using an example.

Model answerRole conflict occurs when the expectations of two roles held by the same person clash (1-2). For example, a working parent may be expected by an employer to attend a late meeting and by their child to attend a school event on the same evening (1-2).
[4 marks]

7. Explain the difference between consensus and conflict theories.

Model answerConsensus theories such as functionalism see society as based on shared values and harmony, with institutions benefiting everyone (2). Conflict theories such as Marxism and feminism see society as divided between groups with unequal power, class or gender, where institutions benefit the powerful (2).
[6 marks]

8. Describe the Marxist view of society.

Model answerSociety is divided into the bourgeoisie, who own the means of production, and the proletariat, who sell their labour (2). The bourgeoisie exploit workers to make profit (1). Institutions like education and the media spread ruling-class ideology (1), creating false consciousness so workers accept inequality (1). Marx predicted this would eventually lead to revolution (1).
[6 marks]

9. Explain two criticisms of functionalism.

Model answerIt exaggerates harmony and value consensus and ignores conflict and inequality, such as class and gender divisions highlighted by Marxists and feminists (3). It struggles to explain social change and tends to justify the status quo by assuming existing institutions must be beneficial (3).
[6 marks]

10. Explain how interactionism differs from structural theories.

Model answerInteractionism is a micro, action approach focusing on small-scale interaction and the meanings people give to situations (2), whereas structural theories such as functionalism and Marxism look at how large-scale social structures shape behaviour (2). Interactionists see individuals as active creators of social reality, for example through labelling (Becker) (1), while structuralists see behaviour as largely determined by society (1).
[12 marks]

11. To what extent is human behaviour the result of socialisation rather than biology?

Model answerFor socialisation: behaviour varies widely between cultures and over time (Mead's New Guinea tribes); feral children such as Genie and Oxana Malaya lack language and social skills without human contact; gender roles have changed rapidly, which biology cannot explain. Against: sociobiologists argue some differences such as aggression are linked to hormones; feral cases are few and complicated by abuse or possible disability. Judgement: biology provides the capacity for behaviour, but its content is overwhelmingly shaped by socialisation. Level 3 answers present both sides with named evidence and reach a supported conclusion.

Quick Recap

  1. Sociology is the systematic study of society and differs from common sense by relying on evidence.
  2. C. Wright Mills's sociological imagination links private troubles to public issues.
  3. Culture, norms, values, roles and status are the building blocks of social life.
  4. Evidence from cross-cultural studies and feral children strongly supports the importance of nurture.
  5. Functionalism (Durkheim, Parsons) is a consensus theory stressing shared values and social order.
  6. Marxism and feminism are conflict theories focusing on class exploitation and patriarchy.
  7. Interactionism is a micro approach focusing on meanings, interaction and labelling.
  8. Good exam answers apply and evaluate these perspectives using named sociologists and studies.
