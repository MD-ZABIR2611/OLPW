Crafting Descriptive and Narrative Prose

Beyond avoiding errors — this chapter is about craft: how professional writers open, build atmosphere, control pace, vary sentences and close with purpose, and how you can reproduce it under exam conditions.

Key Concepts

01

Effective openings in medias res (into the middle of action), with a striking image, a voice, or a question — they establish character and tension immediately rather than clearing the throat with background.

02

Show, don't tell: replace abstract emotion labels ("I was scared") with physical and sensory detail (a pounding pulse, slick palms) that lets the reader infer the feeling.

03

Atmosphere is built through sensory writing (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste), precise verbs, and pathetic fallacy, where weather and setting mirror mood.

04

Pacing is controlled by sentence length: short, fragmented sentences accelerate action and tension; long, flowing sentences slow the moment for reflection or description.

05

Endings should resolve, echo or twist: a circular return to the opening image, a revealed secret, or a resonant final line all give a sense of completion.

06

Paragraphing is a meaning-making tool: shift paragraph when time, place, topic or speaker changes, and use a one-line paragraph for dramatic emphasis.

02

Key Terms

TermExam definition
In medias resOpening a narrative in the middle of the action rather than with background exposition, to grip the reader immediately.
Pathetic fallacyA technique in which weather or landscape reflects a character's emotions, such as storms during turmoil.
Sensory detailDescription appealing to sight, sound, smell, touch and taste to make a scene vivid and immediate.
Show, don't tellA writing principle in which feelings and events are conveyed through action and detail rather than direct statement.
PacingThe speed at which a story unfolds, controlled by sentence length, detail and the sequencing of events.
ClichéA phrase so overused it has lost its impact, such as "cold as ice", which weakens descriptive writing.
First-person narrationA story told by "I", giving intimacy and immediate access to one character's thoughts but a limited viewpoint.
Circular endingA conclusion that returns to an image or idea from the opening, giving a narrative a sense of unity and completion.
03

Exam Tip

Exam tip

In the exam, plan only three anchors before writing: the opening image, the moment of highest tension, and the final line. A composition that nails these three beats with controlled sentence variation reads as crafted even under time pressure.

04

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Give two techniques a writer can use to create a tense atmosphere, with a brief example of each.

Model answerAny two of: short, fragmented sentences to quicken pace, e.g. "He ran. The door slammed." (1); sensory detail, e.g. "the smell of rain on dust" to make the scene immediate (1); pathetic fallacy, e.g. "thunder growled overhead" mirroring fear (1); precise, strong verbs, e.g. "she lunged" instead of "she moved quickly" (1).
CHALLENGE

A student writes: "I was very frightened and the storm was loud. I walked home quickly." Rewrite this to demonstrate how a writer shows rather than tells, and explain the improvements you made.

Model answerImproved version: "Thunder cracked directly overhead; my hood snapped in the wind. I kept my head down, counting the lamp posts, each one a checkpoint between me and the front door." (2) Explanation: fear is shown through physical reactions (flinching, counting checkpoints) and the storm through sound imagery instead of the label "loud" (1); strong verbs ("cracked", "snapped") and varied sentence lengths quicken pace and embody panic rather than naming it (1).
