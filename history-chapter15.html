Chapter 15 · History

The Russian Revolution & Stalin's USSR 1917-1941

Defeat in war, food shortages and an autocratic monarchy produced two revolutions in 1917, and from the wreckage Lenin and then Stalin built a one-party state that transformed Russia at terrible human cost.

Key Concepts

01

Long-term causes of revolution included autocratic tsarist rule, land-hungry peasants and the hardship of rapid industrialisation (long hours, low pay); short-term triggers were defeat in the Russo-Japanese War (1905) and the massive casualties and food shortages of the First World War; Bloody Sunday in January 1905, when troops killed peaceful petitioners in St Petersburg, destroyed faith in Tsar Nicholas II, and the 1905 Revolution forced him to grant the October Manifesto promising a duma (parliament).

02

The February Revolution of 1917 (March by the modern calendar): bread riots and strikes in Petrograd, begun by women textile workers, spread to soldiers who mutinied; the Tsar abdicated in March 1917, and the Provisional Government under Kerensky shared power with the workers' councils (soviets), but by continuing the war and delaying land reform it lost support.

03

The October Revolution of 1917 (November by the modern calendar): Lenin's Bolsheviks won majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets with the slogans Peace, Land and Bread and All Power to the Soviets; organised by Trotsky, they seized railway stations, bridges and the Winter Palace and overthrew the Provisional Government almost bloodlessly; the decrees on land and peace followed, and Russia left the war through the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918), surrendering vast western territories.

04

Civil war 1918-1921: the Reds (Bolsheviks) defeated the Whites and their foreign backers through War Communism (requisitioning grain, nationalising industry), Trotsky's disciplined Red Army, control of the central heartland and railways, and White disunity; the Tsar and his family were shot at Ekaterinburg in July 1918; victory saved Bolshevik power but ruined the economy, and the Kronstadt mutiny of 1921 pushed Lenin into the New Economic Policy (NEP), which allowed peasants to sell surplus grain and small private trade and revived the economy.

05

Stalin's rise: after Lenin's death in January 1924, Stalin used the post of General Secretary to pack the party with supporters, isolated Trotsky (exiled 1929, murdered in Mexico 1940) and defeated the Left (Zinoviev, Kamenev) and then the Right (Bukharin) oppositions; by 1929 he was supreme leader of party and state.

06

Stalin's rule 1929-1941: collectivisation from 1929 merged peasant farms into state-controlled collective farms, provoking resistance and dekulakisation and a famine, worst in Ukraine in 1932-33, that killed millions; the First Five-Year Plan from 1928 built heavy industry — steel, coal, electricity — around projects such as Magnitogorsk and the Dnieper dam, raising output at the cost of consumer goods and living standards; the Great Terror of 1936-38 used the murder of Kirov (1934) as a pretext for show trials and purges that executed roughly 700 000 people and filled the gulag camps, while the Stalin Constitution of 1936 promised rights in name only; the Nazi-Soviet Pact of August 1939 bought time before Germany invaded in June 1941.

Key Terms

TermExam definition
Bloody SundayThe killing of peaceful petitioners by troops in St Petersburg in January 1905, which destroyed faith in Tsar Nicholas II and sparked revolution.
Provisional GovernmentThe temporary government formed after the March 1917 revolution, which continued the war and was overthrown by the Bolsheviks in November 1917.
SovietA council of workers', soldiers' or peasants' deputies, which became the basis of Bolshevik government in Russia.
BolshevikA member of Lenin's Communist faction, meaning majority, who seized power in Russia in November 1917.
War CommunismThe Bolshevik policy of 1918-21 of seizing grain from peasants, nationalising industry and banning private trade to supply the civil-war Red Army.
New Economic PolicyLenin's economic retreat of 1921 allowing peasants to sell surplus grain and small private businesses, which revived the Soviet economy.
CollectivisationStalin's policy from 1929 of forcing peasants to give up their farms and work on state-controlled collective farms.
Five-Year PlanA centrally planned set of production targets for Soviet industry, the first launched in 1928 to build heavy industry.

Exam Tip

Exam tip

For why-revolution questions, split causes into long-term (autocracy, land hunger) and short-term triggers (war losses, bread prices), and attach a linking line such as "this mattered because..." to each cause — two developed causes usually earn more than four bare facts.

Practice Questions

Structured practice — write your answer, then reveal the model.

FOUNDATION

Describe the main causes of the February Revolution of 1917.

Model answerHeavy casualties and defeats in the First World War destroyed confidence in the Tsar's leadership (1). Food shortages and soaring bread prices caused hunger riots, begun by women workers in Petrograd (1). Strikes spread and soldiers of the Petrograd garrison mutinied and joined the crowds (1). The Tsar was widely seen as weak, especially after the Rasputin scandal, and the Duma demanded his abdication (1).
CHALLENGE

Evaluate the effects of Stalin's collectivisation of agriculture on the Soviet Union.

Model answerThe state gained a more reliable supply of grain to feed the growing industrial cities and to export for machinery, and collective farms could use tractors and combines, so supporters claimed modernisation (1). Peasants resisted by slaughtering livestock and hiding grain, so meat and dairy output collapsed and did not recover for decades (1). Resistance was crushed by dekulakisation, the deportation and killing of the better-off peasants, and by the famine of 1932-33, which killed millions, especially in Ukraine (1). Agriculture stayed inefficient, with output per hectare low, showing the human cost brought little economic gain (1). Overall collectivisation achieved Stalin's political aim of controlling the countryside but at a catastrophic cost in lives and lost production (1).
