Biotechnology & Genetic Modification

CORE
SECTION 01 — CHAPTER OVERVIEW

Using Microbes & Changing Genes

Biotechnology puts living things - mostly bacteria, fungi and their enzymes - to work for humans, from bread and beer to penicillin and washing powder. Genetic modification goes further: it moves a gene from one organism into another, such as putting the human insulin gene into bacteria so they manufacture insulin for medicine.

  • Describe how bacteria are genetically modified to produce insulin.
  • Discuss the advantages and concerns of GM crops.
SECTION 03 — CORE THEORY

Fermentation & Industrial Microbes

Yeast respires sugar: aerobically for quick growth, and ANAEROBICALLY (fermentation) when oxygen runs out, producing ethanol and carbon dioxide. Bread-making uses the CO₂ to raise dough (the ethanol evaporates in the oven); brewing uses the ethanol.

An industrial fermenter is a steel tank built to keep microbes at their optimum: thermostats hold the temperature, paddles mix and add oxygen, pH probes and buffers hold acidity steady, and everything is sterilised first so only the wanted microbe grows.

Enzymes are harvested for industry: pectinase clarifies fruit juice, rennin (chymosin) curdles milk for cheese, lactase makes lactose-free milk, and proteases and lipases in biological washing powder digest food stains at low temperatures.

Penicillin is made by growing the mould Penicillium in huge fermenters; the antibiotic is extracted from the broth and purified. The producing strain was originally from a chance mould on a petri dish - Fleming’s famous discovery.

SECTION 04 — KEY DEFINITIONS

BIOTECHNOLOGY: The use of living organisms or their enzymes to make useful products for humans.

FERMENTATION: The anaerobic respiration of yeast: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide.

FERMENTER: A controlled tank (temperature, pH, oxygen, stirring) in which microbes are grown on a large scale.

GENETIC MODIFICATION: Transferring a gene from one organism into the DNA of another so the receiver makes a new protein.

TRANSGENIC: Describes an organism carrying a gene from a different species.

PLASMID: A small ring of bacterial DNA used as a vector to carry an inserted gene into a bacterium.

SECTION 05 — PRACTICAL LAB

AIM

Investigate anaerobic respiration in yeast and the conditions it needs.

METHOD

1. Mix yeast with sugar solution in a flask and fit a balloon or limewater tube.

2. Keep one flask warm (35 °C) and one on ice; compare balloon inflation or limewater turning milky.

3. Repeat without sugar as a control - no gas is made without substrate.

CONCLUSION

Gas (CO₂) is produced only when yeast, sugar and warmth are all present: fermentation needs respiration substrate and enzyme-friendly temperatures.

SECTION 06 — EXAM TECHNIQUE

DESCRIBE: State the steps of genetic modification in order - gene cut with restriction enzyme, plasmid cut with the same enzyme, gene inserted with ligase, plasmid returned to bacterium, bacteria cultured in fermenters.

DISCUSS: Give both sides for GM crops - higher yields and vitamins (golden rice) versus gene spread to wild plants and farmer seed costs.

SECTION 07 — COMMON MISTAKES

Mistake: Saying yeast fermentation produces CO₂ and water.

Correction: Anaerobic fermentation produces ethanol and CO₂ only - no water, and much less ATP than aerobic respiration.

Mistake: Writing that the insulin GENE is "harmful" to bacteria or that the whole human insulin protein is inserted.

Correction: Only the DNA/gene is inserted; the bacterium then reads it and makes the insulin protein itself.

SECTION 08 — QUICK REVISION
  • Yeast + sugar, no oxygen → ethanol + CO₂.
  • Fermenters control temperature, pH, oxygen and sterility.
  • Enzyme uses: pectinase (juice), rennin (cheese), lactase (lactose-free milk), proteases (washing powder).
  • GM insulin: human gene → plasmid → bacterium → fermenter → harvest insulin.
  • GM crops: higher yield, added nutrients; concerns include gene spread and seed cost.
SECTION 09 — EXAM QUESTIONS

Question 1 (Foundation)

List three conditions an industrial fermenter must control and say why each matters.

Temperature kept at optimum so enzymes work at their fastest without denaturing (1);

Oxygen supplied (stirred in) so aerobic microbes like Penicillium respire and grow quickly (1);

pH buffered at the optimum for the enzymes; sterile conditions so no competing microbes take over (any 3 × 1 mark).

Question 2 (Challenge)

Describe how bacteria are genetically modified to produce human insulin.

The human insulin gene is cut out of human DNA using a restriction enzyme (1);

a bacterial plasmid is cut with the same enzyme so the sticky ends match (1);

the gene is joined into the plasmid with DNA ligase and the plasmid is transferred into bacteria (1);

the transgenic bacteria are grown in fermenters and insulin is harvested and purified (1).
