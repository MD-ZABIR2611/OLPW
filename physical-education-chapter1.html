The Skeletal System

How the bones and joints of the human skeleton are organised, what they do for the performer, and how they allow and limit movement in physical activity.

01

Overview

Every movement in sport, from a sprint start to a tennis serve, begins with the skeleton. The skeleton provides the rigid framework that muscles pull on, protects vital organs from impact and produces the blood cells that carry oxygen to working muscles. In the IGCSE Physical Education exam you are expected to name the major bones, classify them, describe the structure of a synovial joint and link joint types to the movements they allow in real sporting actions. Examiners reward answers that apply anatomical knowledge to a named sport rather than simply listing facts, so throughout this chapter every idea is connected to a practical example.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Name and locate the major bones of the axial and appendicular skeleton.
  • Explain the functions of the skeleton and apply each function to a sporting example.
  • Classify bones as long, short, flat, irregular or sesamoid and link shape to function.
  • Describe the structure of a synovial joint and the role of each component.
  • Identify ball-and-socket, hinge, pivot, condyloid, gliding and saddle joints and the movements each allows.
  • Describe the movement terms flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation and circumduction using sporting actions.
  • Explain the short-term and long-term effects of exercise on bones and joints.
02

Organisation of the Skeleton

The adult human skeleton contains 206 bones and is divided into two parts. The axial skeleton forms the central axis of the body and consists of the cranium, the vertebral column, the sternum and the ribs. Its main role is protection and support: the cranium surrounds the brain, the ribs and sternum form the thoracic cage around the heart and lungs, and the vertebral column encloses the spinal cord while holding the body upright. The axial skeleton also provides attachment points for the large postural muscles of the trunk.

The appendicular skeleton consists of the limbs and the girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton. The shoulder (pectoral) girdle is made of the clavicle and scapula, and the hip (pelvic) girdle is formed by the pelvis. The upper limb contains the humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges; the lower limb contains the femur, patella, tibia, fibula, tarsals, metatarsals and phalanges. The appendicular skeleton is mainly concerned with movement, which is why its joints tend to be more mobile than those of the axial skeleton.

The vertebral column is divided into five regions: seven cervical vertebrae in the neck, twelve thoracic vertebrae that articulate with the ribs, five lumbar vertebrae in the lower back that bear most body weight, five fused sacral vertebrae forming the sacrum, and four fused coccygeal vertebrae forming the coccyx. Between most vertebrae are intervertebral discs of cartilage that absorb shock during running and landing, and which allow limited movement at each level that adds up to considerable flexibility along the whole spine, as seen when a gymnast performs a back walkover.

  • Axial skeleton: cranium, vertebral column, sternum, ribs.
  • Appendicular skeleton: clavicle, scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges, pelvis, femur, patella, tibia, fibula, tarsals, metatarsals.
  • Vertebral regions: cervical 7, thoracic 12, lumbar 5, sacral 5 fused, coccygeal 4 fused.
Key fact:The radius is on the thumb side of the forearm; the tibia (shin bone) is the larger, weight-bearing bone of the lower leg.
03

Functions of the Skeleton

The skeleton performs several functions that directly affect sporting performance. Support means the skeleton gives the body its shape and holds it upright so that a performer can maintain a stable stance, such as a goalkeeper ready to dive. Protection means bones surround delicate organs: the cranium protects the brain when a rugby player is tackled, and the ribs protect the heart and lungs from a cricket ball. Movement occurs because bones act as levers and provide attachment points for muscles via tendons, so when muscles contract they pull on bones and move them at joints.

Blood cell production takes place in the red bone marrow found in the spongy bone of flat bones and the ends of long bones. Red blood cells carry oxygen to working muscles, which is essential for endurance events; white blood cells fight infection and help recovery; platelets clot blood after cuts or grazes. The skeleton also stores minerals, especially calcium and phosphorus, which are needed for bone strength and for muscle contraction. When calcium is needed elsewhere in the body it can be released from bone into the blood.

In the exam, a function alone is rarely enough for full marks. A strong answer names the function and then applies it: for example, the femur provides a large surface for the attachment of the quadriceps, allowing a footballer to extend the knee powerfully when shooting. Practise pairing every function with a precise body part and a sporting action.

  • Support and shape
  • Protection of vital organs
  • Movement via muscle attachment and levers
  • Blood cell production in red bone marrow
  • Mineral storage of calcium and phosphorus
04

Classification of Bones

Bones are classified by shape, and shape reflects function. Long bones are longer than they are wide and act as levers for large, fast movements; examples include the femur, humerus, tibia and phalanges. A long bone has a shaft (diaphysis) of dense compact bone surrounding a marrow cavity and two ends (epiphyses) of spongy bone covered in articular cartilage. In young athletes the growth plate between shaft and end allows the bone to lengthen.

Short bones are roughly as wide as they are long, such as the carpals of the wrist and tarsals of the ankle. They are strong, light and absorb shock, and allow small, fine movements, which is useful when a basketball player adjusts wrist position during a shot. Flat bones such as the cranium, sternum, ribs, scapula and pelvis have broad surfaces that protect organs and provide large areas for muscle attachment; the scapula, for instance, anchors many muscles that move the shoulder in swimming.

Irregular bones have complex shapes that suit a specific role, the vertebrae being the main example: they protect the spinal cord and provide attachment points for trunk muscles. Sesamoid bones develop within a tendon; the patella sits in the quadriceps tendon, protecting the knee joint and increasing the leverage of the quadriceps so that a jumper can straighten the knee more effectively.

Key fact:Long = levers for movement; short = strength and fine movement; flat = protection and attachment; irregular = specialised protection; sesamoid = within a tendon.
05

Joints and the Synovial Joint

A joint is a place where two or more bones meet. Joints are classified by how much movement they allow. Fixed or fibrous joints allow no movement, such as the sutures of the cranium. Slightly movable or cartilaginous joints allow a small amount of movement, such as the joints between vertebrae separated by intervertebral discs. Freely movable or synovial joints allow a wide range of movement and are the most important joints in sport, including the shoulder, hip, knee, elbow and ankle.

Every synovial joint shares a common structure. The ends of the bones are covered in articular (hyaline) cartilage, a smooth tissue that reduces friction and absorbs shock. The joint is surrounded by a tough joint capsule, whose inner lining, the synovial membrane, secretes synovial fluid into the joint cavity. Synovial fluid lubricates the joint, nourishes the cartilage and reduces friction so that bones glide smoothly. Ligaments are bands of strong, slightly elastic connective tissue that join bone to bone and stabilise the joint, preventing unwanted or excessive movement.

Some synovial joints have extra features. The knee contains menisci, crescent-shaped pads of cartilage that deepen the joint and absorb shock, and bursae, small fluid-filled sacs that reduce friction between tendons and bone. Tendons, which join muscle to bone, cross joints and transmit muscle force. Remember the difference clearly: ligaments join bone to bone, tendons join muscle to bone.

  • Articular cartilage: reduces friction, absorbs shock
  • Joint capsule: encloses and strengthens the joint
  • Synovial membrane: secretes synovial fluid
  • Synovial fluid: lubricates and nourishes
  • Ligaments: bone to bone, give stability
  • Bursae and menisci: reduce friction and absorb shock
06

Types of Synovial Joint

Synovial joints are subdivided according to the shape of the articulating surfaces, which determines the movements possible. A ball-and-socket joint, found at the shoulder and hip, has a rounded head fitting into a cup-shaped socket, allowing movement in all planes: flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation and circumduction. The shoulder has a shallow socket, giving great mobility for a tennis serve but less stability, which is why dislocations are common there. The hip has a deep socket, giving more stability for weight bearing.

A hinge joint, found at the knee and elbow, allows flexion and extension only, like a door hinge. A pivot joint allows rotation: the atlas and axis vertebrae in the neck let a swimmer turn the head to breathe, and the joint between the radius and ulna allows the forearm to rotate. A condyloid joint, such as the wrist, allows flexion, extension, abduction, adduction and circumduction but not rotation. A gliding joint, found between carpals and tarsals, allows small sliding movements. A saddle joint, found at the base of the thumb, allows movement in two planes and gives the thumb its gripping ability, essential when holding a racket or bat.

When describing a sporting action, identify the joint, the joint type, the articulating bones and the movement. For example, during the upward phase of a squat the knee is a hinge joint between the femur and tibia, and it extends.

Key fact:Hip and shoulder: ball and socket. Knee and elbow: hinge. Neck (atlas/axis): pivot. Wrist: condyloid. Thumb base: saddle.
07

Types of Movement at Joints

Flexion is a decrease in the angle between two bones, such as bending the elbow in the upward phase of a bicep curl. Extension is an increase in the angle between two bones, such as straightening the knee when kicking a ball. Abduction is movement away from the midline of the body, as when a star jump takes the arms and legs outwards; adduction is movement towards the midline, as when the arms and legs return to the sides.

Rotation is the turning of a bone around its own long axis, such as the hip rotating during a golf swing or the shoulder rotating as a bowler releases the ball. Circumduction is a circular movement in which the end of a limb draws a cone shape, combining flexion, extension, abduction and adduction; it occurs only at ball-and-socket and condyloid joints, for example the arm circling in butterfly stroke. At the ankle, plantar flexion means pointing the toes, as a gymnast does or a sprinter does at toe-off, while dorsiflexion means pulling the toes up towards the shin, as when a footballer prepares the foot to strike.

Many sporting actions combine several movements across different joints at once. In a basketball jump shot the hips, knees and ankles extend (with plantar flexion at the ankle) to drive the body upward while the shoulder flexes and the elbow extends to release the ball. Breaking complex actions into single-joint movements is a key exam skill.

08

Effects of Exercise on the Skeletal System

In the short term, exercise increases the production and warmth of synovial fluid, making it less viscous so that joints move more freely; this is one of the reasons for a warm-up. Joint range of movement increases slightly as tissues warm. Regular weight-bearing exercise over months and years produces long-term adaptations. Bone density increases because stress on bones stimulates the deposition of calcium, making bones stronger and less likely to fracture and reducing the risk of osteoporosis in later life.

Long-term training also strengthens ligaments and tendons, improving joint stability, and thickens articular cartilage, improving shock absorption. Flexibility training increases the range of movement at joints, which benefits gymnasts, hurdlers and swimmers. However, excessive high-impact training, particularly in young athletes whose growth plates are still open, can cause overuse injuries such as stress fractures or damage to growth plates, so training must be progressive and allow rest.

Weight-bearing activities such as running, netball, tennis and resistance training are most effective for improving bone density. Swimming and cycling, while excellent for cardiovascular fitness, are less effective for bone density because the body weight is supported.

Key fact:Long-term adaptations: increased bone density, stronger ligaments and tendons, thicker articular cartilage, greater range of movement.

Worked Examples

Analysing a football kick

A footballer strikes the ball with the instep. Analyse the movement at the hip, knee and ankle of the kicking leg during the forward swing, naming the joint type, articulating bones and movement.

  1. Hip: ball-and-socket joint between the pelvis and femur. The leg swings forward, so the hip flexes.
  2. Knee: hinge joint between the femur and tibia. In the backswing the knee flexes; in the forward swing it extends powerfully to accelerate the foot.
  3. Ankle: hinge joint between the tibia, fibula and talus. To strike with the instep the toes point, so the ankle is in plantar flexion.
  4. Link to function: the long bones of the leg (femur, tibia) act as levers, allowing a high foot speed at contact.
Hip: ball and socket, pelvis and femur, flexion. Knee: hinge, femur and tibia, extension. Ankle: hinge, tibia/fibula and talus, plantar flexion. The long bones act as levers to generate foot speed.

Model answer: structure of a synovial joint

Describe how three features of a synovial joint help a hurdler perform repeated jumps safely. (6 marks)

  1. Choose features with a clear performance benefit: articular cartilage, synovial fluid and ligaments.
  2. Articular cartilage covers the ends of the femur and tibia at the knee; it absorbs shock on each landing and reduces friction so bones do not wear.
  3. Synovial fluid is secreted by the synovial membrane; it lubricates the knee so that it moves freely and efficiently through the rapid flexion and extension of hurdling.
  4. Ligaments such as the cruciate ligaments join the femur to the tibia; they stabilise the knee during take-off and landing and prevent dislocation or twisting.
One mark for naming each feature and one mark for linking its function to hurdling: cartilage absorbs landing forces and reduces friction; synovial fluid lubricates to allow smooth, rapid movement; ligaments stabilise the knee to prevent injury during take-off and landing.

Planning bone health through activity choice

A 15-year-old swimmer trains six times a week but does no other exercise. Her coach is concerned about her long-term bone health. Recommend changes and justify them.

  1. Identify the issue: swimming is non-weight-bearing, so bones receive little loading stress.
  2. Recommend two weight-bearing sessions per week, for example circuit training with jumps and a light resistance session using bodyweight squats and lunges.
  3. Justify: loading stimulates calcium deposition, increasing bone density and reducing risk of stress fractures and osteoporosis.
  4. Apply safety: as she is still growing, use progressive overload, correct technique and moderate loads to protect growth plates.
Add two weekly weight-bearing sessions (plyometric circuits and light resistance work) to increase bone density, progressing gradually and emphasising technique to protect developing growth plates.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Confusing ligaments and tendons. Ligaments join bone to bone; tendons join muscle to bone. Learn the phrase 'ligaments link bones'.
  • Stating a function of the skeleton without applying it. Always add a named bone and a sporting example, for instance the ribs protect the lungs from a cricket ball.
  • Describing the knee as able to rotate like the hip. The knee is a hinge joint and in exam answers allows only flexion and extension.
  • Mixing up abduction and adduction. Abduction takes the limb away from the midline; adduction adds it back towards the body.
  • Saying circumduction happens at a hinge joint. It is only possible at ball-and-socket and condyloid joints.
  • Claiming swimming is the best exercise for bone density. Weight-bearing activity is needed to stimulate bone growth.

Exam tips

  • For movement analysis questions, use a four-part structure: joint, joint type, articulating bones, movement.
  • Learn bones in pairs by limb: humerus with radius and ulna; femur with tibia and fibula. This speeds recall in labelling questions.
  • When asked about the structure of a synovial joint, give the feature and its function in the same sentence to secure both marks.
  • Read whether the question asks about the upward or downward phase of an action; flexion and extension reverse between phases.
  • Use precise terms such as plantar flexion and dorsiflexion for the ankle instead of vague words like 'pointing' or 'bending'.
  • In long-answer questions on exercise effects, separate short-term effects (synovial fluid) from long-term adaptations (bone density).

Quick Recap

  1. The skeleton has 206 bones, divided into the axial skeleton (protection) and the appendicular skeleton (movement).
  2. Its functions are support, protection, movement, blood cell production and mineral storage, each of which should be linked to a sporting example.
  3. Bones are classified as long, short, flat, irregular or sesamoid, and shape reflects function.
  4. Synovial joints contain articular cartilage, a joint capsule, a synovial membrane, synovial fluid and stabilising ligaments.
  5. Ball-and-socket joints allow the widest range of movement; hinge joints allow only flexion and extension.
  6. Movement terms include flexion, extension, abduction, adduction, rotation, circumduction, plantar flexion and dorsiflexion.
  7. Regular weight-bearing exercise increases bone density and strengthens ligaments, tendons and cartilage.
