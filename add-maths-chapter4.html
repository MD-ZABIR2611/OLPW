Factors of Polynomials

Using the factor and remainder theorems, polynomial division and trial roots to factorise cubic polynomials and solve cubic equations.

01

Overview

Quadratics can always be solved with the formula, but cubic equations need a different strategy: find one factor, divide it out, and reduce the problem to a quadratic. The factor theorem and the remainder theorem make this possible by letting you test possible factors with a single substitution. This chapter shows you how to divide polynomials, find unknown coefficients from given factors or remainders, and solve cubic equations completely. These are reliable, high-scoring questions in the exam, typically worth five to eight marks, and the techniques are needed again in calculus when you find stationary points of cubic curves.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Understand polynomial vocabulary, including degree, coefficient, quotient and remainder.
  • Divide a polynomial by a linear expression using long division, synthetic division or comparing coefficients.
  • Use the remainder theorem to find the remainder when a polynomial is divided by (x − a) or (ax − b).
  • Use the factor theorem to test whether a linear expression is a factor of a polynomial.
  • Find unknown coefficients in a polynomial using given factors or remainders.
  • Factorise cubic polynomials completely and solve cubic equations, giving exact or rounded answers as required.
02

Polynomial Vocabulary

A polynomial is an expression made of terms of the form axⁿ where n is a non-negative whole number. The degree of a polynomial is the highest power of x that appears: 5x − 2 has degree 1 (linear), x² + 3x − 4 has degree 2 (quadratic) and 2x³ − x + 7 has degree 3 (cubic). Expressions such as 1/x or √x are not polynomials because their powers are negative or fractional.

When writing a polynomial, arrange its terms in descending powers of x and include a zero coefficient for any missing power if you plan to divide. For example, 4x³ − 2x + 5 should be treated as 4x³ + 0x² − 2x + 5 in a division, so that each power has its own column. Forgetting the missing term is one of the most common causes of wrong quotients.

Any division of a polynomial f(x) by a divisor d(x) can be written as the identity f(x) ≡ d(x) × q(x) + r(x), where q(x) is the quotient and r(x) is the remainder. The degree of the remainder is always less than the degree of the divisor, so dividing by a linear expression always leaves a constant remainder. The symbol ≡ means the two sides are equal for every value of x, which is what allows us to substitute convenient values.

Key fact:f(x) ≡ (divisor) × (quotient) + remainder, and the remainder has lower degree than the divisor.
03

Dividing a Polynomial by a Linear Expression

Long division of polynomials follows the same pattern as long division of numbers. To divide x³ + 2x² − 5x − 6 by (x + 1), first divide the leading term x³ by x to get x², which is the first term of the quotient. Multiply (x + 1) by x² to get x³ + x², and subtract to leave x² − 5x. Divide x² by x to get +x, multiply to get x² + x, subtract to leave −6x − 6. Divide −6x by x to get −6, multiply to get −6x − 6, and subtract to leave 0. So the quotient is x² + x − 6 and the remainder is 0.

Synthetic division is a quicker layout for dividing by (x − a). Write the coefficients in a row, bring down the first one, multiply by a and add to the next coefficient, and repeat. Dividing 3x³ − 5x² + 4x − 7 by (x − 2) uses a = 2: bring down 3; 3 × 2 = 6 and −5 + 6 = 1; 1 × 2 = 2 and 4 + 2 = 6; 6 × 2 = 12 and −7 + 12 = 5. The quotient is 3x² + x + 6 and the remainder is 5.

A third method is comparing coefficients, sometimes called division by inspection. If (x − 3) is a factor of 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6, write 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 ≡ (x − 3)(2x² + bx + c). The x³ terms already match. Comparing constants gives −3c = 6, so c = −2. Comparing x² terms gives b − 6 = −3, so b = 3. The quadratic factor is 2x² + 3x − 2, and you can check the x terms: c − 3b = −2 − 9 = −11, which matches.

  • Long division: divide leading terms, multiply back, subtract, repeat.
  • Synthetic division: fastest for divisors of the form (x − a).
  • Comparing coefficients: write the identity and match powers of x, then check with a remaining power.
04

The Remainder Theorem

The remainder theorem states that when a polynomial f(x) is divided by (x − a), the remainder is f(a). The proof follows from the division identity f(x) ≡ (x − a)q(x) + r: substituting x = a makes the first term zero, leaving f(a) = r. This means you can find a remainder with one substitution instead of a full division. For f(x) = x³ + 2x² − 5x + 1 divided by (x − 2), the remainder is f(2) = 8 + 8 − 10 + 1 = 7.

For a divisor of the form (ax − b), the value that makes the divisor zero is x = b/a, so the remainder is f(b/a). For example, dividing f(x) = 4x³ − 2x + 5 by (2x + 1) uses x = −½: f(−½) = 4(−⅛) − 2(−½) + 5 = −½ + 1 + 5 = 5½. Take care with the sign: the divisor (x + 3) corresponds to x = −3, not x = 3.

The remainder theorem is often used to form equations for unknown coefficients. If f(x) = x³ + ax + b leaves a remainder of 7 when divided by (x − 2), then f(2) = 7 gives 8 + 2a + b = 7, so 2a + b = −1. A second condition, such as a remainder of −5 on division by (x + 2), gives −8 − 2a + b = −5, so −2a + b = 3. Adding the two equations gives 2b = 2, so b = 1 and a = −1.

Key fact:Remainder theorem: f(x) ÷ (x − a) leaves remainder f(a); f(x) ÷ (ax − b) leaves remainder f(b/a).
05

The Factor Theorem

The factor theorem is a special case of the remainder theorem: (x − a) is a factor of f(x) if and only if f(a) = 0. In words, if substituting x = a gives zero, then dividing by (x − a) leaves no remainder, so (x − a) divides exactly into f(x). Conversely, if (x − a) is a factor then x = a is a root of the equation f(x) = 0. For f(x) = x³ − 7x − 6, f(−1) = −1 + 7 − 6 = 0, so (x + 1) is a factor.

When a question says 'show that (x − 2) is a factor', you must substitute x = 2, show the arithmetic, and conclude with a sentence such as 'f(2) = 0, so (x − 2) is a factor'. Simply writing f(2) = 0 without evidence may not earn the mark, because the answer was given in the question. Similarly, for a factor (2x − 1), you must evaluate f(½).

The factor theorem also lets you find unknown coefficients. If (x − 3) is a factor of x³ − kx² + 2x + 3, then f(3) = 0 gives 27 − 9k + 6 + 3 = 0, so 9k = 36 and k = 4. If two factors are given, you get two simultaneous equations, which you solve in the usual way before factorising the resulting polynomial completely.

  • (x − a) is a factor ⇔ f(a) = 0.
  • (ax − b) is a factor ⇔ f(b/a) = 0.
  • Every factor gives a root, and every root gives a factor.
06

Finding a First Factor by Trial

To factorise a cubic with no factor given, you need to find one root by trial. If the polynomial has integer coefficients and leading coefficient 1, any integer root must divide the constant term exactly. For x³ − 6x² + 11x − 6, the possible integer roots are ±1, ±2, ±3 and ±6. Testing x = 1 gives 1 − 6 + 11 − 6 = 0, so (x − 1) is a factor. Start with the smallest values, which are quickest to evaluate.

When the leading coefficient is not 1, rational roots of the form p/q are possible, where p divides the constant term and q divides the leading coefficient. For 6x³ + 7x² − x − 2, candidates include ±1, ±2, ±½, ±⅓, ±⅔ and ±⅙. Trying x = −1 gives −6 + 7 + 1 − 2 = 0, so (x + 1) is a factor. In practice, exam questions are designed so that a small integer or a simple fraction works.

Sometimes a cubic can be factorised by grouping, which avoids division altogether. For x³ − 3x² − 4x + 12, group as x²(x − 3) − 4(x − 3) = (x² − 4)(x − 3) = (x − 2)(x + 2)(x − 3). Grouping works whenever the ratio of the first two coefficients equals the ratio of the last two, here 1 : −3 and −4 : 12. It is worth spotting, but the factor theorem method always works when there is a rational root.

Key fact:Integer roots of a monic cubic divide the constant term: test ±1, ±2, … in order.
07

Solving Cubic Equations Completely

Solving a cubic equation f(x) = 0 follows a fixed routine. First find one root x = a by trial or from the question. Next divide f(x) by (x − a) to obtain a quadratic quotient. Then factorise the quadratic or use the quadratic formula to find the other two roots. Finally list all three roots. For 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 = 0, f(3) = 54 − 27 − 33 + 6 = 0, dividing gives 2x² + 3x − 2 = (2x − 1)(x + 2), and the roots are x = 3, ½ and −2.

If the quadratic factor does not factorise, use the formula and present the roots exactly or to the required accuracy. For example, 2x³ + x² − 7x + 3 = (2x − 1)(x² + x − 3), and x² + x − 3 = 0 gives x = (−1 ± √13)/2. So the full solution is x = ½, x = (−1 + √13)/2 ≈ 1.30 and x = (−1 − √13)/2 ≈ −2.30. If the quadratic has a negative discriminant, the cubic has only one real root, and you should state this clearly.

The phrase 'factorise completely' means writing the cubic as a product of linear factors (or a linear factor and an irreducible quadratic). Leaving it as (x − 3)(2x² + 3x − 2) is incomplete if the quadratic factorises further. Always expand your final factors mentally, or check that the constant terms multiply to give the original constant, to confirm the factorisation is correct.

  • Step 1: find a root a so that f(a) = 0.
  • Step 2: divide by (x − a) to obtain a quadratic quotient.
  • Step 3: solve the quadratic and state all real roots.
08

Connecting Factors, Roots and Graphs

Every linear factor of a polynomial corresponds to an x-intercept of its graph. Once you have factorised x³ − 2x² − 5x + 6 as (x − 1)(x + 2)(x − 3), you know the curve crosses the x-axis at x = 1, −2 and 3, and its y-intercept is the constant term, 6. This connects directly with the cubic sketches of the previous chapter and allows you to solve cubic inequalities once the polynomial is factorised.

You can also work backwards from roots to a polynomial. If a cubic with leading coefficient 2 has roots 1, −3 and ½, then it can be written as f(x) = 2(x − 1)(x + 3)(x − ½) = (x − 1)(x + 3)(2x − 1). Expanding gives (x² + 2x − 3)(2x − 1) = 2x³ + 3x² − 8x + 3. Questions sometimes give the roots or graph and ask you to find the polynomial in expanded form.

Finally, remember that a cubic always has at least one real root, because its graph must cross the x-axis as it goes from one end to the other. It can have one, two (with one repeated) or three distinct real roots. The number depends on the quadratic factor left after the first division: its discriminant tells you whether it adds two, one repeated, or no further real roots.

Worked Examples

Solving a cubic equation

Solve the equation 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 = 0.

  1. Let f(x) = 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6. Try x = 3: f(3) = 54 − 27 − 33 + 6 = 0, so (x − 3) is a factor.
  2. Write 2x³ − 3x² − 11x + 6 ≡ (x − 3)(2x² + bx + c). Constants: −3c = 6, so c = −2. x² terms: b − 6 = −3, so b = 3.
  3. Check the x terms: c − 3b = −2 − 9 = −11, which matches. So f(x) = (x − 3)(2x² + 3x − 2).
  4. Factorise the quadratic: 2x² + 3x − 2 = (2x − 1)(x + 2).
  5. So f(x) = (x − 3)(2x − 1)(x + 2) = 0.
x = 3, x = ½ or x = −2.

Finding unknown coefficients

The polynomial f(x) = 2x³ + ax² + bx − 6 has a factor (x + 2) and leaves a remainder of −3 when divided by (x − 1). Find a and b, and hence solve f(x) = 0.

  1. Factor theorem: f(−2) = −16 + 4a − 2b − 6 = 0, so 4a − 2b = 22, i.e. 2a − b = 11.
  2. Remainder theorem: f(1) = 2 + a + b − 6 = −3, so a + b = 1.
  3. Add the equations: 3a = 12, so a = 4 and b = 1 − 4 = −3.
  4. So f(x) = 2x³ + 4x² − 3x − 6 = 2x²(x + 2) − 3(x + 2) = (x + 2)(2x² − 3).
  5. Solve: x + 2 = 0 gives x = −2; 2x² − 3 = 0 gives x² = 3/2, so x = ±√(3/2) = ±√6/2 ≈ ±1.22.
a = 4, b = −3; x = −2, x = √6/2 ≈ 1.22 or x = −√6/2 ≈ −1.22.

Using the remainder theorem with an (ax − b) divisor

Find the remainder when f(x) = 4x³ − 2x + 5 is divided by (2x + 1).

  1. The divisor 2x + 1 is zero when x = −½, so the remainder is f(−½).
  2. Evaluate: 4(−½)³ = 4(−⅛) = −½, and −2(−½) = +1.
  3. So f(−½) = −½ + 1 + 5 = 5½.
The remainder is 5½ (or 11/2).

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Using the wrong sign when applying the remainder theorem: for the divisor (x + 3), substitute x = −3, not x = 3.
  • Leaving out a missing power during division. Write 4x³ − 2x + 5 as 4x³ + 0x² − 2x + 5 before dividing.
  • Writing 'f(2) = 0' without showing the substitution when asked to show that (x − 2) is a factor. Show every term and conclude in words.
  • Stopping at a linear factor times a quadratic when asked to factorise completely. Check whether the quadratic factorises further.
  • Confusing roots and factors: the factor (2x − 1) gives the root x = ½, not x = 1 or x = 2.
  • Subtraction errors in long division. Put the subtracted line in brackets and change every sign carefully.

Exam tips

  • Test x = 1 first: f(1) is simply the sum of the coefficients, so it is the quickest check.
  • Test x = −1 next: f(−1) is the alternating sum of the coefficients.
  • When comparing coefficients, use the leftover power as a check to catch arithmetic errors.
  • Label each condition clearly, e.g. 'f(−2) = 0 (factor theorem)' and 'f(1) = −3 (remainder theorem)'.
  • If the quadratic factor needs the formula, give exact surd answers unless decimals are requested.
  • After factorising, expand quickly or check that the constant terms multiply to the original constant.

Quick Recap

  1. A polynomial of degree n can be written as f(x) ≡ (divisor)(quotient) + remainder, with the remainder of lower degree than the divisor.
  2. Polynomials can be divided by long division, synthetic division or by comparing coefficients.
  3. The remainder theorem: dividing f(x) by (x − a) leaves remainder f(a), and by (ax − b) leaves f(b/a).
  4. The factor theorem: (x − a) is a factor exactly when f(a) = 0.
  5. Given factors or remainders produce equations that can be solved simultaneously for unknown coefficients.
  6. To solve a cubic, find one root by trial, divide to get a quadratic, then solve the quadratic.
  7. Every factor gives an x-intercept of the graph, which links factorising to sketching and inequalities.
