Collaboration and Teamwork

How effective teams work: setting shared goals, allocating roles, communicating, making decisions, resolving conflict and managing the Team Project from planning to outcome.

01

Overview

Collaboration is a core Global Perspectives skill and the heart of the Team Project, which is worth around 30 per cent of the qualification. You will work with classmates to investigate a local issue with global connections, plan an outcome, carry it out and then reflect individually on the process. Teams rarely succeed by accident; they succeed because members agree clear goals, share tasks fairly, communicate well and handle disagreements constructively. Understanding how teams work will help you contribute effectively and write the specific, honest reflection that earns high marks.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain what effective collaboration involves and why it is valued.
  • Describe how teams set goals, allocate roles and plan tasks.
  • Identify features of effective team communication and decision-making.
  • Explain strategies for managing conflict and difficulties within a team.
  • Outline the stages of the Team Project, from choosing an issue to producing an outcome.
  • Evaluate your own and your team's contribution to a collaborative task.
02

What Is Collaboration?

Collaboration means working together with others towards a shared goal, combining different skills, knowledge and perspectives to achieve more than any individual could alone. It is more than simply dividing up tasks; true collaboration involves discussing ideas, building on each other's contributions, making joint decisions and supporting one another when difficulties arise.

Collaboration matters in the wider world because global issues such as pandemics, climate change and migration cannot be solved by one person or even one country. International agreements, scientific research teams and humanitarian responses all rely on people with different backgrounds cooperating. The Team Project gives you a small-scale experience of this kind of cooperation.

A diverse team is often a strength. Members from different cultural backgrounds, with different interests or skills, bring different perspectives on an issue. This can lead to more creative solutions and a more balanced understanding, although it also means the team must work harder to listen and reach agreement.

Key fact:Collaboration = working together towards a shared goal, combining skills and perspectives.
03

Setting Goals and Allocating Roles

Every effective team starts by agreeing a clear, shared aim. For the Team Project, this usually means identifying a local problem connected to a global issue and deciding what outcome you want to achieve, such as raising awareness, changing behaviour or creating a resource. SMART goals help: they are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. 'Reduce single-use plastic bottles in our school canteen by 30 per cent in six weeks' is far more useful than 'help the environment'.

Once the aim is clear, the team should allocate roles based on members' strengths and interests. Possible roles include coordinator or leader, researcher, record keeper, communicator who contacts outside organisations, designer and presenter. Roles do not need to be fixed, and everyone should contribute to research and decision-making, but clear responsibilities prevent tasks being forgotten or duplicated.

A team plan or action plan lists each task, the person responsible, the deadline and the resources needed. Reviewing this plan regularly helps the team stay on track and adapt when problems arise. Keeping minutes or notes of meetings provides a record of decisions that is extremely useful for later reflection.

  • Specific: clear about what will be achieved.
  • Measurable: success can be checked with evidence.
  • Achievable: realistic with the team's time and resources.
  • Relevant: linked to the issue being investigated.
  • Time-bound: has a deadline.
04

Communication and Decision-Making

Good communication is essential for teamwork. It involves speaking clearly, but equally listening actively, which means paying full attention, asking questions to check understanding and responding thoughtfully rather than waiting for your turn to talk. Teams that communicate well share information regularly, so everyone knows what others are doing and can spot problems early.

Teams can make decisions in different ways. Consensus means discussing until everyone can accept the decision; it builds commitment but can be slow. Voting is faster but may leave some members feeling ignored. Delegating a decision to the person with most expertise can be efficient for small matters. Choosing the right method for each situation is a sign of a mature team.

Communication tools matter too. Shared online documents, group messaging and regular short meetings help teams coordinate, especially when members have different timetables. However, online communication can lead to misunderstandings, so important decisions are often best discussed face to face.

05

Managing Conflict and Difficulties

Disagreement is a natural part of teamwork and can be positive if it leads the team to test ideas and consider alternatives. Problems arise when disagreement becomes personal, when members feel unheard, or when conflict stops progress. Common causes include unequal workloads, unclear roles, missed deadlines, different expectations about quality and clashing personalities.

Strategies for managing conflict include focusing on the issue rather than the person, allowing each member to explain their view without interruption, looking for compromise or a solution that combines the best of different ideas, and referring back to the team's shared goal. If a member is not contributing, the team should talk to them early and respectfully, find out if there is a reason, and agree clear tasks. A teacher can be asked to help if problems persist.

How a team handles difficulties is valuable material for reflection. Examiners are not looking for a perfect team; they reward students who can describe a real challenge, explain how it was handled, evaluate whether the approach worked and identify what they would do differently next time.

  • Focus on the problem, not the person.
  • Listen to all viewpoints before deciding.
  • Look for compromise and combine ideas where possible.
  • Return to the shared goal to guide decisions.
  • Address non-contribution early and respectfully.
  • Seek support from a teacher when needed.
06

The Team Project Process

The Team Project usually follows a sequence. First, the team chooses a global topic and identifies a related local issue, for example litter in a local park linked to the global topic of sustainable living. Next, members research the issue, often dividing research so that different members explore different perspectives, causes or possible solutions, and share their findings with the team.

The team then agrees an aim and designs an outcome, which is a practical action or product intended to address the issue. Outcomes might include an awareness campaign, an event, a video, a website, a leaflet or a practical initiative such as setting up a school recycling scheme. The outcome should be informed by the research and realistic for the team to achieve.

After carrying out the outcome, the team evaluates how successful it was, using evidence where possible, such as survey results before and after a campaign. Each member then completes individual work reflecting on the research, the outcome and the collaboration. Because the requirements for team and individual evidence are set out in the syllabus, your teacher will explain exactly what must be submitted.

Key fact:Team Project flow: choose issue → research perspectives → agree aim → design and carry out outcome → evaluate → reflect individually.
07

Evaluating Teamwork

Evaluating teamwork means judging how well the team worked and how effectively you contributed. Useful questions include: Did we have a clear shared aim? Were tasks divided fairly? Did everyone's ideas get heard? Did we meet deadlines? How did we handle disagreements? Did our outcome achieve its aim? Answering with specific examples rather than general statements gives your evaluation credibility.

Evaluate your own contribution honestly. Identify your strengths, such as organising meetings or researching thoroughly, and areas for improvement, such as speaking up more or meeting deadlines. Explain how collaboration affected your thinking: perhaps a teammate's research on a different country changed your view of the issue, or discussing solutions helped you see practical limitations you had not considered.

Finally, suggest improvements. These should be specific and realistic, such as holding a short weekly progress meeting, agreeing roles in writing, or piloting a questionnaire before using it. Forward-looking evaluation shows you have learned from the experience, which is the purpose of reflection.

Worked Examples

Planning a Team Project outcome

A team finds that many students in their school skip breakfast, linked to the global topic of disease and health. Plan an appropriate outcome using a SMART goal and allocated roles.

  1. Research: survey students to find out how many skip breakfast and why; read secondary sources on nutrition and concentration, e.g. national health guidance.
  2. Set a SMART goal: 'Increase the percentage of Year 10 students eating breakfast on school days from 55% to 70% within four weeks.'
  3. Design the outcome: a breakfast awareness campaign with posters, a short assembly presentation and a trial healthy breakfast stall before school.
  4. Allocate roles: coordinator (schedule and meetings), researcher (survey design and analysis), designer (posters), communicator (contact school canteen and head teacher), presenter (assembly).
  5. Plan evaluation: repeat the survey after four weeks to measure change.
The outcome is realistic, informed by research and measurable. Clear roles and a before-and-after survey allow the team to judge whether the campaign achieved its SMART goal.

Handling a team conflict

In a four-person team, two members want to make a video while two want to organise a school event. Discussions have stalled for a week. Explain how the team could resolve this.

  1. Return to the shared goal: which outcome best addresses the issue identified in the research?
  2. Let each pair present reasons for their idea without interruption, listing benefits and drawbacks such as cost, time and reach.
  3. Evaluate against criteria agreed by the team: impact on the audience, feasibility and link to research.
  4. Look for a compromise: organise the event and film it to create a short video for the school website, combining both ideas.
  5. Record the decision in minutes and allocate tasks with deadlines.
By using agreed criteria and seeking a compromise, the team can make a decision everyone supports, reducing resentment and restoring progress. This episode also provides specific evidence for each member's reflection.

Model reflection on collaboration

Write a short reflection evaluating your contribution to a team and the effect of collaboration on your perspective.

  1. Describe your role specifically: 'I was responsible for designing and analysing our questionnaire on plastic use.'
  2. Evaluate a strength: 'My analysis identified that 60% of students did not know which plastics were recyclable, which shaped our campaign's focus.'
  3. Evaluate a weakness: 'I did not pilot the questionnaire, so one question was misunderstood and those results were unusable.'
  4. Explain the impact of collaboration on your perspective: 'A teammate's research on waste exports to Southeast Asia made me realise that recycling in my country can shift problems elsewhere.'
  5. Suggest an improvement: 'Next time I would test questions with a small group first.'
A strong reflection is specific, balanced and forward-looking: it identifies a real contribution, a genuine weakness, how working with others changed the student's view and a practical improvement.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Dividing the project into separate individual tasks with no discussion, which is cooperation without genuine collaboration.
  • Setting vague goals such as 'raise awareness' with no way to measure success; use SMART goals.
  • Choosing an outcome that is not linked to the team's research or is too ambitious to complete.
  • Ignoring early signs of conflict or non-contribution until they seriously damage the project.
  • Writing reflections that only describe what the team did, rather than evaluating contributions and learning.
  • Blaming teammates in reflection; instead explain the difficulty, how it was handled and what you learned.

Exam tips

  • Keep a team log or meeting minutes from the first week to provide evidence for reflection.
  • Use a shared action plan with names and deadlines, and review it at every meeting.
  • Collect evidence of impact, such as before-and-after surveys, to evaluate your outcome.
  • When reflecting, use the pattern: what happened, why it mattered, what I learned, what I would change.
  • Mention how teammates' research or views changed your own perspective on the issue.
  • Be honest about challenges; well-evaluated difficulties often earn more credit than claims of perfection.

11. A team's outcome was a leaflet about recycling, but a follow-up survey showed only 10% of students had read it. Evaluate the outcome and suggest improvements.

Maximum 6.
[8 marks]

12. 'Working in a team always produces better results than working alone.' Evaluate this claim with reference to the Team Project.

Model answerSupport: teams combine different skills and perspectives (1-2); tasks can be shared, allowing more research and a more ambitious outcome (1-2); discussion tests ideas and improves decisions (1). Challenge: conflict, unequal contributions or poor communication can slow progress (1-2); decisions by consensus may take longer (1); some tasks, such as writing a focused report, may be done more efficiently alone (1). Judgement: teams produce better results when they have clear goals, roles and communication, so the claim is only true under those conditions (1-2). Maximum 8.

Quick Recap

  1. Collaboration means working together towards a shared goal, combining skills and perspectives.
  2. Effective teams agree SMART goals, allocate roles based on strengths and use an action plan.
  3. Good communication includes active listening, regular updates and appropriate decision-making methods.
  4. Conflict is normal; manage it by focusing on issues, listening, compromising and returning to the shared goal.
  5. The Team Project moves from identifying a local issue with global links to research, outcome, evaluation and reflection.
  6. Measure the success of an outcome with evidence such as before-and-after surveys.
  7. Strong evaluation of teamwork is specific, honest and suggests realistic improvements.
