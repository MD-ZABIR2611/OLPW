The Pakistan Movement

The growth of Muslim political organisation from the founding of the All-India Muslim League in Dhaka in 1906 to the creation of Pakistan, including East Bengal, on 14 August 1947, with special attention to the role of Bengal and its leaders.

Overview

Between 1906 and 1947 the Muslims of British India moved from asking for separate representation to demanding a separate homeland. Bengal was at the heart of this story: the Muslim League was founded in Dhaka, the Lahore Resolution was moved by the Bengali leader A.K. Fazlul Huq, and Bengal provided the largest Muslim League vote in the elections of 1946. At the same time, Bengal politics had its own character, shaped by peasant grievances, the Krishak Praja Party and the leadership of Fazlul Huq, Khwaja Nazimuddin and H.S. Suhrawardy. This chapter explains the key stages, documents and personalities of the Pakistan Movement as they affected Bengal. In the exam you will need to explain why events happened and judge the contribution of individuals, so focus on causes and consequences as well as dates.

By the end of this chapter you should be able to:

  • Explain why the All-India Muslim League was founded in 1906 and describe its early aims.
  • Describe the main constitutional developments from the Morley-Minto Reforms to the Government of India Act 1935.
  • Explain the role of A.K. Fazlul Huq and the Krishak Praja Party in Bengal politics, especially the 1937 ministry.
  • Describe the Lahore Resolution of 1940 and explain its significance for Bengal.
  • Explain the importance of the 1946 elections, the Cabinet Mission Plan and Direct Action Day.
  • Describe the United Bengal proposal, the partition of Bengal in 1947 and the Sylhet referendum.
02

The Founding of the Muslim League (1906)

By 1906 many Muslim leaders believed that the Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, represented mainly Hindu interests. The fierce Hindu agitation against the partition of Bengal convinced them that Muslims needed their own organisation. On 1 October 1906 a delegation of 35 Muslim leaders led by the Aga Khan III met Viceroy Lord Minto at Simla and asked for separate electorates, so that Muslims would elect their own representatives. Minto responded sympathetically.

On 30 December 1906, at the session of the All-India Muhammadan Educational Conference held at Shahbagh in Dhaka and hosted by Nawab Salimullah, the All-India Muslim League was founded on Salimullah's proposal. Its early aims were to promote loyalty to the British government, to protect and advance the political rights of Muslims and to prevent hostility towards other communities. The Aga Khan became its first president. The League was initially a small organisation of landlords and educated professionals, but its foundation in Dhaka gave Bengal a special place in its history.

Key fact:The All-India Muslim League was founded in Dhaka on 30 December 1906 on the proposal of Nawab Salimullah.
03

Constitutional Developments 1909-1935

The Morley-Minto Reforms (Indian Councils Act 1909) granted separate electorates for Muslims, a major success for the League. After the annulment of partition in 1911, relations between the League and Congress improved, and in the Lucknow Pact of 1916, negotiated largely by Muhammad Ali Jinnah, Congress accepted separate electorates. However, Bengali Muslims, who formed a majority of about 52 per cent in Bengal, received only 40 per cent of the seats in the provincial council, which many resented. The Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms of 1919 introduced dyarchy in the provinces. From 1919 to 1922 Muslims joined the Khilafat and Non-Cooperation movements with Gandhi, but these collapsed after violence at Chauri Chaura in 1922.

In Bengal, the Congress leader Chittaranjan Das negotiated the Bengal Pact of 1923, promising Muslims 55 per cent of government jobs, but Congress abandoned it after his death in 1925. The Nehru Report of 1928 rejected separate electorates, and Jinnah answered with his Fourteen Points in 1929. In his Allahabad Address of December 1930, the poet Allama Muhammad Iqbal proposed a separate Muslim state in north-west India, and in 1933 Chaudhry Rahmat Ali coined the name Pakistan. The Communal Award of 1932 gave Bengali Muslims 119 of the 250 seats in the Bengal Legislative Assembly, and the Government of India Act 1935 introduced provincial autonomy, making the provincial elections of 1937 very important.

  • 1909: Morley-Minto Reforms, separate electorates.
  • 1916: Lucknow Pact between Congress and League.
  • 1923: Bengal Pact of C.R. Das.
  • 1929: Jinnah's Fourteen Points; 1930: Iqbal's Allahabad Address.
  • 1935: Government of India Act, provincial autonomy.
04

Fazlul Huq and the Krishak Praja Party

Abul Kashem Fazlul Huq (1873-1962), popularly known as Sher-e-Bangla (the Tiger of Bengal), was a lawyer from Barishal who had been a founder member of the Muslim League and Mayor of Calcutta. He understood that most Bengali Muslims were poor peasants burdened by debt and oppressive zamindars. His Krishak Praja Party (KPP), which grew out of the Nikhil Banga Praja Samiti, campaigned in the 1937 election on a programme of abolishing the Permanent Settlement without compensation, reducing rents and freeing peasants from moneylenders.

In the 1937 election no party won a majority in Bengal. Congress refused to form a coalition, so Fazlul Huq became Prime Minister of Bengal in April 1937 at the head of a KPP-Muslim League coalition, and he later joined the League. His ministry passed important laws for peasants: the Bengal Agricultural Debtors Act of 1938 set up Debt Settlement Boards to reduce rural debt, the Bengal Tenancy (Amendment) Act of 1938 weakened landlords' powers, and the Money-Lenders Act of 1940 limited interest rates. He also encouraged Muslim education. In 1941 he quarrelled with Jinnah, was expelled from the League and formed a new ministry with the Hindu Mahasabha leader Syama Prasad Mookerjee, which fell in March 1943. Khwaja Nazimuddin then formed a Muslim League ministry (1943-1945).

Key fact:Fazlul Huq's 1937 ministry passed the Bengal Agricultural Debtors Act (1938), creating Debt Settlement Boards to relieve peasants.
05

The Lahore Resolution (1940)

At the Muslim League session at Lahore on 23 March 1940, Fazlul Huq moved the famous resolution, which was adopted on 24 March. It stated that geographically contiguous units in which Muslims were a majority, as in the north-western and eastern zones of India, should be grouped to constitute 'independent states' in which the constituent units would be autonomous and sovereign. Although the word Pakistan did not appear in the text, the press immediately called it the Pakistan Resolution, and it became the official goal of the League. Jinnah's presidential speech explained the Two-Nation Theory: that Hindus and Muslims were two separate nations with different religions, cultures and histories.

The resolution was particularly significant for Bengal because its use of the plural 'states' seemed to allow an independent eastern Muslim state separate from the north-west. Later, at the Delhi Convention of Muslim League legislators in April 1946, a resolution moved by H.S. Suhrawardy called for a single sovereign state of Pakistan. Bengali leaders such as Abul Hashim argued that this changed the original meaning of the Lahore Resolution. After 1947 many Bengali nationalists pointed to the Lahore Resolution as evidence that the eastern region had been promised autonomy, and the idea influenced later demands such as the Six-Point Programme.

06

War, Famine and the 1946 Elections

The Second World War affected Bengal deeply. Japan's conquest of Burma in 1942 brought the front line close, the Cripps Mission of 1942 failed to satisfy either Congress or the League, and Congress launched the Quit India movement in August 1942, after which its leaders were jailed. The Bengal Famine of 1943 killed about three million people, caused by wartime disruption, the loss of rice imports from Burma, hoarding and speculation, the destruction of boats in coastal areas, and a slow government response. The famine increased anger against British rule and damaged the reputation of the provincial government.

The elections of 1945-46 were effectively a referendum on Pakistan. The Muslim League won all 30 Muslim seats in the Central Legislative Assembly. In Bengal it won 113 seats, almost all of the 119 seats reserved for Muslims, a stronger result than in any other province. This was due to effective organisation by Suhrawardy and Abul Hashim, the support of students, and the appeal of Pakistan as a promise of freedom from zamindars and moneylenders. In April 1946 Huseyn Shaheed Suhrawardy became Prime Minister of Bengal at the head of a Muslim League ministry.

  • Bengal Famine 1943: about three million deaths.
  • 1946 Bengal election: Muslim League won 113 of 119 Muslim seats.
  • Suhrawardy ministry: April 1946 - August 1947.
07

The Cabinet Mission and Direct Action Day (1946)

In 1946 the British government sent a Cabinet Mission of three ministers to arrange a transfer of power. Its plan of 16 May 1946 rejected a fully sovereign Pakistan but proposed a loose federation with a weak centre controlling only defence, foreign affairs and communications, and three groups of provinces: Group A of the Hindu-majority provinces, Group B of the north-west and Group C of Bengal and Assam. The League accepted the plan in June because the groups gave Muslims substantial autonomy, but withdrew its acceptance after Jawaharlal Nehru suggested that Congress would not be bound by the grouping arrangements.

The League then called for Direct Action Day on 16 August 1946 to show Muslim determination to achieve Pakistan. In Calcutta the day turned into terrible communal violence, the Great Calcutta Killings, in which several thousand people died over a few days. Violence spread to Noakhali and Tippera in East Bengal in October 1946 and to Bihar, where many Muslims were killed. The riots convinced many British and Indian leaders that partition was unavoidable.

08

The United Bengal Plan and Partition (1947)

On 20 February 1947 British Prime Minister Clement Attlee announced that Britain would leave India by June 1948, and Lord Mountbatten became Viceroy. When it became clear that Punjab and Bengal would be divided, Suhrawardy, supported by Abul Hashim and the Congress leaders Sarat Chandra Bose and Kiran Shankar Roy, proposed in April-May 1947 an independent, undivided sovereign Bengal. Supporters argued that Bengal had a common language and culture and that its economy, centred on Calcutta and the jute trade, would be ruined by partition. The plan failed because the national Congress leadership and the Hindu Mahasabha, led by Syama Prasad Mookerjee, strongly opposed it, and many Hindus feared living in a Muslim-majority state.

Mountbatten's plan of 3 June 1947 provided for the partition of India. On 20 June 1947 the members of the Bengal Legislative Assembly voted: those from Muslim-majority districts voted to join Pakistan undivided, while those from Hindu-majority districts voted to partition the province. A referendum in Sylhet on 6-7 July 1947 produced a majority for joining East Bengal. The Radcliffe Boundary Commission drew the frontier, and its award, published on 17 August, gave Calcutta and Murshidabad to India and Khulna and the Chittagong Hill Tracts to East Bengal; most of Sylhet joined East Bengal, but Karimganj went to India. Pakistan came into being on 14 August 1947, with East Bengal as its eastern wing, separated from West Pakistan by about 1,600 km of Indian territory. Khwaja Nazimuddin became the first Chief Minister of East Bengal.

Key fact:Pakistan was created on 14 August 1947; East Bengal (renamed East Pakistan in 1955) was about 1,600 km from West Pakistan.

Worked Examples

Model answer: why was the Muslim League founded?

Explain why the All-India Muslim League was founded in 1906. [7 marks]

  1. Reason 1: Congress was seen as a Hindu-dominated body that did not protect Muslim interests; explain how Hindu majorities would win elections.
  2. Reason 2: Hindu agitation against the 1905 partition of Bengal threatened a province that benefited Muslims, showing the need for organised Muslim political action.
  3. Reason 3: The success of the Simla Deputation in October 1906 encouraged leaders to create a permanent body to follow up the demand for separate electorates.
  4. Reason 4: British reforms were expected; Muslims needed an organisation to speak for them in negotiations.
  5. Conclude with a link: the partition controversy was the immediate trigger, while fear of Hindu majority rule was the long-term cause.
The League was founded because Muslims feared that Congress would not protect their interests under a system of majority rule, because the anti-partition agitation threatened the gains of eastern Bengal's Muslims, and because the Simla Deputation showed that an organised Muslim voice could win concessions such as separate electorates. The partition controversy made the need urgent, and Dhaka, as capital of the new province, was the natural place for its foundation.

Model answer: Fazlul Huq's contribution

How important was A.K. Fazlul Huq to the Muslims of Bengal between 1937 and 1947? Explain your answer. [12 marks]

  1. Importance 1: as Prime Minister of Bengal from 1937 he passed the Agricultural Debtors Act (1938), Tenancy Amendment Act (1938) and Money-Lenders Act (1940), giving real relief to Muslim peasants.
  2. Importance 2: he moved the Lahore Resolution on 23 March 1940, giving Bengal a central role in the demand for Pakistan.
  3. Importance 3: he promoted Muslim education and employment.
  4. Limitations: he quarrelled with Jinnah, left the League in 1941 and formed a coalition with the Hindu Mahasabha; his ministry fell in 1943 and he played little role in the 1946 victory.
  5. Compare with others: Suhrawardy and Abul Hashim organised the League's 1946 success; Nazimuddin led the 1943-45 ministry.
  6. Judgement: very important in improving peasant welfare and in 1940, but less so in the final stage of the movement.
Fazlul Huq was highly important because his peasant reforms improved the lives of millions of Bengali Muslims and because he moved the Lahore Resolution. However, his break with the League after 1941 meant that Suhrawardy and Abul Hashim led the decisive 1946 campaign. Overall he was the most important Bengali Muslim leader of the late 1930s, but his influence declined in the final years before 1947.

Common Mistakes & Exam Tips

Common mistakes

  • Saying the Lahore Resolution demanded 'Pakistan' by name. The word did not appear; it demanded 'independent states' for Muslim-majority regions.
  • Confusing Fazlul Huq, Nazimuddin and Suhrawardy. Learn their ministries: Huq 1937-43, Nazimuddin 1943-45, Suhrawardy 1946-47.
  • Stating that the Muslim League was founded in Lahore or Karachi. It was founded in Dhaka in 1906.
  • Forgetting Bengal's special points: the plural 'states' in 1940, the 1946 election result, and the United Bengal Plan.
  • Describing Direct Action Day as intended to be violent. It was called as a protest; the violence that followed is a separate point.
  • Assuming all of Sylhet joined East Bengal. Karimganj was given to India by the Radcliffe Award.

Exam tips

  • Make a timeline with Bengal-specific events highlighted: 1906, 1916, 1923, 1937, 1940, 1943, 1946, 1947.
  • When asked about an individual, balance achievements against limitations and compare with other leaders.
  • Use the exact wording 'independent states' when explaining the significance of the Lahore Resolution for East Bengal.
  • In 'explain why' questions on 1947, separate long-term causes (Two-Nation Theory, fears of Hindu majority) from short-term triggers (riots, Attlee's deadline).
  • Link this chapter to the next: the idea of autonomy in the Lahore Resolution and East Bengal's distance from the West help explain later grievances.
  • Quote figures precisely but only when sure: 113 of 119 Muslim seats in 1946, about three million deaths in the 1943 famine.
